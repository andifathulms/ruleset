# Equipment evolution

Softball's equipment history is a slow retreat from its own name.

The original ball was sixteen inches around and genuinely soft, thrown and
caught barehanded. Competitive fastpitch settled on a twelve-inch ball, wound
and covered like a small baseball, with a compression and restitution standard
that a gymnasium ball never had. Sixteen-inch barehanded softball survives in
Chicago, where it started, and nowhere much else.

Bats moved from wood to aluminium in the 1970s and then to composites, and this
is where the sport hit a problem no other bat-and-ball sport faces as sharply.
A composite bat can return more energy to the ball than wood, and softball's
pitcher finishes their delivery roughly forty feet from the plate, moving
forward, with no time to react to a line drive.

So bats are regulated by what they do rather than what they are. Certification
standards cap the batted-ball speed a bat may impart, tested on a machine, and
a bat can be decertified after release if it breaks in a way that makes it
livelier. Face masks for pitchers and corner infielders became standard in much
of the game for the same reason.

The catcher's equipment tracked baseball's. The pitcher wears nothing special
except, increasingly, a mask — the one piece of protective equipment in this
sport that exists because of a bat.
