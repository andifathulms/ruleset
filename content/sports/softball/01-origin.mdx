# Origin and invention

Softball began indoors, in Chicago, in 1887, as a joke about a football game.

The story is well attested: in the Farragut Boat Club on the day of the
Harvard–Yale football game, a man threw a boxing glove at another, who swung at
it with a pole. George Hancock tied the glove into a ball, chalked a diamond on
the floor, and wrote rules that evening. The game was called indoor baseball,
and its point was that baseball could be played in a gymnasium in winter.

Everything that distinguishes softball follows from that constraint. The ball
was large and soft so it would not travel far or break anything. The bases were
close together because the room was small. The pitch was underhand because a
short distance and an overhand delivery are incompatible with anyone surviving
the at-bat.

The game moved outdoors and hardened. It acquired other names — kitten ball,
mush ball, diamond ball, playground ball — and in 1926 settled on softball,
by which time the ball was no longer soft. The Amateur Softball Association
formed in 1933 around a tournament at the Chicago World's Fair, and the
international federation followed in 1952.

Two forms separated. Fastpitch kept the underhand delivery and made it a
weapon; slowpitch required the ball to arc, which removed the pitcher from the
contest almost entirely and made the game a hitting exercise. Slowpitch is far
more widely played. Fastpitch is what the Olympics contests.
