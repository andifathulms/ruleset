# Officiating and technology

Boxing has done less with officiating technology than any sport on this site,
and it is the sport with the worst documented officiating record. Those two
facts are related, and the relationship is not the obvious one.

The sport did try. The 1992 computerised system was a serious attempt to
mechanise scoring, and it failed on its own terms twice over. It changed how
boxing was fought, because it only counted blows the judges could individually
separate and register. And it did not remove corruption, because the buttons
were still pressed by people.

That is the crucial difference between boxing and taekwondo, which faced an
almost identical judging crisis and solved it. Taekwondo put the sensor on the
athlete: a kick registers because it landed hard enough, and no official is
involved. Boxing put the button in the judge's hand, so the instrument recorded
opinions faster without making them more honest.

There is no technical reason boxing could not be scored the way taekwondo is.
Impact sensors in gloves and body protectors have been trialled. The sport has
not adopted them, and the reasons offered are that boxing scores quality —
cleanness, effect, ring generalship — rather than mere contact, and that a
sensor cannot tell a jab that lands from a jab that lands well.

That objection is real and it is also the objection every sport makes before it
automates. Whether boxing's central judgement is genuinely unmeasurable, or
whether the sport has simply preferred not to find out, is the open question of
its officiating.

The referee's count, meanwhile, remains one of the best-designed safety
instruments in sport: a judgement converted into ten seconds.
