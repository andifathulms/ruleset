# From prize fights to three-minute rounds

Boxing began as prize fighting with bare fists. In eighteenth-century England
a fight allowed wrestling throws, and a <Term id="round">round</Term> ended
only when a man went down. The fighters followed customs. Nothing was written.

<Photo id="boxing-sullivan" />

The Queensberry Rules, published in 1867, made it a sport. John Graham
Chambers drafted them, and the Marquess of Queensberry gave them his name as
patron. Three of the rules built modern boxing. Gloves became compulsory.
Rounds were fixed at three minutes, with a minute of rest between them. A
fighter who went down had ten seconds to get up.

<Photo id="boxing-queensberry" />

The ten <Term id="count">count</Term> turned a judgement into a number.
Someone used to have to decide whether a man could go on. Now the referee
counts, and the fighter is either ready at ten or beaten. John L. Sullivan,
the last bare-knuckle heavyweight champion, lost his title in 1892 in one of
the first championship fights under the new rules. He wore gloves.

<Photo id="boxing-on-the-ropes" />

Amateur boxing organised itself apart from the professional sport almost from
the start. The Fédération Internationale de Boxe Amateur was founded in 1920
and became AIBA in 1946. Boxing has been at every Olympic Games since 1904
except 1912, when Swedish law prohibited it.

Women waited much longer. Women's boxing joined the Games in 2012, the last
sport on the programme to include women. Nicola Adams won the flyweight final
in London and became the first woman to win an Olympic boxing title.
