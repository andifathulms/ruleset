# Origin and invention

Boxing is old and its rules are not. Prize fighting existed in eighteenth-
century England as a bare-knuckle activity with wrestling throws allowed and
rounds that ended only when a man went down, and it was governed by conventions
rather than by anything written.

The document that made it a sport is the **Queensberry Rules**, published in
1867 and drafted by John Graham Chambers under the patronage of the Marquess of
Queensberry. Three of its provisions built modern boxing: gloves became
compulsory, rounds were fixed at three minutes with a minute's rest, and a
downed fighter had ten seconds to rise.

The ten count is the interesting one for this site. It is the first
safety-motivated stopping rule in any sport here, and it works by converting a
judgement — is this man able to continue — into a countable interval. That is
the same move badminton made with its service height in 2018 and athletics
makes wherever it can.

Amateur boxing organised separately from the professional sport almost from the
start, with the Fédération Internationale de Boxe Amateur founded in 1920 and
becoming AIBA in 1946. Boxing has been on every Olympic programme since 1904
except 1912, when Swedish law prohibited it.

Women's boxing was admitted to the Games only in 2012, the last sport on the
programme to include women.
