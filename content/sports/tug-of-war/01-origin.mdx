# Origin and invention

Tug of war has no inventor, which makes it the oldest thing on this site and
the hardest to date.

Rope-pulling contests are attested across the ancient world — in Egypt, in
China, in Greece, in India — and in most cases they are ceremonial or
ritual before they are competitive. It is one of a very small number of
physical contests that appear to have arisen independently almost everywhere,
presumably because a rope and two groups of people is not a difficult idea to
have.

What is datable is the codification. Nineteenth-century Britain organised it,
as it organised most things: athletics clubs, police forces, fire brigades and
army regiments fielded teams, and the sport acquired written rules through
athletics governing bodies rather than through a body of its own.

That detail matters more than it sounds. When tug of war entered the Olympic
programme in 1900, it went in as an athletics event, administered by athletics
officials, with entries from clubs rather than nations. It was contested at
five consecutive Games — 1900, 1904, 1908, 1912 and 1920 — and Great Britain,
Sweden and the United States won most of the medals, frequently through police
and club teams that had entered as themselves.

When the IOC reduced the programme in the 1920s, tug of war was cut. It had no
international federation to argue for it, and would not have one until 1960.
The sport that carried on afterwards is the one this page describes: TWIF, a
codified rulebook, world championships, and a place at every World Games since
the first in 1981.
