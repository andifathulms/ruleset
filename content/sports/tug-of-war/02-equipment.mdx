# Equipment evolution

One rope, and a pair of boots that started an international incident.

The rope is specified more tightly than its simplicity suggests: at least 33.5
metres long, between 10 and 12.5 centimetres in circumference, and with no
knots or loops anywhere along it. The circumference is a fairness rule — a
thicker rope is harder for smaller hands to grip — and the ban on knots removes
any chance of a purchase that one team has and the other does not. Manila and
natural fibres have largely given way to synthetics with more consistent
behaviour when wet.

Boots are the sport's real equipment story. At the London Games in 1908 the
United States protested that the City of Liverpool Police team was wearing
heavy reinforced boots that amounted to unfair equipment. The British team was
a police team wearing its ordinary duty issue. The protest failed and the
Americans withdrew from the competition.

The modern rule addresses the same question directly: a boot may have a
reinforced sole and heel, and nothing may project beyond or beneath the sole.
The heel is allowed to be a solid edge to brace against the ground; it is not
allowed to become a spike that digs in. That line is inspected before
competition.

Indoor tug of war removes the argument entirely by removing the ground from it:
on a mat with a fixed footing, grip is standardised and only pulling remains.
It is the cleanest example on this site of a sport creating a second discipline
in order to control one variable.
