# A rope and a pair of boots

Tug of war needs one rope, and the rules describe it closely. It must be at
least 33.5 metres long and between 10 and 12.5 centimetres around, with no
knots or loops anywhere. The thickness is about fairness, because a thicker
rope is harder for small hands to grip. The ban on knots means neither team
gets a handhold the other does not have. Most ropes are now synthetic, which
behave more evenly when wet than manila and other natural fibres.

## London, 1908

The boots have the better story. At the London Games in 1908 the City of
Liverpool Police team pulled in heavy reinforced boots. These were their
ordinary duty boots. The United States team protested that the boots were
unfair equipment. The protest failed, and the Americans withdrew from the
competition. It is one of the earliest equipment protests in Olympic history.
The final was an all-police affair: the City of London Police beat Liverpool.

<Photo id="tug-of-war-final-1908" />

The [modern footwear rule](#tow-1908-boots-dispute) answers the same question.
A boot may have a reinforced sole and heel. Nothing may stick out beyond or
beneath the sole. The heel may be a solid edge that braces against the ground.
It may not become a spike that digs in. Officials inspect boots before
competition.

## The mat

<Term id="indoor">Indoor tug of war</Term> takes the ground out of the
argument. Teams pull on a rubber mat with a fixed footing, so every team has
the same grip and only the pulling is left. Outdoors on grass, the grip of a
boot in the turf is a large part of the contest. The federation treats the two
as separate disciplines and holds separate championships for each.
