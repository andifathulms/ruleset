# Officiating

A tug of war judge watches bodies, not the rope.

The rope's position is a fact anyone can see: a mark passes a line, and the end
is over. Nothing about the outcome requires adjudication. What requires
adjudication is whether the eight people on each side were pulling in a legal
posture for the several minutes it took.

The offences are all postural. **Sitting** — deliberately dropping to the
ground to anchor. **Rowing** — repeatedly sitting and rising to jerk the rope,
which converts a pull into a series of shocks. **Locking** — passing the rope
under an arm or fixing it against the body, which is legal only for the anchor
and only in a defined way. **Leaning** on the ground with anything but the
feet. **Propping** an elbow or forearm on a thigh to rest.

A judge issues cautions during the end, and two cautions disqualify a team from
it. This means the officiating happens continuously, under load, on eight
people at once, while they are at their physical limit and their form is
naturally degrading — and the judge has to separate a body failing from a body
cheating.

The start is the other controlled moment: "pick up the rope", "take the
strain", a check that the centre mark sits over the line, then "pull". Pulling
before the call is an offence, which makes the start an adjudicated event
rather than a reaction test.

There is no technology anywhere in this. The sport is judged entirely by eye,
and its most consequential judgement is whether someone's knee touching the
ground was a fall or a tactic.
