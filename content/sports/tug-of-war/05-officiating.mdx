# Who makes the calls

A tug of war <Term id="judge">judge</Term> watches bodies, not the rope.

Anyone can see where the rope is. A mark reaches the line, and the end is
over. What needs a judge is whether the eight people on each side pulled in a
legal position for the minutes it took.

<Photo id="tug-of-war-st-louis-judges" />

The start is the first moment the judge controls. The judge calls "pick up the
rope", then "<Term id="take-the-strain">take the strain</Term>", checks that
the <Term id="centre-mark">centre mark</Term> sits over the line, and calls
"pull". Pulling before the call is an offence, so the start is judged, not
raced.

Then the offences are all about how the body is used. Only the feet may touch
the ground.

- **<Term id="sitting">Sitting</Term>**: dropping to the ground on purpose to
  hold the rope with body weight.
- **<Term id="rowing">Rowing</Term>**: sitting and rising again and again to
  jerk the rope.
- **<Term id="locking">Locking</Term>**: passing the rope under an arm or
  fixing it against the body. Only the <Term id="anchor">anchor</Term> may do
  this, in one set way.
- **Leaning** on the ground with anything but the feet.
- **<Term id="propping">Propping</Term>**: resting an elbow or arm on the leg.

<Photo id="tug-of-war-north-somerset-anchor" />

The judge gives a <Term id="caution">caution</Term> for an offence, and two
cautions in an end disqualify the team from it. So the judging never stops. It
covers both teams at once, all at their limit, whose form is breaking down
as they tire. The judge has to tell a body that is failing from a body that is
cheating.

No technology is used. Everything is judged by eye. The hardest call is often
whether a knee on the ground was a slip or a tactic.
