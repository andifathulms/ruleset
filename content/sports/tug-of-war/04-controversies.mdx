# Controversies that forced rule changes

**The boots at London, 1908.** The earliest equipment protest in Olympic
history that this site records. The United States objected to the reinforced
boots worn by a British police team, the protest failed, and the Americans
withdrew. The modern footwear rule — reinforced sole and heel permitted,
nothing projecting beneath — is the direct descendant of that dispute.

**Who is allowed to enter.** Olympic tug of war was contested by clubs, police
forces and regiments rather than by national teams, and teams from the same
country competed against each other for medals. That was normal in 1900 and
increasingly anomalous by 1920, and it is part of why the sport sat badly with
the Games as they developed.

**Every way of not pulling.** The bulk of the modern rulebook defines
prohibited body positions: sitting, rowing, locking the rope to the body,
leaning on the ground, propping an arm on a leg. None of these is a safety
rule. They exist because a contest this simple can only be corrupted through
posture, and each was written after someone did it.

**Weighing the team rather than the person.** Classification by combined weight
is the sport's most distinctive law and it produces a distinctive problem:
weigh-in becomes a team event, one member's fluctuation can put a whole squad
out of its class, and the incentives around making weight are shared across
eight people rather than borne by one.

**The mat.** Indoor competition on a fixed footing was introduced to
standardise grip, and it changed what the sport rewards — technique over
ground conditions. Whether indoor and outdoor are one sport or two is a
question the federation answers by holding separate championships for both.
