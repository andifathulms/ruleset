# Equipment evolution

A belt, a clay platform, and a great deal of nothing.

The **mawashi** is the only garment: a long strip of heavy silk or cotton
wound around the waist and between the legs. It is not clothing that happens to
be worn during the sport — it is functional equipment, because a large share of
the sport's recognised winning techniques involve gripping it. Sumo is one of
the few combat sports where what your opponent is wearing is the primary handle
by which you move them, which puts it in the same family as judo's jacket and
some styles of belt wrestling.

The **dohyō** is a raised clay platform with a circle 4.55 metres across marked
by half-buried straw bales. Clay and sand rather than a mat, and raised rather
than flush, so a wrestler leaving the ring falls off it. There is no padding
anywhere in the sport.

That absence is the equipment story. Judo has mats, boxing has gloves and a
canvas, taekwondo has sensors and body protectors, and fencing has jackets and
masks — every combat sport on this site has acquired protective equipment as it
learned what it was doing to its athletes. Sumo has acquired none, and its
wrestlers land on clay, on sand, or on the people sitting at ringside.

The pillars that once supported the roof over the ring were removed in the
1950s so television cameras could see, and replaced by a suspended canopy. It
is a small change and a telling one: the sport has accepted broadcast
modifications to its architecture while declining every modification to what
happens inside the ring.
