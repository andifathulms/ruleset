# Governing body politics

Sumo's governance is the clearest example on this site of an international
federation that does not govern the top of its own sport.

The **Japan Sumo Association** runs professional sumo. It holds six
tournaments a year, administers the stables in which wrestlers live and train,
maintains the ranking ladder, and answers to nobody outside Japan. It has no
weight classes, admits no women, and does not enter world championships.

The **International Sumo Federation**, formed in 1992, governs amateur sumo
everywhere else. It has weight classes, men's and women's divisions, IOC
recognition, and a World Games place. It does not govern a single professional
wrestler.

The consequence is that sumo's world championship is a real competition that
does not contain the best sumo wrestlers in the world, and cannot. This is a
more extreme version of the problem that got baseball removed from the Olympic
programme after 2008 — a professional league declining to release its players
— except that here the professional body is not withholding athletes from the
international federation so much as existing entirely outside it.

The federation's Olympic ambitions run into this immediately. A sport can
plausibly argue for inclusion when its Olympic event would be its pinnacle.
Sumo's would not be, and everyone involved knows it.

The professional association has its own political history, and it is not
quiet: match-fixing investigations, hazing deaths in stables, and disputes
about foreign wrestlers — Mongolian, Eastern European, Pacific Islander — who
have dominated the top ranks for extended periods, prompting limits on the
number of foreign-born wrestlers a stable may hold.
