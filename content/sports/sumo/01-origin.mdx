# Origin and invention

Sumo is the oldest continuously practised sport covered on this site, and the
only one whose competitive form is inseparable from a religious one.

Its origins are in Shinto ritual — wrestling performed as an offering, to
divine an outcome or to ensure a harvest — and elements of that survive
unchanged in the professional sport today. The salt thrown into the ring is
purification. The referee's costume is that of a Shinto priest. The roof
suspended above the ring in a professional arena is a shrine roof, and it was
supported by pillars until they were removed in the 1950s because they blocked
the view of television cameras.

Court sumo is recorded from the eighth century. Professional sumo as a
spectacle dates from the Edo period, when it became a public entertainment
performed by paid wrestlers, with the ring, the ranking system and the stable
structure taking recognisable shape.

The modern institution is much more recent than the practice. The national
professional association was reorganised in 1925, producing a single written
rulebook where regional and house traditions had differed. That association —
the Japan Sumo Association — still governs the professional sport, alone.

The international sport is younger still. The International Sumo Federation was
formed in 1992 to govern amateur sumo outside Japan, and made two decisions the
professional body had never made: it introduced weight classes, and it admitted
women. Sumo entered the World Games programme in 2001, at Akita, in northern
Japan.

So there are two sumos, and the split is the single most important fact about
the sport's present. The one with the world championships is not the one with
the best wrestlers.
