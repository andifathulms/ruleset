# From shrine ritual to two sports

Sumo began as an offering. In Shinto ritual, wrestling was performed to
divine an outcome or to ensure a good harvest. Parts of that ritual survive
unchanged in the professional sport. The salt a wrestler throws into the
<Term id="dohyo">dohyō</Term> purifies it. The <Term id="gyoji">gyōji</Term>,
the referee, dresses as a Shinto priest. The roof that hangs over the ring in
a professional arena is a shrine roof.

<Photos ids="sumo-salt sumo-hanging-roof" />

Sumo at the imperial court is recorded from the eighth century. Professional
sumo as a public show dates from the Edo period. Paid wrestlers performed for
crowds, and the ring, the rankings and the stables took a shape that is still
recognisable.

<Photos ids="sumo-shunsho-1785 sumo-kunisada-1854" />

The modern institution is much younger than the practice. The national
professional association was reorganised in 1925. Regional and house
traditions had differed, and the new body wrote [one rulebook for
all](#sumo-loss-conditions). That body, the Japan Sumo Association, still runs
professional sumo on its own. It holds six tournaments a year, runs the
stables where wrestlers live and train, and keeps the ranking ladder.

<Photo id="sumo-dohyo-iri-2014" />

## Two sumos

The International Sumo Federation was formed in 1992 to govern amateur sumo
outside Japan. It made two decisions the professional body had never made. It
[introduced weight classes](#sumo-1992-ifs-founded), and it [admitted
women](#sumo-womens-competition). Sumo joined the World Games at Akita, in
northern Japan, [in 2001](#sumo-2001-world-games).

<Photos ids="sumo-hetal-dave sumo-women-worlds-2014" />

So there are two sumos. The federation holds the world championships and the
World Games place, but it does not govern a single professional wrestler. The
professional association does not enter world championships. The best
wrestlers in the world are in Japan, and they never meet the rest.
