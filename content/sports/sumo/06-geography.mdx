# Where it is played

Professional sumo exists in Japan and nowhere else. Amateur sumo has a map, and
it is more interesting than you would expect.

**Mongolia** is the outstanding case. Mongolian wrestlers have dominated the
top of the Japanese professional ranks for extended periods, drawing on a
national wrestling tradition — bökh — that transfers unusually well, and on a
pathway into Japanese stables that has been well established for decades. The
professional association's limits on foreign recruitment exist substantially
because of this.

Beyond Mongolia, amateur sumo has real presence in **Brazil**, through the
Japanese-Brazilian community, which is one of the largest Japanese diaspora
populations anywhere; in **Eastern Europe**, particularly Russia, Ukraine,
Georgia, Bulgaria and Estonia, drawing on their own belt-wrestling and heavy
grappling traditions; in **the United States**, especially Hawaii, which
produced several professional champions; and in Egypt, China and across the
Pacific.

The pattern is that sumo travels where a country already has a heavy grappling
culture. It is not a sport that arrives somewhere with no wrestling tradition
and takes root.

The women's game exists only in the amateur sport and is strongest in the same
places — Japan's university system, Eastern Europe, and Brazil — which produces
the odd situation of a country's women competing at a world championship in a
sport whose professional form would not let them stand in the ring.
