# Controversies that forced rule changes

**Weight, and the refusal to class it.** Professional sumo has no weight
divisions, mass is therefore a direct competitive advantage in a small ring,
and wrestlers eat and train to be enormous. The health outcomes — diabetes,
joint failure, shortened life expectancy — are documented and are a consequence
of a competitive structure rather than of an accident. The international
amateur federation introduced weight classes the moment it was founded. The
professional sport has not, and this site records that as an unresolved
argument rather than a settled tradition.

**Women and the dohyō.** Women may not enter the professional ring at all. The
prohibition has produced repeated public incidents, most notably one in which
women who climbed into the ring to give medical aid to a collapsed official
were asked over the public address system to leave. The association's stated
basis is that the dohyō is a sacred space; critics describe it as
discrimination maintained under a religious description. Amateur sumo has had
women's world championships since 1997.

**Foreign wrestlers.** Mongolian, Eastern European, Hawaiian and other
foreign-born wrestlers have held the sport's highest ranks for long stretches,
and the professional association has imposed limits on how many a stable may
recruit. It is a nationality restriction of a kind that would be unthinkable in
most sports covered here.

**Hazing and stable discipline.** Deaths and serious injuries arising from
violence within stables have forced changes to the professional sport's
internal discipline, and are the reason the association's governance has been
scrutinised by Japanese authorities rather than by any sporting body.

**Match-fixing.** Investigations into arranged bouts led to tournament
cancellation and expulsions. Because a bout has no score, a thrown sumo match
is unusually difficult to detect from the record.
