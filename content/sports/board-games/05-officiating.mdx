# Officiating

An arbiter decides nothing about the game and almost everything about the
conditions.

The position on the board is a fact. The rules apply to it mechanically:
whether a king is in checkmate, whether a stone group is captured, whether a
move is legal are not matters of judgement in the way that every other
officiating decision on this site is. There is nothing to see and nothing to
mark.

What an arbiter does instead:

**Enforces the clock.** Exceeding the time control loses the game, and in
fast time controls the difference between a flag falling and a move being
completed is decided by a human watching two hands and a display.

**Handles illegal moves.** These carry escalating consequences — added time to
the opponent, then loss of the game — and the arbiter must reconstruct what
happened from a position that has already moved on.

**Runs the anti-cheating regime.** Screening on entry, control of the playing
area, monitoring absences from the board, and referring suspicious games for
statistical analysis. This is now the substantial part of the job at any event
that matters.

**Applies the tie-breaks.** Tournaments are resolved by published systems
applied in order, and an arbiter computing a tie-break is doing arithmetic that
decides a title.

The comparison worth drawing is with boules and orienteering, elsewhere on this
site: three sports where the result is a fact rather than a judgement, and the
officials exist almost entirely to police the conditions under which the fact
was produced.
