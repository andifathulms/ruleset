# Controversies that forced rule changes

**Cheating with an engine.** The defining modern controversy, and it is unlike
any other integrity problem on this site. In a physical sport, assistance
improves a competitor. Here it replaces them — a player relaying engine moves is
not advantaged but unbeatable, and the difference is why the anti-cheating
regime is so intrusive: screening on entry, prohibition of devices, delayed
broadcast, restrictions on leaving the board, and statistical comparison of a
player's moves against engine choices.

That last method is remarkable in itself. It detects cheating by measuring how
*good* the moves were, on the reasoning that beyond a certain agreement with an
engine there is no human explanation.

**The end of adjournment.** Games were once sealed overnight and resumed.
Engines made the overnight analysis decisive, and the practice was abolished.
A rule removed entirely because a machine made it meaningless.

**Draw arrangement.** Two players can agree a result that suits both and harms
the competition. Some events prohibit draw offers before a set move number, and
prearranged results are an integrity offence — a rule regulating collusion
between opponents rather than misconduct by one.

**Rating inflation.** Whether a rating today means what it meant decades ago is
argued seriously, and the formula and its parameters have been adjusted more
than once. It is a comparability problem in the sport's own measuring
instrument.

**Who is world champion.** Chess ran two rival championship lineages through a
long schism, and the sport's title was genuinely ambiguous for over a decade.
