# Equipment evolution

A board, pieces and a clock — and then a category of regulation about objects
the player is forbidden to carry.

**The board and pieces** have been effectively stable for centuries. Chess
standardised on the Staunton pattern in the nineteenth century so that pieces
would be unambiguous across countries, which is a genuine equipment
standardisation and the last one the game needed.

**The clock** is the only technology the sport added, and it changed the game
more than it looks. Before it, a player could think indefinitely and games were
abandoned or decided by exhaustion. With it, time became a resource to be
spent, and different time controls turned into different disciplines with
separate world championships and separate ratings. Increment systems — seconds
added per move — exist so that a won position cannot be lost purely to the
clock.

**Adjournment** was equipment-adjacent and is gone. Unfinished games were once
sealed and resumed the following day, and overnight analysis was part of the
craft. Engines made that analysis decisive rather than skilful, and the
practice was abolished.

**What is banned** is now the larger part of the equipment rules. No electronic
device may be carried into the playing area, players are screened on entry, and
broadcasts of moves are delayed so a transmitted position cannot be analysed
live and relayed back.

That is the equipment story of these games in one sentence: nothing the player
uses has changed in a century, and everything they are forbidden to use has
been invented in the last thirty years.
