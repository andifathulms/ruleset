# Three games, three histories

Chess, go and xiangqi have separate origins. They have no players in common
and no shared governing body.

## Chess

Chess descends from chaturanga, a game played in India. It reached Persia as
shatranj, moved through the Islamic world and came into Europe. The pieces
had their names and shapes long before the modern moves. In the fifteenth
century the queen and the bishop gained their long-range moves, and that made
the game fast.

[The international chess federation, FIDE, was founded in
1924](#board-1924-fide). Before it, each country played to its own
conventions, and no body could run a world championship.

## Go

Go is the oldest of the three. It is called weiqi in Chinese and baduk in
Korean, and it has been recorded in China for well over two thousand years.
It is probably the oldest board game still played in its original form. From
China it went to Korea and Japan. Japan built the professional houses,
schools and ranks that the modern game inherited. The International Go
Federation dates from 1982.

## Xiangqi

Xiangqi shares its ancestry with chess and went its own way in China. It
gained a <Term id="river">river</Term> across the board and a
<Term id="palace">palace</Term> that holds the general, and its pieces stand
on the <Term id="intersection">intersections</Term>. Its international
federation dates from 1993.

## One listing

What the three share is a shape: two people, a board, nothing hidden and no
luck. [The Asian Games put them together under one listing at Guangzhou in
2010](#board-2010-asian-games). The host was China, where go and xiangqi are
very widely played. [Boules](/sports/boules/) and
[billiards](/sports/billiards/) are grouped in the same way, because a
multi-sport programme has one entry per sport.
