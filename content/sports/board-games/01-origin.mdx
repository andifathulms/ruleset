# Origin and invention

Three games, three origins, and one Asian Games listing that groups them for
administrative convenience.

**Chess** descends from chaturanga in India, reached Persia as shatranj, moved
into the Islamic world and then into Europe, where the modern moves settled in
the fifteenth century — the queen and bishop acquired their long-range powers
then, which is what made chess fast enough to be the game it is. FIDE was
founded in 1924.

**Go** — weiqi in Chinese, baduk in Korean — is the oldest of the three and
probably the oldest board game still played in its original form, attested in
China for well over two thousand years. It went to Korea and Japan, and it was
Japan that built the professional structures, the schools and the ranking
system that the modern game inherited. The International Go Federation dates
from 1982.

**Xiangqi** shares chess's ancestry and diverged in China, acquiring the river
across the board, the palace that confines the general, and pieces played on
intersections rather than squares. Its international federation dates from
1993.

They have no players in common and no shared governing body. What they have is
a shape — two people, a board, complete information, no luck — and it was
enough for the Asian Games to contest them together from Guangzhou in 2010, in
a host country where go and xiangqi have very deep participation.

The most consequential events in all three games' histories are much more
recent than any of this, and neither was a rule change. A machine beat the
world chess champion in 1997, and one beat a top go professional in 2016.
