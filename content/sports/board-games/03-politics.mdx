# Governing body politics

Three federations, one Asian Games listing, and an Olympic recognition that has
led nowhere for thirty years.

**FIDE** is the oldest and largest, founded in 1924, and it holds IOC
recognition. Chess has never been on an Olympic programme and there is no
serious prospect of it. The federation runs a world championship cycle, a title
system and the rating, and it has had a turbulent political history —
contested presidencies, a long schism in the 1990s and 2000s during which two
rival world championship lineages ran in parallel, and sustained questions
about its governance and its funding sources.

**The International Go Federation** and **the World Xiangqi Federation** are
smaller and quieter, and both sit beneath national professional systems that
matter more than they do. Professional go in China, Japan and South Korea has
its own titles, its own money and its own ranking, and an international
federation does not govern any of that.

The Asian Games listing from 2010 grouped all three, in the same way boules and
billiards group unrelated codes: the multi-sport system recognises one entry
per sport, so unrelated games are presented together in order to be present at
all.

The genuine political question these games face is not about programmes. It is
that their competitive life has moved onto commercial online platforms with
more rated players than every federation combined, and those platforms run
their own events, their own ratings and their own anti-cheating regimes. The
governing bodies are no longer where the game happens.
