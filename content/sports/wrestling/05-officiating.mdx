# Officiating and technology

Wrestling uses three officials where most combat sports use one, and the reason
is that its scoring actions are frequently invisible.

A referee on the mat, a judge at the side and a mat chairman must agree on
points, because a turn or an exposure often happens inside a tangle of two
bodies and the person with the best view changes constantly. Points are awarded
by majority, which distributes a judgement nobody can make alone.

**Video review** exists as a coach's challenge and works well, because most
wrestling questions are questions of fact: did the shoulders touch, did the
foot cross the line, was the opponent exposed. A camera answers those.

**Passivity** is the exception and the sport's hardest call. Deciding that a
wrestler is not genuinely trying to score is a judgement about intent over
time, and it is the same problem judo has with shido and boxing has with
holding.

Wrestling's answer is the most interesting officiating move on this page: it
converted the judgement into a **clock**. A wrestler warned for passivity has a
set time to score or concede a point. The referee still has to make the initial
call, but the consequence is now mechanical and visible rather than
accumulating silently in an official's head.

That is the same instrument basketball invented in 1954, applied to a combat
sport sixty-four years later, for exactly the same problem — a competitor with
no obligation to act. Wrestling arrived at it independently and late, and it
works for the same reason.
