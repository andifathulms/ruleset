# Three officials and a clock

A wrestling <Term id="bout">bout</Term> has three officials: a referee on the
mat, a judge at the side and a mat chairman. A turn or an
<Term id="exposure">exposure</Term> often happens inside a tangle of two
bodies, and the person with the best view keeps changing. So points are given
by majority.

<Photos ids="wrestling-rio-referee wrestling-rio-scoreboard" />

A coach can ask for a <Term id="challenge">challenge</Term>, and the officials
look at the video. This works well, because most questions in wrestling are
questions of fact. Did the shoulders touch? Did the foot cross the line? Was
the back exposed? A camera can answer those.

## The hardest call

<Term id="passivity">Passivity</Term> is different. To call it, the referee
has to judge whether a wrestler is really trying to score, over a stretch of
time.

Wrestling's answer was a clock. [Since 2018 a wrestler warned for passivity
goes on a shot clock](#uww-2018-passivity-shot-clock) and must score within a
set time or give up a point. The referee still makes the first call. What
follows is mechanical, and everyone in the hall can see it.
[Basketball](/sports/basketball/) invented the same tool in 1954 for the same
problem: a competitor who is under no pressure to act.

Greco-Roman needed more. With no attacks below the waist, it is easy for two
wrestlers to cancel each other out. [Since 2017 a passive Greco-Roman
wrestler is put in par terre](#uww-2016-greco-parterre), on hands and knees
with the opponent behind, so the opponent gets a real chance to throw.
