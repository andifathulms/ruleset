# Governing body politics

Cycling's governance has been dominated for thirty years by doping, and it would be dishonest to write this section without saying so. The sport's credibility collapse in the 2000s, the retrospective stripping of results, and the reconstruction of anti-doping under an independent body are the central facts of its modern administration.

This site does not cover doping rules, and the reason is worth stating: doping regulation is not sport-specific. It is administered under a common code across almost every sport here, so it tells you nothing about what distinguishes one sport's rulebook from another's. Cycling's equipment rules do.

The equipment politics are genuinely cycling's own, and they have a consistent shape: an individual or a manufacturer finds an advantage inside the rules, and the federation writes a rule against it afterwards. Obree in 1994 and 1996, disc wheels and low positions before that, frame shapes after. The Lugano Charter was an attempt to stop legislating case by case by defining the object once, and it has largely worked.

The hour record is where that politics is most visible, because the federation had to decide not only what equipment was legal but what the record was *for*. Its answer in 1997 was that the record should measure the rider; its answer in 2014 was that a record nobody attempts measures nothing. Both are defensible and they contradict each other, which is the most honest thing in this sport's rulebook.
