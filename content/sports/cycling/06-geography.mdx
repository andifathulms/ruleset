# Where it is played

Cycling's participation is close to universal and its elite geography is narrowly European, and the gap between those two facts is larger than in any sport here except swimming.

Bicycles are the most widely owned piece of sports equipment on earth, and in much of the world they are transport rather than recreation. But competitive road cycling is concentrated in a belt running from Belgium and the Netherlands through France, Italy and Spain, with Britain, Denmark, Colombia, Australia and Slovenia as significant additions in the last two decades.

The reason is infrastructure of a particular kind: not facilities but a race calendar. A young rider needs a dense programme of amateur races within travelling distance, a club structure to enter them through, and roads that can be closed. Colombia's emergence rests on altitude and a domestic racing culture; Britain's rests on a track programme funded from lottery money in the late 1990s, which is one of the most legible examples anywhere of money producing medals.

The track discipline is different again, and its geography is a map of velodromes. There are few of them, they are expensive, and countries that built them win.

The hour record has no geography at all, which is part of its appeal. It requires one rider, one track and one hour, and it has been set in Mexico City, Manchester, Grenchen and Roubaix by riders from six countries.
