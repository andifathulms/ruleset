# Who makes the calls

The officials of a bicycle race are called
<Term id="commissaire">commissaires</Term>, and they do two jobs that have
little in common.

## Timing and placing

This job is done by instruments and is rarely disputed. Track events are
timed electronically to a thousandth of a second. A photograph of the finish
settles a close sprint. On the road, each rider carries a transponder.

## Checking the machine

This is where the effort goes. Before an event the commissaires measure the
bicycles against the numbers of the Lugano Charter: the shape of the frame,
the position of the saddle, the reach of the handlebars, the weight. The work
is closer to a customs inspection than to refereeing.

Since 2016 the inspection also looks for hidden motors. Officials use
magnetic resonance devices and thermal cameras at races to find a machine
that the eye cannot see. This is the check for
<Term id="technological-fraud">technological fraud</Term>.

## What nobody sees

The road is the hard part. A <Term id="road-race">road race</Term> is a
moving convoy spread over 200 kilometres, watched by a small number of
officials in cars and on motorcycles. A rider who shelters behind a team car,
takes a bottle outside the permitted zone, or holds onto a vehicle on a climb
is often out of their sight. The rules against these things exist, and much
of the time they cannot be enforced.
