# Controversies that forced rule changes

**Obree's bicycle, 1993.** An amateur built a machine in his kitchen, rode it in a position nobody had tried, and took the sport's oldest record. Within a year the position was illegal. Within three years his second invention was illegal too. Nothing either machine did was against any rule at the time, and both were faster.

The federation's stated grounds were safety and the definition of a bicycle. The competing reading is that a governing body was embarrassed by an outsider and legislated him out. This site records both, and notes that a rule written to exclude one person's idea is a recognisable pattern here — basketball did it repeatedly to Wilt Chamberlain.

**Boardman's 56.375 km, 1996.** The Superman position produced a distance so far beyond what a conventional bicycle could achieve that it effectively ended the record as a contest. The UCI's response the following year was the most drastic act in this collection: it split the record in two, moved every aerodynamic mark to a separate list, and reinstated Eddy Merckx's 1972 distance as the target.

For seventeen years the sport had two official answers to how far a person had ridden in an hour, differing by seven kilometres. Almost nobody attempted the restricted one.

**Reunification, 2014.** The federation reversed itself. The restricted record had produced two rides in seventeen years, and the reason was obvious: riders were being asked to train and attempt on equipment they never otherwise used. Merging the lists brought attempts back immediately — five in the following five years — and the mark passed Boardman's 1996 distance in 2022, twenty-six years later.

**The motor, 2016.** A concealed motor was found in a bicycle at a world championship. The sport had spent two decades arguing about frame tubes and handlebar reach, and the thing that finally arrived was an engine.
