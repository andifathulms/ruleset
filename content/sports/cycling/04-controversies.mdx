# The hour record, and an engine in a frame

## An amateur takes the oldest record

In 1993 Graeme Obree rode 51.596 km in an hour on a bicycle he had built
himself. He was an amateur, and his riding position was his own invention.
Within a year [the position was illegal](#uci-1994-praying-mantis-ban).
Within three years [his second invention was illegal
too](#uci-1996-superman-ban). Neither had broken any rule when he first used
it.

The federation gave two reasons: safety, and the definition of a bicycle.
Many people read it differently. In their account a governing body had been
beaten by an outsider on a homemade machine, and wrote rules to keep him out.
Both accounts are on record.
[Basketball](/sports/basketball/) wrote rules against one player, Wilt
Chamberlain, in the same way.

## 56.375 kilometres

In 1996 Chris Boardman rode Obree's second position, the Superman, to 56.375
km. The position was worth several kilometres an hour and needed no extra
power from the rider. Since 1984 the record had moved forward by more than
seven kilometres, almost all of it through position and equipment.

[In 1997 the federation split the record in two](#uci-1997-hour-record-split).
The official <Term id="hour-record">hour record</Term> was limited to
equipment like that of Eddy Merckx in 1972: a steel frame with round tubes,
spoked wheels, and no aerodynamic handlebars. Every distance set on a modern
machine was moved to a separate list, called the Best Human Effort. The
record to beat went back to the 49.431 km Merckx had ridden in Mexico City
twenty-five years earlier.

<Photo id="cycling-merckx-bike-1972" />

The federation said it wanted the record to measure the rider again. Critics
said it had cancelled rides that were legal when they were made.

For seventeen years cycling had two official answers to how far a person
could ride in an hour, and they were seven kilometres apart. Almost nobody
tried for the restricted one. It was beaten twice in that time. In 2000
Boardman himself rode 49.441 km on Merckx's kind of bicycle, ten metres
further than Merckx. In 2005 Ondřej Sosenka rode 49.700 km.

## Put back together

[In 2014 the federation merged the two lists](#uci-2014-hour-record-unified).
Riders could again use any bicycle that was legal for track endurance events,
with an aerodynamic frame and
<Term id="disc-wheel">disc wheels</Term>. The mark to beat was Sosenka's
49.700 km. Boardman's 56.375 km stayed out of reach for now.

The federation said it wanted riders and sponsors to care about the record
again, by letting riders use the bicycles they already trained and raced on.
Others read the change as an admission that the split had failed.

Attempts came back at once. Jens Voigt rode 51.115 km in 2014, the first
attempt under the new rules. Rohan Dennis and Bradley Wiggins both raised the
record in 2015, and Victor Campenaerts did in 2019. In 2022 Filippo Ganna
rode 56.792 km. It was the first ride longer than Boardman's, twenty-six
years after it.

## The motor

For two decades the sport argued about frame tubes and the reach of
handlebars. Then a motor was found hidden inside a bicycle at the 2016
cyclo-cross world championships. It was the first confirmed case in
competition, after years of rumour.
[Racing with a hidden motor is now a named
offence](#uci-2016-technological-fraud), called technological fraud, with suspensions of years and large fines, and frames are scanned at races.

## Doping

For thirty years the governing of cycling has been dominated by doping. The
sport lost its credibility in the 2000s. Results were taken away from riders
years after the races, and anti-doping was rebuilt under an independent body.
The doping rules themselves belong to a code shared by almost every sport.
The equipment rules are cycling's own.
