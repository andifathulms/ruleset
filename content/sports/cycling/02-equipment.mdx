# What counts as a bicycle

In cycling the machine provides a large part of the speed, so the rules
describe the bicycle in more detail than they describe the race.

## Faster without a stronger rider

In 1984 Francesco Moser rode 51.151 km in an hour. The record had stood for
twelve years, and he added more than a kilometre and a half to it. He was the
first to do it on <Term id="disc-wheel">disc wheels</Term>, and he rode in a
very low position.

In 1993 Graeme Obree, an amateur from Scotland, took the record on a bicycle
he had built himself, partly from washing-machine bearings. He rode with his
arms folded under his chest, a position nobody had used before. [The
federation banned that position in 1994](#uci-1994-praying-mantis-ban). Obree
then invented a second one, with the arms stretched straight out in front,
called the Superman. [It was banned in 1996](#uci-1996-superman-ban). Both
positions were legal when he first rode them, and both were faster.

## The Lugano Charter

The federation then stopped ruling on one invention at a time and defined the
bicycle once. [The Lugano Charter, in force from
2000](#uci-2000-lugano-charter), says a racing bicycle has a
<Term id="double-triangle">double-triangle frame</Term> made of tubes within
stated dimensions. It sets a minimum weight of 6.8 kg. It limits the design
of the wheels and where the rider may sit relative to the
<Term id="bottom-bracket">bottom bracket</Term>.

The federation's stated reason was that free development would turn the sport
into a contest between manufacturers, and would shut out riders and teams
with less money.

The Lotus 108, which Chris Boardman rode to the Olympic pursuit gold medal in
1992, shows what the Charter excluded. It was made in one piece of carbon,
with no tubes and no double triangle.
