# Equipment evolution

No sport on this site has a closer relationship between its equipment and its rules, because in cycling the equipment is most of the performance.

**The 1980s and the disc wheel.** Francesco Moser's 1984 hour record used disc wheels and a radically low position, and added more than a kilometre and a half to a mark that had stood for twelve years. Almost none of it came from the rider.

**Graeme Obree.** In 1993 an amateur Scot built a bicycle partly from washing-machine bearings, invented a tucked position nobody had used, and took the hour record. The UCI banned the position in 1994. He invented a second one — arms extended straight ahead, the Superman — and Chris Boardman used it in 1996 to ride 56.375 km. The UCI banned that too. Two rules, written against one man's ideas, both of which had been entirely legal.

**The Lugano Charter.** In force from 2000, it settled the underlying question by defining a bicycle as a shape: a double-triangle frame of tubes within stated dimensions, a minimum weight of 6.8 kg, limits on where the rider may sit. Cycling decided that a bicycle is a thing that looks like a bicycle, and everything since has been an argument about the edges of that definition.

**Concealed motors.** The one equipment problem the sport did not see coming. A motor found in a frame at the 2016 cyclo-cross world championships forced the UCI to define technological fraud as an offence and to start scanning frames — an equipment rule about an object that is not supposed to be there at all.
