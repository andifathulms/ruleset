# Officiating

Players call their own faults, and call an official over when they would rather
not.

Croquet is self-officiated in ordinary play, and the reason is practical: most
faults are visible only to the person committing them. Whether a mallet pushed
rather than struck the ball, whether contact was momentary or sustained,
whether both balls moved in a croquet stroke — these happen at the striker's
feet and nobody else can see them.

So the laws place the obligation on the striker, and croquet's culture around
that is strict. Calling a fault against yourself in a match you are winning is
expected rather than admirable.

**Referees exist and are requested.** A player may call one to watch a
difficult stroke before playing it — a hampered shot near a hoop, or a croquet
stroke where a fault is likely — which is an unusual arrangement: the official
is summoned in advance to observe a specific act, rather than positioned to
watch everything.

**Contested strokes** and questions of law go to a referee or, in tournaments,
to a tournament referee whose ruling stands.

That structure puts croquet alongside ultimate, orienteering and boules on this
site: sports where the competitor is trusted with the adjudication, for
different reasons. Ultimate made it a principle, orienteering replaced it with
an electronic record, boules can settle its central question with a ruler, and
croquet does it because nobody else has the angle.
