# Equipment evolution

A mallet, four balls, six hoops and a peg — and the hoops are the part that
became a precision instrument.

**The hoops** are where croquet's difficulty lives. A garden set has hoops
comfortably wider than the ball, and running one is a formality. A championship
hoop is set with a clearance measured in fractions of a millimetre, firmly in
ground prepared so it will not move, and running one from any distance or angle
is a genuine shot.

That single specification is most of the difference between the game people
have played at a party and the sport. It is the same relationship this site
records between a pub pool table and a competition snooker table, or a
recreational bowling lane and a sport oil pattern: the equipment has been
tightened until the activity is hard.

**The mallet** moved from wood to composite shafts with heads of dense
materials, and it is struck with either face. Head length, weight and balance
are personal in the way a putter is.

**The balls** are milled and matched, and the sport's regulation of them is
about consistency rather than performance: four balls that behave identically
matter enormously in a game where you are using three of them as tools.

**The lawn** is the largest piece of equipment and the real barrier. It must be
level and mown to a consistent speed, which is a groundskeeping problem rather
than a purchase, and it is why croquet exists where clubs exist and almost
nowhere else.
