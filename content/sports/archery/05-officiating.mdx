# Officiating and technology

Archery is the easiest sport on this site to officiate correctly, and it has
almost no officiating controversies as a result. An arrow is either in a ring or
touching a line, which is a question of fact with a physical answer that can be
inspected at leisure by two people standing at the target.

Where technology has gone is scoring speed and broadcast. Electronic scoring
systems read arrow position optically and produce a score before the archers
have walked to the target, which serves the audience more than the result.
Cameras behind the target and on the bow have made the sport far more legible
than it was.

The genuinely difficult call is the line-cutter — an arrow whose shaft touches
the boundary between two rings — and the sport's answer is characteristic: it
did not improve the judgement, it removed the incentive. Limiting shaft
diameter to 9.3 mm in 2014 reduced how often the question arises and how much
is gained by engineering for it.

Timing is the other officiated thing, and it is mechanical: twenty seconds,
alternating, with lights. An arrow shot after the clock forfeits the
highest-scoring arrow of that end, which is a penalty that requires no
interpretation at all.

What archery shows, set against gymnastics at the other extreme, is what a
sport's officiating looks like when its central question has a correct answer.
There is no panel, no review protocol, no statistical monitoring of judges, and
no equivalent of the handball law. The whole apparatus that gymnastics and
football have built exists to manage judgement, and archery has almost none to
manage.
