# An old weapon, a young sport

The bow has been in use for tens of thousands of years. Shooting it at a
ringed target for points is far younger.

<Photo id="archery-morella" />

When armies gave up the bow, target shooting survived in England as a pastime
of the gentry. Archery societies kept it going through the eighteenth and
nineteenth centuries, and they wrote the first fixed rounds: set distances, a
set number of arrows and a scoring target. The Grand National Archery Society
dates from 1861.

## Four Games, four different contests

Archery was shot at the Olympic Games of 1900, 1904, 1908 and 1920, and each
host ran it by its own rules. In St. Louis in 1904 every archer was American,
and the tournament doubled as the American national championship. London in
1908 held British rounds and, beside them, a separate event shot in the French
and Belgian style. Eugène Grisot of France won that one with 263 points from
62 hits. The four tournaments had almost nothing in common.

<Photos ids="archery-st-louis-1904 archery-grisot-1908" />

After 1920 archery was dropped, partly for that reason. No international body
existed to give it one set of rules. The Fédération Internationale de Tir à
l'Arc, known as FITA, was founded in 1931 to be that body. Archery came back
to the Games in 1972, fifty-two years after it left. FITA renamed itself World
Archery in 2011.

So the rules of competition are a twentieth-century invention laid over a very
old skill. Archery has no ancient custom of how a contest should run, and the
sport has been willing to rewrite its format more than once.
