# Officiating and technology

Squash asks its officials a question no other sport on this site asks: what
would have happened?

The rules require a player to give their opponent unobstructed access to the
ball, a free swing, and the ability to play it to the front wall. When that
fails, the referee chooses between three outcomes. A **let** replays the point.
A **stroke** awards it outright. **No let** means play should have continued.

Deciding between them requires answering a counterfactual — could the
obstructed player have reached the ball, and would the shot have been a winner,
and was the obstruction avoidable. Every other officiating decision on this
site is about something that happened. Squash's central one is about something
that did not.

That is why the sport uses three officials at professional level rather than
one, voting on appeals, and why **video review** arrived in 2019 without
settling anything. A camera shows where the players were. It does not show
whether a player could have got there, or what they would have done if they
had.

The comparison worth drawing is with fencing's priority and football's
handball. All three are judgements about structure or intent rather than about
position, and all three have resisted every instrument thrown at them. Squash's
version is the hardest of the three, because it is explicitly hypothetical.

Everything else in squash officiates cleanly. The ball is in or it is not; the
serve is legal or it is not. The sport has one problem, it has had it since the
1800s, and it comes directly from putting both players in the same box.
