# The hardest call: what would have happened?

Most of squash is easy to referee. The ball is up or it is down. The serve is
good or it is not. One decision is different, and it comes up in rally after
rally.

Both players are on the same floor. The player who has just hit the ball must
give the other a clear path to it, room to swing, and a clear line to the
<Term id="front-wall">front wall</Term>. Often that does not happen. The
blocked player stops and asks, and the referee has three answers.

- A <Term id="let">let</Term>: the rally is played again.
- A <Term id="stroke">stroke</Term>: the blocked player wins the point.
- <Term id="no-let">No let</Term>: the rally stands, and the player who
  stopped has lost it.

<Photos ids="squash-farag-asal squash-farag-asal-2" />

To choose, the referee has to answer questions about something that never
took place. Could the player have reached the ball? Would the shot have been
a winner? Could the opponent have moved out of the way? Other calls in sport
are about what happened. This one is about what did not.

That is why the professional game uses three officials, who vote when a
player appeals. [Video review was added in
2019](#wsf-2019-video-review) for lets, strokes and whether the ball was
still up, with a limited number of failed reviews in a match. It has not
ended the arguments. A camera shows where the players were. It cannot show
whether a player would have got to the ball.

[Fencing](/sports/fencing/) has a call of the same family in its priority
rule, and [football](/sports/football/) has one in handball. All three ask
the official to judge more than the position of a ball or a body, and none
has been settled by a machine.
