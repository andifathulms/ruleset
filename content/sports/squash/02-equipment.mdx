# Glass walls, a lower tin and a slow ball

For most of its history squash could not be watched. An ordinary court is a
closed box with four solid walls and a small gallery above and behind the
back wall. There is no place for a crowd and no place for a camera.

<Photo id="squash-hong-kong-centre" />

## The glass court

[In the early 1980s courts with glass walls were approved for
championships](#wsf-1980s-glass-court). The game stayed the same, and the
walls changed. A crowd can sit on all four sides of a glass court, and a
camera can film through it.

<Photos ids="squash-british-open-1981 squash-pyramids" />

A glass court can also be taken apart and built somewhere else. Championship
squash has been played in the concourse of a railway station in New York and
in front of the Pyramids of Giza.

<Photo id="squash-nantes" />

## The tin

The <Term id="tin">tin</Term> is the strip at the bottom of the
<Term id="front-wall">front wall</Term> that the ball must clear. By 2000 the
top level of the game had become a test of fitness. Rallies were very long
and were decided by who tired first. [In 2001 the tin was lowered from 19
inches to 17 for professional men](#wsf-2001-ball-and-tin). A lower tin makes
the target larger, so a player who tries to finish the rally is rewarded.
[Basketball](/sports/basketball/) did something similar with its three-point
arc: it changed a measurement of the court to change how the game is played.

## The ball

Squash balls are graded by speed and marked with coloured dots. Professionals
use the slowest, the <Term id="double-yellow-dot">double yellow dot</Term>,
because a slower ball makes longer rallies. The ball bounces properly only
when it is warm, so players hit it back and forth before a match to heat it.

<Photo id="squash-ball" />

Rackets went from wood to composite materials with no trouble for the record
books. Squash keeps no measured records that a better racket could break.
