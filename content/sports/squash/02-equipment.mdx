# Equipment evolution

Squash's equipment history has one item nothing else on this site has: it
rebuilt its playing area out of a different material so that people could watch.

A conventional squash court is a sealed box with four solid walls and a small
gallery behind one of them. There is nowhere for a camera and nowhere for a
crowd. For most of the sport's history it was genuinely unwatchable — not
uninteresting, physically impossible to see.

**The all-glass court**, from the early 1980s, solved it. Championship squash
is now played in a transparent box that can be assembled anywhere, in a
railway station or in front of the Pyramids, with an audience on all four
sides. It is the only case here of a sport changing the substance of its venue
for the sole purpose of being visible.

**The tin** is the other structural change. The out-of-play strip at the bottom
of the front wall was lowered from nineteen inches to seventeen for
professional men's play in 2001, enlarging the target and rewarding a player
who attacks rather than retrieves. Adjusting a court dimension to change how a
sport is played is the same instrument basketball used with its three-point arc
and fencing used with its timing constants.

**The ball** is graded by speed, and the professional ball is the slowest,
because a slower ball produces longer rallies. It only behaves correctly once
warm, which makes it the only implement on this site whose properties depend on
how long play has been going on.

Rackets went from wood to composite without a comparability crisis, because
squash keeps no numbers a racket could break.
