# Where it is played

Squash's map is the British Empire again, with two significant additions and a
serious structural problem underneath.

Egypt is the dominant nation by a distance, in both the men's and women's game,
and has been for two decades — an unusually complete dominance built on a
concentrated club system in Cairo and Alexandria and a coaching tradition that
compounds. Pakistan's earlier dominance, through the Khan dynasty, was similar
in kind and lasted from the 1950s into the 1990s.

Beyond those: England, Australia, New Zealand, Malaysia, France, and increasing
strength in the United States.

The structural problem is the court. Squash needs a purpose-built enclosed
space, and in Britain and Australia in particular those courts are being
converted to gyms and studios, because a squash court is a poor use of floor
area for a commercial operator. A sport losing facilities loses participants,
and squash has been doing so in some of its historic strongholds for twenty
years.

That makes the Olympic admission for 2028 more consequential than it would be
for most sports here. Squash campaigned for a quarter of a century partly
because visibility drives facility investment, and its federation has been
explicit that the Games are a means to that end.

Egypt's rise is the counter-example and the encouraging one: a national
concentration built without much wealth, on courts that already existed and a
coaching culture that did not.
