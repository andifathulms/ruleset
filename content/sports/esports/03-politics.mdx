# Who owns the game

A <Term id="publisher">publisher</Term> owns its game outright. It decides the
competitive rules. It licenses the game for an event or refuses to. It can
run its own league, and it can stop supporting the game, which ends the sport
built on it.

<Photo id="esports-the-international-2014" />

The biggest competitions are run by publishers and by commercial organisers
working under a publisher's licence. That is where the money, the audience
and the professional players are.

## Two federations

The International Esports Federation, formed in 2008, and the Global Esports
Federation, formed in 2019, both have national member associations, world
championships and anti-doping rules. Their mandates overlap, and their
relationship has been uneasy. They govern the conditions of competition.
Neither controls the rules of a single game.

## The Olympic line

The IOC has drawn its own line. [Its virtual sports events are built on
simulations of physical sports](#esports-2021-olympic-content-line), such as
virtual cycling, rowing, archery and taekwondo. Its stated reason is that
games showing violence do not fit Olympic values. That rules out most of the
games esports is made of commercially.

<Photo id="esports-olympic-esports-week-2023" />

Much of the industry says a programme of sports simulations misses the sport,
because those games have small competitive scenes. The IOC says its values
are not negotiable. So there are two kinds of esports: the kind the Olympic
movement will stage, and the kind with the audiences and the professionals.

The Asian Games chose the second kind. Hangzhou had League of Legends, Dota
2, Arena of Valor, EA Sports FC Online, PUBG Mobile and Street Fighter V.
Several were played in versions the publishers made for the event, with
in-game purchases removed and content adjusted. The owner of the field of
play rebuilt it to suit the programme.
