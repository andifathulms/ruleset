# Governing body politics

Two international federations, neither of which governs a game, and a set of
companies that do.

The **International Esports Federation** (2008) and the **Global Esports
Federation** (2019) both have national member associations, world
championships, anti-doping compliance and the institutional apparatus that
recognition requires. Their mandates overlap and their relationship has been
uneasy.

Neither has the thing that matters. A publisher owns its game outright, decides
its competitive rules, licenses or refuses to license it for any event, runs
its own franchised league if it chooses, and can discontinue the title. This
site records fragmented governance in powerlifting and kickboxing, and
professional structures outside the federation in sumo, muaythai and snooker.
Esports is a further category again: the federations do not govern a diminished
version of the sport, they govern none of its laws.

The **IOC's position** adds a second split. Its virtual sports events have been
built around simulations of physical sports — virtual cycling, rowing, archery,
taekwondo — on the stated ground that games depicting violence do not align
with Olympic values. That is a content criterion, and it excludes most of the
titles esports is commercially made of.

So there are effectively two esports. The one the Olympic movement will stage,
which has small competitive scenes, and the one with the audiences, the money
and the professionals, which it will not.

The Asian Games took the second one. Hangzhou 2022 contested League of Legends,
Dota 2, PUBG Mobile and Street Fighter V — with publishers producing modified
versions for the event, which is the clearest possible demonstration of who
holds the pen.
