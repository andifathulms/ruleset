# Controversies that forced rule changes

**Match fixing.** Esports' most serious integrity problem and one it has at
scale. Heavy betting markets, young players, low pay in the tiers below the
top, and unregulated leagues have produced fixing cases in many countries,
including criminal prosecutions and lifetime bans. The structural conditions
are worse than in most sports here, and the response — integrity units,
betting monitoring, education — is the conventional one.

**Cheating software.** Aim assistance, wall vision and input automation are the
sport's doping equivalent, and detection runs through anti-cheat software on
the machine plus statistical analysis of inputs. As in chess, the assistance
does not improve a competitor so much as replace them.

**Player welfare.** Very young professionals, extremely long practice hours,
repetitive strain injuries, and contracts that have been found exploitative
have all produced reform in the larger leagues — minimum ages, contract
standards, mandated rest. It is a labour history more than a sporting one,
because the employers are companies rather than federations.

**Who governs.** Two international federations with overlapping mandates and no
control over any game, alongside publishers who need neither.

**The Olympic content line.** A programme built on sports simulations rather
than on the games people actually compete in. The industry regards it as
missing the sport; the IOC regards its values as not negotiable. Nothing has
moved.

**Patches mid-tournament.** Which version a competition is played on, and
whether a patch may land inside a season, is a competition rule the organisers
do control — and it exists because the game would otherwise change underneath
the event.
