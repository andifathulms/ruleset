# The referee and the server

No official judges the play in esports. Whether a shot hit, whether an
ability landed, whether a player was inside a zone: the server has already
calculated it, and the result cannot be appealed.

So a referee's work is about everything around the game.

**Pauses.** A player may call a
<Term id="technical-pause">technical pause</Term> for a hardware failure, a
software fault or a lost connection. In a serious case the game gets a
<Term id="remake">remake</Term>. Both are needed because the field of play
can fail. Both can also be abused to break an opponent's momentum, so every
use is regulated and logged.

**The version.** Officials decide which <Term id="version">version</Term> a
competition is played on, and whether a patch may arrive during an event.
Without that rule the game could change in the middle of a tournament.

**Outside information.** <Term id="ghosting">Ghosting</Term> is the reason
booths and headsets exist, and officials police the room for it.

## Cheating and fixing

Cheating software is the sport's version of doping. Aim assistance, seeing
through walls and automated inputs all exist. Anti-cheat software on the
machines looks for them, and so does statistical analysis of what each player
does. As in chess, among the [board games](/sports/board-games/), this kind
of help does more than improve a competitor. It replaces them.

Match fixing is the most serious integrity problem. Betting markets are
heavy, players are young, and pay is low in the leagues below the top. Cases
in many countries have ended in criminal prosecutions and lifetime bans. The
response has been integrity units, monitoring of betting, and education.

Players also share accounts, which is a form of impersonation. The sport has
to police it because a player's identity is a login.
