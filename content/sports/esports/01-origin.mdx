# From a university lab to the arena

People have competed at video games for as long as there have been video
games. In 1972 a tournament was held at Stanford in Spacewar, a game written
ten years earlier. Competition then grew through arcade high scores in the
1980s and games played over networks in the 1990s.

It became a professional spectator sport in South Korea in the early 2000s.
The Korean leagues were shown on television, and their players were paid
salaries.

<Photos ids="esports-spacewar-pdp1 esports-gomtv-2011" />

No governing body ever owned the games. The laws of
[football](/sports/football/) belong to a board, and the technical rules of
[athletics](/sports/athletics/) belong to a federation. The rules of an
esport are software, and the software belongs to the
<Term id="publisher">publisher</Term> that made it.

## Federations and the Games

[The International Esports Federation was formed in South Korea in
2008](#esports-2008-iesf). The Global Esports Federation followed in 2019.
Both have national members and world championships. Neither owns a game, and
both must ask a publisher's permission to stage one.

Recognition by multi-sport games came first in Asia. [Esports was a
demonstration event at the 2018 Asian Games in
Jakarta](#esports-2018-asian-games-demo). [It was a full medal sport at
Hangzhou](#esports-2022-medal-sport), at Games named for 2022 and held in
2023 after a postponement.

The IOC has run [virtual sports events since
2021](#esports-2021-olympic-content-line). They are built around simulations
of physical sports, and the big competitive games are left out.
