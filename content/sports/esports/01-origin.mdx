# Origin and invention

Esports has no founding rulebook, because it has never had rules of its own.

Competitive video gaming is as old as video games — a Spacewar tournament was
held at Stanford in 1972 — and it grew through arcade high-score competition in
the 1980s, networked play in the 1990s, and the South Korean broadcast leagues
of the early 2000s, which are where esports first became a professional
spectator sport with televised leagues and salaried players.

What it did not develop, at any point, was a governing body that owned the
games.

That is the difference between esports and everything else on this site.
Football's laws belong to a board. Athletics' technical rules belong to a
federation. Even the sports here whose professional structures sit outside
their federations — sumo, muaythai, snooker — have laws that some sporting body
wrote. Esports' laws are software, and the software belongs to the companies
that made it.

The International Esports Federation was formed in South Korea in 2008 and the
Global Esports Federation in 2019, both with national members, world
championships and the apparatus of a recognised sport. Neither owns a game.
Both must ask a publisher's permission to stage one.

Multi-sport recognition came from Asia first: a demonstration at the 2018 Asian
Games in Jakarta, and full medal status at Hangzhou in 2022 — a Games named for
2022 and held in 2023 after postponement, at which several titles were
contested in versions their publishers had modified for the occasion.

The IOC has run virtual sports events since 2021 built around simulations of
physical sports rather than the games esports is actually played on.
