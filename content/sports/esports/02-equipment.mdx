# Equipment evolution

The equipment question splits cleanly in two, and the interesting half is not
hardware.

**The hardware is standardised.** Organisers supply identical machines, and the
one thing competitors bring is peripherals — mouse, keyboard, controller,
headset — which are personal, inspected, and as individual as a fencer's grip
or a bowler's drilled ball. Settings are locked to a tournament configuration
and macros are generally prohibited.

**The software is the field of play, and it is owned.** Every element that
would be a law of the game in any other sport — what a weapon does, how fast a
character moves, what a map's sightlines are, how the economy works — is a
value in a build, and the build changes on a cycle of weeks.

That makes esports' equivalent of an equipment arms race unlike any other on
this site. There is no javelin to redesign or bat to certify, because the
implement is not a physical object anyone owns: it is a version number.

Two pieces of physical equipment exist for reasons worth recording. **Booths
and noise-cancelling headsets** stop crowd reaction telling a team where the
opposition is — a spectator gasping at the right moment is unauthorised
information, the same problem bridge built screens to solve. And **anti-cheat
software** runs on the competition machines, which is a class of equipment that
inspects the competitor rather than serving them.

The peripheral market is the sport's only conventional equipment industry, and
it behaves like every other one: incremental gains, sponsorship, and claims
about latency that are difficult to verify.
