# Machines, booths and the game itself

The hardware in esports is kept the same for everyone. The game is the part
that changes.

Organisers supply identical machines. Players bring only their
<Term id="peripherals">peripherals</Term>, which are personal and are
inspected. A player's mouse is as individual as the grip of a sword in
[fencing](/sports/fencing/) or a drilled ball in
[bowling](/sports/bowling/). Settings are locked to the tournament's
configuration, and a <Term id="macro">macro</Term> is generally banned.

## Keeping the crowd out

A spectator who gasps at the right moment tells a team where its opponents
are. So teams play in a <Term id="booth">booth</Term>, or wear
noise-cancelling headsets. [Bridge](/sports/bridge/) built screens across its
tables to solve the same problem.

The competition machines also run <Term id="anti-cheat">anti-cheat</Term>
software. It is unusual equipment, because its job is to inspect the
competitor.

## The software

Everything that would be a law of the game in another sport is a value in the
software: what a weapon does, how fast a character moves, what can be seen
from each point on a <Term id="map">map</Term>, how the in-game economy
works. [The publisher owns that software](#esports-publisher-owns-the-field)
and changes it with a <Term id="patch">patch</Term>, usually every few weeks.
A season is played across several versions, and teams learn parts of the game
again between tournaments.

Esports has no implement to redesign and no bat to certify. Its equipment
race is a version number.
