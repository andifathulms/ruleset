# From the gymnasium to the pool

Diving began as a way for gymnasts to land. In Germany and Sweden in the
nineteenth century, gymnasts took their somersaults outdoors over water. Over
time, what they did in the air came to matter more than how they got there.

The Olympic Games took the sport up a piece at a time. St Louis in 1904 held
a platform event and a plunge for distance. In the plunge, a diver pushed off
and floated face down as far as possible without moving. It was never held
again.

<Photos ids="diving-dickey-1904 diving-plunge-1904" />

London added the <Term id="springboard">springboard</Term> in 1908. Stockholm
held the first women's event in 1912, from the platform, and Greta Johansson
of Sweden won it.

<Photos ids="diving-london-1908 diving-johansson-1912" />

From 1912 to 1924 the Games also held plain high diving, which allowed only
simple headers from the platform. It was then dropped. From 1928 there were
four events: springboard and platform, for men and for women. That programme
stayed the same until 2000, when [four synchronised events were
added](#ioc-2000-synchronised-diving).

<Photos ids="diving-plain-high-1912 diving-java-1947" />

Diving has been governed together with swimming since FINA was founded in
London in 1908. The federation has been called World Aquatics since 2023. Its
technical diving committee writes the diving rules and keeps the tables of
difficulty.

Those tables make the sport partly a matter of arithmetic. Every dive has a
<Term id="degree-of-difficulty">degree of difficulty</Term>, worked out by
adding five published values. Its worth is known before the diver leaves the
board, and the judges mark only how well it is done.
[Gymnastics](/sports/gymnastics/) did not separate the two until 2006.
