# Tack, helmets and fences that give way

Most equipment rules in equestrian sport are there to protect the horse.

Every piece of <Term id="tack">tack</Term> is specified: bits, nosebands,
spurs and whips. The specifications have tightened steadily. The
<Term id="noseband">noseband</Term> is the clearest example. A tight one
stops a horse opening its mouth and can hide resistance, so its tightness is
now measured with a gauge at competition. [Badminton](/sports/badminton/) did
something similar when it fixed its service height: a measurement replaced a
judgement.

Whips are limited in length, in how they may be used and in how many times.
<Term id="steward">Stewards</Term> watch the warm-up arena as closely as the
competition arena.

Rider safety equipment followed injuries and deaths. Helmets became
compulsory, then body protectors for cross-country. Air jackets became common
without being required.

## The fixed fence

A <Term id="cross-country">cross-country</Term> fence is fixed. It does not
fall when a horse hits it, and that is what makes it dangerous. The worst
accident is a rotational fall, in which the horse turns over the fence. Most
eventing deaths happen that way.

<Photos ids="equestrian-chatsworth-fence equestrian-frangible-pin" />

The <Term id="frangible-pin">frangible pin</Term> was first used in eventing
in 2002. It holds the rail and gives way under a set load, so the fence
collapses before it can turn the horse over. Pins and similar devices have
reduced rotational falls. It is the change with the largest effect, and a
spectator can hardly see it.
