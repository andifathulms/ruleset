# Equipment evolution

Equestrian equipment rules are welfare rules wearing a different name, and the
sport has stopped pretending otherwise.

**Tack.** Bits, nosebands, spurs and whips are each specified, and the
specifications have tightened steadily. The noseband is the clearest example: a
tight one restricts a horse's ability to open its mouth and can mask
resistance, and it is now measured with a gauge at competition. That is a
measurement replacing a judgement, the same move badminton made with its
service height — except here the thing being measured is discomfort.

**Whips** are limited in length, in how they may be used, and in how many times.
Stewards police the warm-up arena as closely as the competition arena, because
the warm-up is where the problems occur.

**Safety equipment for riders** followed injury and death: helmets became
compulsory, then body protectors for cross-country, then air jackets became
common without being mandated.

**Frangible obstacles** are the most consequential change and the least
visible. Cross-country fences are fixed by definition — that is what makes them
dangerous — and the introduction of pins and devices designed to give way under
a specific load has reduced rotational falls, which are the mechanism behind
most eventing fatalities.

The pattern across all of it is unlike any other sport here. Elsewhere,
equipment rules exist to keep competition fair or to stop the numbers running
away. In equestrian they exist because one of the two competitors cannot
complain.
