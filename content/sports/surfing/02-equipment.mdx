# Equipment evolution

Surfing's equipment history is enormous and almost none of it is regulated,
because in a judged sport with unequal conditions there is no number for
equipment to break.

Boards went from solid wood weighing sixty kilograms to balsa to foam and
fibreglass, and the shortboard revolution of the late 1960s cut length by
several feet and made the modern high-performance manoeuvres possible. Fin
configurations changed the sport again in the 1980s. Wetsuits opened cold-water
coastlines that had been unsurfable.

The ISA regulates almost none of it. Olympic competition is on shortboards and
a leash is required, and beyond that a surfer chooses their board for the wave.
Choosing wrongly is a competitive error, not a rules matter.

That is a coherent position for a judged sport whose conditions are already
uncontrolled: adding equipment rules would not make anything comparable,
because nothing is comparable anyway.

**The wave pool changes this completely**, and the sport has not fully worked
out what follows. A machine-generated wave is equipment — an artificial field
of play with settings, manufactured by companies with competing technologies.
Once the wave is a product, its specification becomes a rule, and the
federation will have to write one.

This is the position cycling reached with the Lugano Charter and athletics
reached with shoe regulations, and surfing is about to arrive at it from a
direction no other sport has: by manufacturing its playing surface for the
first time.
