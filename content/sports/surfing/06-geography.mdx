# Where it is played

Surfing has the most rigidly geographic map of any sport on this site, and the
constraint is absolute: you need a coast with rideable waves.

Australia, the United States — principally Hawaii and California — Brazil,
Portugal, France, South Africa, Indonesia, Japan and Peru produce essentially
all elite competitors. Brazil's rise over the last fifteen years is the most
significant recent shift and was built on a large coastal population and a
domestic competitive culture.

There is no version of this map that includes a landlocked country, and no
amount of investment changes it. Rowing needs a river and rivers are widely
distributed; swimming needs a pool and pools can be built. Surf cannot be
found, funded or manufactured.

Until it can. The wave pool is the first technology in the sport's history that
would let somebody train, compete and reach an elite level without an ocean —
and there are pools in Europe, North America and the Middle East, in places
with no coastline worth surfing.

That makes the 2028 venue decision a geography decision as much as a fairness
one. A sport that has been the exclusive property of coastlines for a thousand
years is about to become available to anyone who can build a facility, which is
the same transition swimming made when pools became common.

Hawaii's position is different from all of this and worth stating: it is the
sport's origin, retains an authority in it that no governing body confers, and
its breaks remain the ones by which surfers measure themselves.
