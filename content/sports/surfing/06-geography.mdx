# Where the waves are

Surfing needs a coast with waves that can be ridden, and most of the world
does not have one.

Nearly all top surfers come from Australia, the United States (mainly Hawaii
and California), Brazil, Portugal, France, South Africa, Indonesia, Japan and
Peru. Brazil is the newest power. Its rise over about fifteen years was built
on a large population living by the sea and a strong contest scene at home.
Gabriel Medina became Brazil's first world champion in 2014.

<Photos ids="surfing-medina-2012 surfing-carissa-moore" />

No landlocked country is on that list, and money alone has never changed it.
[Rowing](/sports/rowing/) needs a river, and rivers are common.
[Swimming](/sports/swimming/) needs a pool, and a pool can be built. For most
of its history surf could not be built.

Now it can. There are <Term id="wave-pool">wave pools</Term> in Europe, North
America and the Middle East, some of them far from any coast worth surfing.
One stands in fields outside Edinburgh. In Munich people surf a standing wave
in a city river, hundreds of kilometres from the ocean. A person can now learn
to surf, and train, without living near the sea.

<Photos ids="surfing-lost-shore surfing-eisbach" />

Hawaii has a place of its own. It is where surfing began, and no federation
gave it that standing. Surfers still measure themselves against its breaks.
