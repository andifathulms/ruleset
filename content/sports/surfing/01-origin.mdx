# From Hawaii to the world

Surfing comes from Polynesia. In Hawaii it is called *heʻe nalu*, and it was
part of social life, ritual and rank. There were particular boards for
particular classes of people, and <Term id="break">breaks</Term> kept for
them.

<Photos ids="surfing-surf-playing-1851 surfing-surf-riding-1858" />

Calvinist missionaries arrived in the nineteenth century and discouraged
surfing, along with much else. By the late 1800s very few people still rode
waves.

<Photo id="surfing-niihau-1890" />

A small number of Hawaiians brought it back in the early twentieth century.
The best known is Duke Kahanamoku, an Olympic champion in
[swimming](/sports/swimming/). He showed surfing to crowds in California and
in Australia, and his demonstrations there in 1914 and 1915 are where the
sport took hold in that country.

<Photos ids="surfing-duke-kahanamoku surfing-waikiki-1920" />

## Rules came late

For most of its history surfing had no rules, because it had no contests.
Contests began in the middle of the twentieth century, and the rulebook is
entirely modern. It had to answer a question that surfing for pleasure never
asks: how do you compare two people riding different waves?

The answers are the <Term id="heat">heat</Term>, the rule of
<Term id="priority">priority</Term>, and a score made from each surfer's two
best waves. [Priority](#isa-priority-system) decides who may choose a wave.
[Two-wave scoring](#isa-two-wave-scoring) stops a surfer from winning just by
catching more of them.

The international federation dates from 1964. Kahanamoku had campaigned for an
Olympic place for surfing about a century before it got one. The sport [was
admitted for the 2020 Games](#isa-2016-olympic-inclusion), and the first
Olympic heats were surfed in 2021 at Tsurigasaki beach in Chiba, Japan. Ítalo
Ferreira of Brazil won the men's gold medal, and Carissa Moore of Hawaii won
the women's.
