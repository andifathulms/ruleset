# Origin and invention

Surfing is the only sport on this site that a colonial administration
deliberately tried to suppress and that survived.

Wave riding is Polynesian, and in Hawaii — where it is *heʻe nalu* — it was
woven into social structure, ritual and rank, with specific boards for
specific classes and dedicated breaks. Calvinist missionaries arriving in the
nineteenth century discouraged it along with much else, and by the late 1800s
it had contracted severely.

Its revival is attributable to a small number of Hawaiians in the early
twentieth century, Duke Kahanamoku above all — an Olympic swimming champion who
demonstrated surfing in Australia and California and is the reason the sport
travelled.

That origin sits awkwardly with what this site does. Surfing did not have rules
until it had competitions, and it did not have competitions until the middle of
the twentieth century. Its rulebook is entirely modern and was written to
solve a problem the activity itself does not have: how to compare two people
riding different waves.

The ISA dates from 1964. Surfing entered the Olympic programme in 2020, and
Duke Kahanamoku had campaigned for exactly that a century earlier.

The tension between surfing as a practice and surfing as a scored competition
has never resolved, and the wave pool decision has made it sharper than it has
ever been.
