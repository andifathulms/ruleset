# Two games, one name

The name sepak takraw is a compromise between two countries. *Sepak* is the
Malay word for kick. *Takraw* is the Thai word for the woven ball.

The game behind the name is much older. For centuries people across Southeast
Asia have kept a woven rattan ball in the air without using their hands. It is
*sepak raga* in the Malay world, *takraw* in Thailand, *chinlone* in Burma,
*sipa* in the Philippines and *da cau* in Vietnam. Most of these were
<Term id="circle-game">circle games</Term>. A group kept the ball up together.
They were admired for grace and for how long the ball stayed up, and nobody
won.

A version with a net and two sides appeared in the twentieth century. Malaysia
and Thailand both had strong claims to it. They had different names for it and
different rules, and competition between countries needed one code.

## Kuala Lumpur, 1965

Representatives met in Kuala Lumpur in 1965 and [wrote one sport from the
Malay and Thai forms](#takraw-1965-codification). They joined the two words so
that neither country's name for the game became the sport's name.

The game they wrote down is the game played now: three a side, a net, a court
the size of a [badminton](/sports/badminton/) court, three
<Term id="touch">touches</Term>, and no hands.

They kept one thing from the circle games. [One player may take all three
touches](#takraw-three-touches-one-player). [Volleyball](/sports/volleyball/)
forbids a player from touching the ball twice in a row, and that single
difference separates the two sports more than anything else. A sepak takraw
player can control the ball, set it and <Term id="spike">spike</Term> it
alone.

<Photo id="sepak-takraw-hangzhou-2023" />

The new sport entered the Southeast Asian Games at once. It [became a full
medal sport at the Asian Games in Beijing in 1990](#takraw-1990-asian-games),
the same Games that added [kabaddi](/sports/kabaddi/). Thailand has won the
great majority of the titles since.
