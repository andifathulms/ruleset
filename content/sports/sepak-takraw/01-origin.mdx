# Origin and invention

Sepak takraw's name is a treaty.

Games in which a woven rattan ball is kept airborne without using the hands are
recorded across Southeast Asia for centuries — *sepak raga* in the Malay world,
*takraw* in Thailand, *chinlone* in Burma, *sipa* in the Philippines, *da cau*
in Vietnam. Most were circle games: a group keeping the ball up cooperatively,
judged by grace and duration rather than won.

The competitive net version appears in the twentieth century, and the crucial
moment is 1965, when representatives met in Kuala Lumpur and codified one sport
from the Malay and Thai forms. The name they adopted — *sepak* from Malay,
*takraw* from Thai — was a compromise so that neither country's word for the
game became the sport's. Almost no other sport on this site carries the
diplomacy of its founding in its title.

What they wrote is the game described here: three a side, a badminton-sized
court, a net, three touches, and contact with feet, knees, chest and head but
never the hands. It kept one thing from the circle games that volleyball's
rules forbid — a single player may take all three touches — and that
inheritance is why the sport plays as it does.

It entered the Southeast Asian Games immediately and became a full Asian Games
medal sport at Beijing in 1990, the same edition that added kabaddi. Thailand
has won the great majority of titles since.

It has never been on an Olympic programme, and unlike most sports covered here
it is not on the World Games one either — its only multi-sport home is
continental.
