# Governing body politics

The founding act was a negotiation between two countries, and the sport's
politics have been about the balance between them ever since.

Malaysia and Thailand both had strong claims to the game, different names for
it and different rules, and the 1965 codification in Kuala Lumpur settled both.
The compound name is the visible result; the rulebook is the substantive one.
The Asian Sepaktakraw Federation followed, and international governance now
runs through the International Sepaktakraw Federation.

Two things have shaped the sport's institutional life since.

**Thai dominance.** Thailand has won the overwhelming majority of Asian Games
titles and world championships, with Malaysia the main challenger and Myanmar,
Indonesia, Vietnam, South Korea and Japan competing. A sport that was founded
as a two-country compromise has been won by one of them ever since, which
complicates every argument about universality.

**Regional confinement.** Sepak takraw is a fixture of the Southeast Asian
Games and the Asian Games and appears on no global multi-sport programme —
not the Olympics, and not the World Games either, which is unusual for a sport
of its organisational maturity. Its only multi-sport home is continental.

The federation's development work targets exactly that: introducing the sport
in Europe, the Americas and Africa, where it has small but real footholds. The
obstacle is not equipment cost, which is negligible, but the technical wall.
A sport whose basic rally requires striking a ball above head height with the
foot is hard to establish in a population with no childhood exposure to it.
