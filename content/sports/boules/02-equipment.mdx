# Equipment evolution

Boules went from wood, to wood with nails, to steel, and the change altered the
game more than the dates suggest.

Traditional boules were turned from boxwood root. Through the nineteenth
century they were reinforced with hammered nails — *boules cloutées* — driven
into the surface in patterns, which made them heavier, more durable and, as it
turned out, decorative. Making them was a craft trade.

Steel boules arrived in the 1920s and standardised everything. A modern
competition boule is hollow steel, 650 to 800 grams and 70.5 to 80 millimetres
across, and a player chooses within those limits deliberately: heavier boules
sit down where they land and suit a *pointeur*, lighter ones travel and suit a
*tireur*, and hardness affects how much a boule rebounds when struck.

That is a genuine equipment-fitting discipline, and it is regulated the way
this site has seen elsewhere — by dimension and weight rather than by
standardisation, so choice remains part of the sport. It is the same instrument
finswimming uses for the monofin.

The **jack** is a small wooden ball, and it is the sport's most interesting
piece of equipment because it is the target and it moves. Strike it and the
whole geometry of the end changes, which is a property no target in archery,
shooting or golf has.

Everything else is absent. No footwear requirement, no clothing rules of
consequence, no protective equipment. What competition does add is a
**measuring device** — callipers and tape — used by the players themselves,
mid-game, to settle which boule is nearer.
