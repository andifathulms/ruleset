# Officiating

Boules is largely self-officiated, and the reason is that its central question
is answerable.

Which boule is nearer? That is a fact, and the players determine it themselves
with callipers and a tape, in the middle of the game, by agreement. In
recreational play there is no official at all, and in competition the umpire is
called only when the players cannot agree or when the measurement is
consequential enough that neither wants to make it.

This puts boules in a small group on this site with ultimate, which removed
officials entirely, and orienteering, which replaced them with an electronic
record. The difference is that boules' self-officiation covers a measurable
fact rather than a foul, which is why it works without a Spirit of the Game
clause to hold it up.

What officials do judge is behaviour:

**The circle.** Did a foot lift or leave before the boule landed? Watched
closely, and the most common technical offence.

**The ground.** Was a stone removed, a rut smoothed, a hole filled? Prohibited
in the target area, and tempting precisely because the terrain is
unstandardised.

**Displacement.** If a boule or the jack is moved by something other than play
— a spectator, an animal, a player's foot — the rules prescribe how the
position is restored or the end voided, and this requires an official's ruling
about what would have happened.

There is no instrumentation beyond the callipers, and none is needed. The
sport's hardest question is the one it can answer with a ruler.
