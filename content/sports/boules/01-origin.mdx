# A game made for one man

<Term id="petanque">Pétanque</Term> began when the run-up was taken out of an
older game so that one player could go on playing.

Throwing games are ancient. They were played around the Mediterranean for
centuries. By the early twentieth century the main French form was *jeu
provençal*. A player took a three-step run-up before letting go, and threw to
a <Term id="jack">jack</Term> fifteen or twenty metres away. It was a game for
people who could run.

## La Ciotat, 1907

The story the sport tells is set in La Ciotat, a town near Marseille. Jules
Lenoir played jeu provençal there with his friends. Rheumatism had made the
run-up impossible for him. So the friends [changed the
rules](#petanque-1907-feet-planted). Both feet would stay together on the
ground, inside a small <Term id="circle">circle</Term>. The jack would be
thrown much closer. The Provençal words for feet planted, *pieds tanqués*,
gave the new game its name.

This is folk history. No committee wrote the decision down, and the sport's
own memorial does not agree with the usual date. The plaque at the boulodrome
in La Ciotat says the game was created there in 1910, and spells it
"pied-tanqué".

## What the change did

A throw made standing still can be made by anyone who can stand, at any age,
on any rough ground. Pétanque spread across France in a way its parent game
never did. It is played in town squares by whoever turns up. Jeu provençal is
still played in the south, and pétanque is what France plays.

<Photos ids="boules-glackens-1930 boules-drome-village" />

The game also travelled beyond Provence, into the territories France then
ruled, and local habits grew up about distances and boules. [An international
federation was formed in 1958](#petanque-1958-fipjp). It fixed the rules for
everyone: games to thirteen points, the size and weight of the boule, the
circle, and how far the jack must be thrown.
