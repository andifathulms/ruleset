# The tip of the cue

The most important piece of equipment in cue sports is a small disc of leather
on the end of the stick.

The cue appeared in the seventeenth century. The mace it replaced did not
vanish at once: a drawing in a billiards book of 1859 still shows a cue
and a mace in use at the same table.

<Photo id="billiards-cue-and-mace" />

The leather tip came in the early nineteenth century. With it a player could
strike the cue ball away from its centre without the cue slipping off. Side
spin, backspin and topspin all became possible, and so did control of where
the cue ball stops. That control, called
<Term id="position">position</Term>, is what separates players. Chalk on the
tip followed for the same reason.

## Balls, cloth and tables

Balls were once made of ivory. Ivory varied in weight and bounce from one ball
to the next, and it came from elephants. Composite materials replaced it and
made every ball the same.

<Photo id="billiards-ivory" />

Cloth and cushion rubber have improved steadily, and tables are now faster and
more predictable. Snooker's highest possible
<Term id="break">break</Term>, 147, has become somewhat easier to reach as a
result. It is still rare. Compare the perfect 300 in
[bowling](/sports/bowling/), which reactive resin balls made far more common.

The three tables cannot stand in for each other. A carom table is about ten
feet by five, has no pockets, and is often heated to keep the cloth fast. A
pool table is nine feet by four and a half with six generous pockets. A
snooker table is twelve by six, with pockets cut tight to the ball, so a long
<Term id="pot">pot</Term> is much harder there than at pool.
