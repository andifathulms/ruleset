# Equipment evolution

Three tables that cannot substitute for each other, and a cue that has been
refined for four centuries.

**The tables** are the sport's whole argument about itself. A **carom** table
is about ten feet by five and has **no pockets**; it is often heated, to keep
the cloth fast and consistent. A **pool** table is nine by four and a half with
six generously cut pockets. A **snooker** table is twelve by six with pockets
cut tight relative to the ball, which is why potting over distance is far
harder there than on a pool table and why the two games reward different
things.

**The cue** replaced the mace in the seventeenth century, and the decisive
addition was the **leather tip** in the early nineteenth, which allowed a player
to strike the cue ball off centre without miscuing. That is the invention the
modern game rests on: side, screw and topspin all became available, and cue
ball control — the thing that actually separates players — became possible.

**Chalk** followed for the same reason, and the two together are the most
consequential equipment change in the sport's history.

**Balls** moved from ivory to composites, which standardised weight and
elasticity and removed both the variability and the elephants.

**Cloth** and cushion rubber have improved steadily, making tables faster and
more predictable. That matters for one comparison this site cares about:
snooker's maximum break of 147 has become somewhat more attainable as
conditions improved, but nothing like the way bowling's 300 was devalued by
reactive resin. The number still means close to what it meant.
