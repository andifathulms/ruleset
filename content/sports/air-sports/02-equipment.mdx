# The aircraft

In air sports the equipment is an aircraft. It can be a
<Term id="sailplane">sailplane</Term> with a wingspan of fifteen metres or
more, a paraglider that packs into a rucksack, or a racing drone that fits in
a hand.

## Gliders and their classes

Gliders went from wood and fabric to composite materials. The figure that
matters is the <Term id="glide-ratio">glide ratio</Term>, and it improved
enormously. A modern open-class sailplane glides more than sixty metres
forward for each metre it sinks.

<Photos ids="air-grunau-baby air-eta" />

A better glider costs more money, so gliding divides its aircraft into a
<Term id="class">class</Term> system: standard, 15-metre, 18-metre, open and
club. Each class limits the wingspan or fixes a standard design, and the open
class sets no limit on span. This keeps a championship a contest between
pilots. [Sailing](/sports/sailing/) uses classes for the same reason.

## Wings you can carry

Paragliders and hang gliders went the other way, towards equipment that is
carried up a hill and launched on foot. They are the cheapest way into the
air, with no tow and no airfield.

<Photo id="air-hang-glider" />

Parachutes became steerable canopies shaped like a wing. A descent became a
flight, and new disciplines such as
<Term id="canopy-piloting">canopy piloting</Term> were built on flying the
canopy.

## The drone

A racing drone costs very little. The pilot builds it and repairs it, and it
is expected to crash. It is the only disposable aircraft in the federation,
and that is why drone racing is so much easier to start than any other air
sport.

<Photo id="air-racing-drone" />

Parachutes, paragliders and hang gliders must be certified as safe to fly.
Few sports need a rule of that kind. Elsewhere a failure of equipment loses
the contest. In the air it can cost much more.
