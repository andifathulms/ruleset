# Governing body politics

The FAI's problem is that it governs something magnificent that nobody can
watch.

Its disciplines are among the most spectacular activities humans do, and almost
all of them happen out of sight. A championship gliding task covers hundreds of
kilometres of open country over five hours; the spectator experience is an
empty airfield and, eventually, some aircraft coming back. Ballooning and
cross-country hang gliding have the same problem. Skydiving formation work
happens at terminal velocity, thousands of metres up.

Everything the federation has done competitively in recent decades follows from
this. The disciplines it takes to the World Games — paragliding accuracy,
canopy piloting, glider aerobatics, drone racing — are precisely the ones that
can be staged in a venue with an audience and a finish line.

**Drone racing** is the sharpest example. It grew entirely outside the
federation, organised by commercial leagues and online communities, and it is
the first discipline the FAI has that is natively watchable: a stadium, lit
gates, a race lasting a minute, and a camera feed the audience sees exactly as
the pilot does. The federation brought it inside in 2018.

The other half of FAI politics is not sport at all. As the ratifier of absolute
aeronautical records it deals with manufacturers, air forces and space
programmes, and its definitions carry weight outside its own membership. A body
whose competition arm is a niche pursuit is simultaneously the accepted
authority on where space begins.

There is no Olympic prospect and, unusually, very little sign the federation
wants one.
