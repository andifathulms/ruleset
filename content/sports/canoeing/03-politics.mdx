# Governing body politics

The ICF's defining recent decision was correcting an exclusion it had
maintained for eighty-five years.

Until the 2020 Games, Olympic women could compete in kayak events only. The
canoe classes — kneeling, single blade — were men's events. Women's canoe world
championship events had existed for years without reaching the Games, and the
reasons historically offered for the exclusion did not survive examination.

It took the IOC's gender-parity requirement to force it, and because the
athlete quota is fixed, women's canoe events arrived by removing men's events.
That is the same arithmetic rowing faced when it cut lightweight events and
shooting faced when it removed three men's events for mixed teams: parity
inside a quota means exchange, and something gets deleted.

This is the latest such correction of any sport on this site. Boxing admitted
women in 2012; canoeing's canoe classes waited until 2021.

The federation's other structural problem is that it governs two sports.
Sprint's constituency is flat-water clubs; slalom's is whitewater, requires
expensive artificial courses, and has a much narrower geography. They compete
for the same quota and the same attention, and kayak cross — added for 2024 —
is slalom's attempt to become more watchable without becoming sprint.

The artificial course question underlies everything in slalom. A sport that
needs a purpose-built engineered channel has a facility barrier it did not
choose and cannot easily reverse.
