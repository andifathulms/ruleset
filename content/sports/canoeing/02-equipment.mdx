# Equipment evolution

Canoeing's equipment rules are about stopping boats getting narrower, and its
most consequential equipment change was not a boat at all.

**The boats** went from wood and canvas to fibreglass to carbon composite, and
each material made them lighter and stiffer. Minimum weights and lengths by
class hold that in check. Slalom boats acquired a **minimum width** after
composite construction let them become extremely narrow — narrower is faster
through a gate and less stable, and without a limit the sport was heading for
an equipment race with a stability cost.

That is the same instrument cycling used in the Lugano Charter: define the
shape and stop the arms race, rather than banning a specific design after it
appears.

**The artificial course** is the change that matters most, and it is a venue
rather than equipment. Munich built the first purpose-made whitewater channel
for the 1972 Games, and slalom has been migrating from rivers to engineered
channels ever since.

The effect parallels hockey's move to artificial turf: a controllable,
schedulable, spectator-friendly surface replacing a natural one, at a cost.
Whitewater courses are expensive facilities, there are not many, and a country
without one cannot develop slalom paddlers.

Unlike hockey, canoeing did not mandate the change — it simply became necessary
to be competitive, which is the quieter version of the same thing.

**Rudders** are permitted in sprint kayak and not in canoe, which is why
holding a straight line in a canoe is a skill and in a kayak is a fitting.
