# Governing body politics

Golf's rules are owned jointly by the R&A and the USGA, who divide the world
between them and must agree on any change. It is a partnership of equals with
no arbiter, which makes the Rules unusually stable and unusually slow.

The recurring political question is **distance**, and the argument has run for
thirty years. One side holds that increasing driving distance is making
historic courses obsolete, forcing lengthening that costs land, money and
water, and that the governing bodies have a duty to act. The other holds that
distance is not a problem, that manufacturers and players have invested in the
current game, and that rolling back equipment punishes everyone for what a few
hundred professionals can do.

The 2023 decision to change the ball testing conditions from 2028 is the
governing bodies choosing the first position after decades of not choosing.
Manufacturers and a substantial part of the professional game opposed it.

**Bifurcation** is the deeper question underneath. The proposal could have
applied only to elite competition, leaving everyday golfers alone — and the
governing bodies withdrew that version. Golf's distinctive claim is that
everyone plays the same game under the same rules, from a club medal to a major
championship, and bifurcation would have ended it. They preferred to change the
ball for everybody.

The other institutional feature worth recording is that golf's four major
championships are run by four separate organisations, two of which own the
Rules. The sport's most important competitions are not governed by any single
body, which is why their play-off formats still differ.
