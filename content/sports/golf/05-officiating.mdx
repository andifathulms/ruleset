# Officiating and technology

Golf is officiated by the people playing it, and takes that seriously enough
that it is close to the sport's identity.

A player who breaks a rule is expected to call the penalty on themselves,
including when nobody saw and nobody would ever know. Referees and rules
officials exist and are consulted for procedure, but they cannot watch
everyone: a field is spread across two hundred acres.

The nearest relative on this site is sailing, where a boat that has broken a
right-of-way rule takes its own penalty turn on the water without an official
intervening. Both sports have strong amateur traditions and both treat
self-enforcement as constitutive rather than convenient.

**Television** broke this for a while, and the way it broke is worth recording.
High-definition slow motion could see things no player or official could,
viewers telephoned breaches in, and players were penalised for infractions they
could not have detected — but only if they had been on camera. A sport that
enforces its rules through the honesty of participants ended up with
enforcement determined by broadcast schedule.

The 2019 rewrite closed it: call-in penalties ended, and a player is not
penalised for a breach that could not reasonably have been seen.

That is a rare direction of travel on this site. Almost everywhere else,
technology revealed what officials were missing and the sport adopted it.
Golf's technology revealed more than the sport wanted enforced, and the sport
declined it.
