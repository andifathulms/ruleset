# Thirteen rules on one page

The oldest written rules of golf that survive come from Leith, in Scotland,
in 1744. There were thirteen articles, drafted for a competition, and they fit
on a single page.

<Photo id="golf-leith-1867" />

The main principle was already there and has not moved: play the ball as it
lies. Everything difficult in the modern rulebook follows from that. The ball
may end up anywhere, and the player may not move it to somewhere fair. The
book has grown from one page to a small volume as the game found out how many
places a ball can come to rest.

## Two owners, one book

Two bodies became the keepers of the Rules: the R&A at St Andrews and the
United States Golf Association, the USGA, founded in 1894. They still own the
Rules jointly. There is one book, the two divide the world between them, and
neither can change a rule without the other. [Football](/sports/football/) and
[cricket](/sports/cricket/) also split the writing of the laws from the
running of the sport. Golf's version is a partnership of equals with no
referee between them, which makes the Rules stable and slow to change.

## A number for every player

Courses differ enormously, and so do players. A raw score says little unless
both are known. Golf could not make its courses alike, so it measured how hard
each one was and adjusted the scores. This is the
<Term id="handicap">handicap</Term>, [formalised from
1911](#golf-handicap-system). With it, a beginner and an expert can play the
same competition on level terms.

## The Games, twice and then not for a century

Golf was played at the Olympic Games in 1900 and 1904. In 1900 Margaret
Abbott won the women's event at Compiègne and became the first American woman
to win an Olympic event. After 1904 golf was absent for 112 years. It returned
in 2016, with 72 holes of <Term id="stroke-play">stroke play</Term> for men
and for women.
