# Origin and invention

Golf's first surviving written rules date from Leith in 1744 — thirteen
articles, drafted for a competition, and short enough to fit on a page. The
modern book runs to a small volume, and the growth between the two is the
history of a sport discovering how many situations a ball can end up in.

The founding principle was there in 1744 and has not moved: **play the ball as
it lies**. Everything difficult in the rulebook follows from refusing to move
the ball to somewhere fair.

The R&A at St Andrews and the USGA emerged as the two custodians, and they
still jointly own the Rules — one book, two bodies, dividing the world between
them. That is a third variant of the ownership split this site keeps finding:
football has IFAB and FIFA, cricket has MCC and the ICC, golf has two equal
partners who must agree.

The handicap is golf's real invention and it arrived later, formalised from
1911. It solved a problem the sport had because of what it is: courses differ
enormously, players differ enormously, and a raw score carries almost no
information without both contexts. Rather than standardise the courses — which
was impossible — golf built a system to measure their difficulty and adjust
scores accordingly.

Golf was on the Olympic programme in 1900 and 1904 and then absent for 112
years, the longest absence of any returning sport.
