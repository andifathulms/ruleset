# Where it is played

Golf's map is drawn by land and money, and it needs an unusual amount of both.

The United States, Great Britain and Ireland, Japan, South Korea, Australia,
South Africa, Sweden, Spain and increasingly China produce the elite. What they
have is courses, and courses are the constraint: eighteen holes occupies
between fifty and eighty hectares, requires continuous maintenance, and in many
climates requires a great deal of water.

That last point is now a live political issue rather than a background fact.
Golf's land and water use is criticised in exactly the regions where the sport
has expanded most aggressively, and it is part of the argument against
lengthening courses to keep pace with distance — which is one of the reasons
the governing bodies chose to shorten the ball instead.

South Korea is the case worth recording. Its women's programme is the most
successful national pipeline in the sport, built on a dense network of
practice facilities and a domestic tour, and it produces players in numbers
disproportionate to the country's course inventory.

Public access varies enormously and shapes participation more than climate
does. Scotland's municipal links and Japan's driving ranges produce golfers;
private-club structures elsewhere produce fewer.

The handicap system is what makes any of this a single sport rather than a
collection of local ones. Since 2020 a handicap issued anywhere means the same
thing everywhere, which is the only global comparability infrastructure on this
site.
