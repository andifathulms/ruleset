# Land, water and a place to practise

Eighteen holes take up between fifty and eighty hectares. The land has to be
maintained all the time, and in many climates it needs a great deal of water.
Golf is played where there is land and money for that.

The United States, Great Britain and Ireland, Japan, South Korea, Australia,
South Africa, Sweden and Spain produce most of the best players, and China
increasingly does.

Land and water are now a political question. Golf is criticised for its use of
both in the same regions where it has grown fastest. That is part of the case
against making courses longer, and one reason the governing bodies chose to
shorten the ball.

## South Korea

South Korea's women's programme is the most successful national system in
golf. It is built on a dense network of practice facilities and a domestic
tour, and it produces far more top players than the country's number of
courses would suggest.

## Who gets in

Public access shapes who plays more than climate does. Scotland has municipal
links courses. Japan has driving ranges, some stacked three storeys high and
some squeezed into a residential street. Both produce golfers. Countries where
golf sits inside private clubs produce fewer.

## One handicap everywhere

Six regional handicap systems were [merged into one World Handicap System in
2020](#golf-2020-world-handicap-system). A
<Term id="handicap">handicap</Term> given in any country is now worked out the
same way, with the weather on the day taken into account. Professionals do not
use it. They play off <Term id="scratch">scratch</Term>, and their scores on
different courses cannot be compared. That is why golf's records count titles
and not strokes.
