# Controversies that forced rule changes

**The pad, and the 1980 Code.** Batters had worked out that the safest response
to a ball outside off stump was to push a pad at it and take no risk with the
bat. It was legal, it was effective, and it made long passages of cricket
uneventful. The 1980 Code extended leg before wicket to cover a ball pitching
outside off where the batter made no genuine attempt to play. A rule was
rewritten because the optimal tactic had become to do nothing.

**Melbourne, January 1971.** A Test match washed out, a crowd already in the
ground, and an improvised one-day exhibition on what would have been the final
day. It drew forty-six thousand people. The one-day international existed by
accident before it existed by design, and cricket has kept two record books
ever since.

**Falling attendances, 2003.** Twenty20 was designed around a working evening,
and it is the most commercially successful rule change on this site. It also
gave the sport a third set of statistics, and this is the case this site finds
most instructive: cricket knew from the start that the numbers would not be
comparable, and simply never merged them.

**Lord's, 14 July 2019.** The World Cup final was tied. The Super Over was
tied. England were awarded the title on the number of boundaries they had hit
across the match — a tiebreak with no relationship to the contest, applied to
decide a world championship. The reaction was close to universal, and the ICC
removed the rule within months. It is the only rule on this site used exactly
once, at the highest possible stakes, and then deleted.

**Umpire's call.** Still argued. The review system's tolerance means the same
delivery can be out or not out depending on what the on-field umpire said
first, which defenders call an honest acknowledgement of measurement error and
critics call incoherent.
