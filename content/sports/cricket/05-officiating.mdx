# Who makes the calls

A cricket match has two umpires on the field, a third umpire who can watch
replays, and a match referee.

<Photo id="cricket-umpire-out" />

## The review

For years television showed viewers ball-tracking pictures that the players
could not use. A few plainly wrong decisions in big matches made that hard to
defend. [The Decision Review System came in from
2009](#icc-2009-decision-review-system). A team can ask the third umpire to
check a decision, using ball tracking, edge detection and slow-motion replay.
The questions are about dismissals: was the batter out or not? Each team has
a limited number of failed reviews in an <Term id="innings">innings</Term>.

## Umpire's call

Cricket made one choice of its own. When ball tracking shows a result that is
very close, the decision made on the field stands. This is
<Term id="umpires-call">umpire's call</Term>. The same ball can be out or not
out, depending on what the umpire said first.

The sport says this is deliberate. Ball tracking does not see where the ball
went after it hit the batter. It works out where the ball would have gone,
and that answer has a margin of error. Inside the margin, cricket prefers the
human. [Tennis](/sports/tennis/) went the other way and replaced its line
judges with the machine.

People still argue about it. Supporters say the rule is honest about what the
machine can know. Critics say a sport should not give opposite verdicts on
the same evidence.

Edge detection works in the same spirit. The tools that show whether the ball
touched the bat give evidence, and the third umpire weighs it and decides.
