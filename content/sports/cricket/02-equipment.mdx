# Equipment evolution

Cricket's equipment history is a bat that grew for two hundred years inside a
specification that only measured two of its dimensions.

The Laws had constrained the bat's **length** and **width** since the
eighteenth century — the width limit dates from an incident in 1771 in which a
player arrived with a bat as wide as the wicket — and said nothing about how
thick it could be. For most of that time this did not matter, because a thicker
bat was a heavier bat and nobody wanted one.

Then manufacturers learned to press lighter timber. By the 2010s bats had edges
around twice the thickness of a generation before at the same weight, and the
consequence was measurable: mishit shots were carrying for six. The balance
between bat and ball had moved without any rule changing, because the rule had
never anticipated the variable that changed.

The 2017 Code added limits on edge thickness and depth. It is the first time in
the sport's history that the shape of a bat was constrained in more than two
dimensions.

The ball has barely moved: weight and circumference, specified for a century
and a half. Protective equipment is the real safety story — helmets from the
late 1970s, and after Phillip Hughes's death in 2014, neck protectors and
revised helmet standards.

The pattern is the same one this site keeps finding. A loosely written
specification tolerates gradual change until something visible happens, and
then the specification has to grow a new dimension.
