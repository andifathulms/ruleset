# What is still argued about

## Children in the ring

Children fight for money in Thailand, inside a gambling economy. Deaths and
brain injuries have been documented. There have been attempts in Thai law to
raise the minimum age and to limit the techniques young fighters may use.
They have met resistance from the stadium industry and from families who
depend on the income. Child welfare advocates, fighters' families and the
industry still disagree, and it is the most serious open question in the
sport.

## Betting at ringside

Betting is part of professional muaythai in Thailand. The odds move during a
bout, loudly enough to hear, and they are part of the atmosphere. Betting of
this kind creates the conditions for fixed fights. An internationally
recognised federation cannot ignore that, and it cannot govern the stadiums
either.

## Two versions

The recognised <Term id="amateur-code">amateur code</Term> is padded and
limits elbows. The professional sport in Thailand does neither, and it has
the best fighters. Each version scores a bout in its own way, and neither has
replaced the other. Whether the amateur version can ever be the top of the
sport is not settled.

The Olympic question follows from this. The federation has been recognised
since 2021 and has no place on the programme. The elbows and the
<Term id="clinch">clinch</Term> are what make muaythai distinctive, and they
are what the amateur code holds back.

## Smaller arguments

**Headguards.** [Boxing](/sports/boxing/) removed them for elite men in 2013.
Amateur muaythai kept them. In a sport with legal elbows to the head, more
depends on the answer.

**The name.** Cambodia, Laos and Myanmar contest closely related forms. What
to call the shared tradition has caused real diplomatic friction, including
at regional multi-sport games.
