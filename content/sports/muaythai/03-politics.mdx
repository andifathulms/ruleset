# Governing body politics

IFMA governs muaythai everywhere except where it matters most.

The federation was formed in 1993, built a genuine international structure,
obtained provisional IOC recognition in 2016 and full recognition in 2021, and
holds a World Games place. By the standards of the sports on this site, that is
a successful three decades.

What it does not govern is the Thai professional stadium system, which is where
the best fighters in the world compete, under different rules, with different
equipment and a different way of scoring a round. Thailand's professional
muaythai is a domestic industry with its own institutions, its own economics
and a legal ringside gambling structure that shapes how bouts are watched and
judged. It is not a rogue operation outside a federation's reach; it is the
sport's homeland doing what it has done for a century.

This site records the same structure in sumo — an internationally recognised
federation running weight-classed, gender-inclusive competition while the
sport's summit remains a national professional institution that enters none of
it. In both cases the international body is not withholding anything or being
defied; the two simply exist in parallel.

Thailand's position inside IFMA is correspondingly central: Thai federations
made retention of the wai kru ritual a condition of internationalisation, and
the sport's name, spelled as one word by the federation, is itself a statement
about ownership.

The Olympic case is unresolved. Recognition has been held since 2021 and no
programme place has followed, and the sport's most obvious selling points —
the elbows, the clinch — are the parts the amateur code exists to restrain.
