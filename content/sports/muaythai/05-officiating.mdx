# Officiating

A referee in the ring, judges at ringside, and a scoring philosophy that
depends on which country you are in.

**The clinch** is the officiating decision that most defines the sport. In
boxing the referee separates fighters who close; in muaythai the referee leaves
them to work — knees, elbows, sweeps, neck control — and intervenes only when
neither is making progress. Judging when a clinch has become unproductive is
continuous, consequential, and entirely a matter of feel.

**Scoring** diverges. International amateur judges use a ten-point must system,
round by round. Thai professional judges assess the bout as a whole with the
later rounds weighted most heavily, rewarding dominance, balance and visible
effect rather than counting strikes — which is why a fighter can concede the
opening rounds deliberately and still win.

That difference is not a technicality. It means the same performance, judged in
Bangkok and at a world championship, can produce different winners, and a
fighter has to know which sport they are being scored in.

**Legality.** With eight striking surfaces legal and throws prohibited, the
referee is drawing a line muaythai draws in an unusual place: a sweep from the
clinch is legal, a hip throw is not. The distinction is between off-balancing
and lifting, judged live.

**The ritual.** The wai kru is required and is performed before the bout under
the officials' supervision — one of very few things any referee on this site
oversees that is not part of the contest.

There is no instrumentation anywhere in the sport.
