# From the fair to the stadium

Muaythai began as a way of fighting for Siamese soldiers. It became a contest
at festivals, then a stadium business, and only much later a sport with an
international federation.

Its ancestor is Siamese close-quarters fighting. It was practised as military
training and contested at temple fairs and festivals, with the hands bound in
rope. There were no weight classes, no rounds and no gloves. The techniques
were the ones the sport still allows, the
<Term id="eight-limbs">eight limbs</Term>: fists, elbows, knees and shins.
That older form is now called muay boran.

## Into the ring

The move to a ring happened in the early twentieth century. Gloves replaced
the rope bindings. Timed <Term id="round">rounds</Term> and weight categories
came from Western [boxing](/sports/boxing/).

<Photos ids="muaythai-rope-hands-1915 muaythai-bangkok-1920s" />

Bangkok's stadiums then made the contest a professional industry. Rajadamnern
opened in the 1940s and Lumpinee in the 1950s. Between them they set the
conventions of the professional sport: five rounds, a bout judged as a whole
with the later rounds counting most, and betting at ringside that shapes how
a fight is watched and judged.

## Beyond Thailand

The International Federation of Muaythai Associations was formed in 1993 to
take the sport abroad. [It wrote the eight limbs and the fighting clinch into
its rulebook](#muaythai-eight-limbs). It also wrote an
<Term id="amateur-code">amateur code</Term> [with protective equipment and
limits on elbows](#muaythai-amateur-protective-code), so that the sport could
meet the standards of international multi-sport games.

The International Olympic Committee gave the federation provisional
recognition in 2016 and [full recognition in
2021](#muaythai-2021-ioc-recognition). Muaythai is contested at the World
Games. It has never been on an Olympic programme.
