# Origin and invention

Muaythai is a battlefield method that became a festival contest, then a stadium
business, then an internationally governed sport — in that order, over a very
long time.

Its ancestor is Siamese close-quarters fighting, practised as military training
and contested at temple fairs and festivals with the hands bound in rope. That
form — *muay boran* in retrospect — had no weight classes, no rounds and no
gloves, and the techniques it used are the ones the modern sport still permits:
fists, elbows, knees and shins.

The transition to a ring sport happened through the early twentieth century.
Rope bindings gave way to boxing gloves, timed rounds and weight categories
arrived from Western boxing, and Bangkok's stadiums turned the contest into a
professional industry. Rajadamnern opened in the 1940s and Lumpinee in the
1950s, and between them they established the conventions that still define
professional muaythai: five rounds, a bout assessed as a whole with the later
rounds weighted, and a ringside gambling economy that is inseparable from how
the sport is watched and judged.

International governance is much more recent. The International Federation of
Muaythai Associations was formed in 1993 to take the sport beyond Thailand, and
it did something significant in the process: it wrote an amateur code with
protective equipment and restricted elbows, so that the sport could meet the
standards the international multi-sport system requires.

IFMA received provisional IOC recognition in 2016 and full recognition in 2021.
Muaythai is contested at the World Games and has never been on an Olympic
programme — and the version that holds the recognition is not the version
fought in Bangkok.
