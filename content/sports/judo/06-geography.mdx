# Where it is played

Judo is the most widely practised martial art in the world and one of the most
evenly distributed sports on this site. Its medal table is genuinely global in
a way that badminton's and table tennis's are not: Japan, France, Georgia,
Brazil, Russia, South Korea, Cuba, the Netherlands, Mongolia and Kosovo have
all produced Olympic champions, and several of those are small countries where
judo is a national sport.

The evenness has a cause. Judo needs a mat, a jacket and a coach, and nothing
else — no facility on the scale gymnastics needs, no pool, no bicycle, no
grass. It travelled through the twentieth century on the back of Japanese
emigration and military instruction, and it embedded in school and club systems
rather than in expensive institutions.

France is the case worth recording. It has more registered judoka than Japan,
by a wide margin, built on a club structure that treats judo as a children's
activity first and a combat sport second. That is a participation strategy
rather than an elite one, and it has nonetheless produced sustained elite
success.

The rule consequence is the one running through this whole page. Because the
sport is genuinely global and its strongest competitors increasingly come from
outside its founding tradition, the question of what judo should look like has
real stakes. The leg grab ban fell hardest on nations with wrestling cultures
and lightest on those with classical judo lineages, and whether that was the
purpose or the side effect is exactly what the sources disagree about.
