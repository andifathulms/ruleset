# Where it is played

Judo's Olympic champions come from all over the world. Japan, France, Georgia,
Brazil, Russia, South Korea, Cuba, the Netherlands, Mongolia and Kosovo have
all produced one. Compare the medal tables of [badminton](/sports/badminton/)
or [table tennis](/sports/table-tennis/), where a few nations win almost
everything.

Several of those countries are small, and judo is a national sport there.
Majlinda Kelmendi won Kosovo's first Olympic gold medal in any sport, in judo,
in 2016. A statue of her stands in the town of Pejë.

Judo spread this evenly because it needs very little: a mat, a jacket and a
coach. It travelled through the twentieth century with Japanese emigrants and
with military instruction, and it settled in schools and clubs.

## The first champion from outside Japan

In 1961 Anton Geesink of the Netherlands won the world title. He was the first
world champion from outside Japan. The question of who owns judo starts there.

## France

France has far more registered <Term id="judoka">judoka</Term> than Japan.
French clubs treat judo as an activity for children first and a combat sport
second. That is a plan for getting many people to take part, and it has still
produced champions year after year.

## What the spread means for the rules

The strongest competitors now often come from outside the tradition that
founded the sport. So the question of what judo should look like has real
consequences. The 2013 ban on leg grabs fell hardest on nations with a
wrestling culture and lightest on those with a classical judo lineage. The
sources disagree on whether that was the purpose or a side effect.
