# The referee who can be overruled

A judo contest has one referee on the mat, and that referee does not have the
last word.

Video officials sit at the side of the mat with a replay. They can change the
referee's score, and they often do. No one has to appeal or challenge. The
officials act on their own, within seconds. The call made on the mat is a
first answer, and the rules intend it that way.

<Photos ids="judo-referee-2018 judo-referee-2015" />

Other sports use video differently. In [football](/sports/football/) the video
officials advise a referee who still makes the decision. In
[gymnastics](/sports/gymnastics/) two panels each judge their own part of a
routine. Judo has a second layer that outranks the first.

The sport has mostly accepted this, and the reason is in the scoring. A score
depends on whether the throw landed the opponent largely on the back, with
control and with force. That is very hard to see correctly once, from mat
level, at full speed. It is much easier to see on a second look from a camera.
The question sounds like an opinion, but it is a question about where a body
was at one moment, and a camera can answer it.

## What a camera cannot settle

Penalties are different. A <Term id="shido">shido</Term> for passivity, for a
false attack or for defensive gripping is a judgement about how a contestant
has behaved over a stretch of time. A replay of one moment does not help. These
remain the most disputed calls in judo. Since 2018 a shido cannot win a
contest for the opponent. It can only lose one for the contestant who receives
it, so a little less depends on each call.
