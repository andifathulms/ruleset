# Officiating and technology

Judo has the most interesting officiating arrangement on this site, because the
official on the mat is not the final authority.

One referee runs the contest. Video review officials sit at the side with
access to replay, and they can and routinely do correct the mat referee's
score — not on appeal, not on challenge, but on their own initiative and within
seconds. The referee's call is provisional by design.

No other sport here works this way. Football's VAR advises a referee who
remains the decision-maker. Gymnastics splits its judgement across two panels
that each own their part. Judo simply has a second layer that outranks the
first, and the sport has largely accepted it, because what a score depends on —
whether a throw landed largely on the back, with control, with force — is
almost impossible to see correctly once from mat level and quite easy to see
correctly twice from a camera.

That is the underlying reason the technology fits here and fits badly
elsewhere. Judo's central judgement is about the geometry of a body at a moment
in time, which is a question of fact even though it sounds like an opinion.
Football's handball law asks about intent and football's cameras cannot help.

What review cannot settle is the penalty. Shido for passivity, for a false
attack, for defensive gripping — these are judgements about a pattern of
behaviour over time rather than about a moment, and they remain the most
disputed calls in the sport. Since 2018 they can no longer win a contest for
the opponent, only lose it for the offender, which reduced how much rests on
them.
