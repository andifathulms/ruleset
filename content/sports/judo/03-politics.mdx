# Governing body politics

Judo's politics is a long negotiation between the country that invented it and
the federation that runs it, and almost every modern rule change is legible as
a move in that negotiation.

Japan holds a position no other nation holds in any sport on this site: it is
not merely the strongest competitor but the source of the sport's definition of
itself. The Kodokan is a separate institution from the IJF and retains
authority over judo as a practice rather than as a competition. When the two
disagree — over the blue judogi, over scoring, over which techniques belong —
the disagreement is not about who wins but about what the thing is.

The IJF's direction for thirty years has been towards spectacle and legibility:
distinguishable colours, fewer scores, no draws, no referees' decisions,
contests that end with something visible happening. Each step is defensible on
its own and the cumulative direction is unmistakable, and it is the direction
the Kodokan tradition has resisted at every stage.

The leg grab ban is where those two forces produced something unusual. The
federation did not claim safety or fairness. It said judo was starting to look
like wrestling, and legislated on the basis of identity — the only governing
body on this site to give that as a stated reason, and it could only do so
because Kano wrote down what the sport was supposed to be.

Weight categories are the other governance issue, and it is a health one. The
sport is contested at a made weight and the practice of cutting to it remains
inadequately regulated.
