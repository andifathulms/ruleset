# Origin and invention

Judo is the only sport on this site whose founder wrote down what it was for.

Jigoro Kano established the Kodokan in 1882, taking the throwing and grappling
techniques of jujutsu and removing the strikes, the joint attacks the body
cannot survive, and the assumption that the goal was to injure. What he built
was a martial art reorganised as an educational method, with two stated
principles: maximum efficiency with minimum effort, and mutual welfare and
benefit.

That origin explains why judo's rule arguments sound different from every other
sport's here. When the federation banned leg grabs in 2013 and justified it by
saying that judo should look like judo, it was making a claim that only a sport
with a written purpose can make. Football cannot say a tackle is wrong because
it is not football. Judo can, and does.

The system spread through the Japanese education system and then internationally
through demonstration and emigration; the International Judo Federation formed
in 1951. Judo entered the Olympic programme at Tokyo in 1964, was dropped for
1968, and has been permanent since 1972. The women's competition arrived in
1992, twenty-eight years after the men's.

Kano's other invention was the coloured belt, which is now so universal across
martial arts that its origin is invisible. It was a nineteenth-century
administrative idea about how to grade a syllabus.
