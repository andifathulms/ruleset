# One man's school in Tokyo

Judo has a founder and a founding date. Jigoro Kano opened a school called the
Kodokan in 1882, and the sport is still the thing he designed there.

Kano started from jujutsu, the older Japanese fighting systems. He kept the
throws and the grappling. He removed the strikes, the joint attacks the body
cannot survive, and the idea that the goal was to injure. Kano presented what
was left as a method of education, and he wrote down two principles for it:
maximum efficiency with minimum effort, and mutual welfare and benefit.

## Out of Japan

Judo spread first through Japanese schools. It then travelled abroad through
demonstrations and through emigration. The International Judo Federation was
formed in 1951.

Judo joined the Olympic Games at Tokyo in 1964. Anton Geesink, a Dutchman, won
the open class there, in the sport's home city. Judo was left out in 1968 and
has been on the programme at every Games since 1972. Women's judo arrived in
1992, twenty-eight years after the men's.

## Two authorities

The Kodokan still exists, and it is a separate body from the federation. The
federation runs competition. The Kodokan keeps authority over judo as a
practice.

<Photo id="judo-kodokan-now" />

The two have often disagreed. For about thirty years the federation has moved
towards a sport that is easy to watch: two colours of jacket, fewer scores, no
draws, no referees' decisions. The Kodokan tradition has resisted each of
those steps. The arguments are about what judo is, more than about who wins,
and a sport with a written purpose can have that kind of argument.
