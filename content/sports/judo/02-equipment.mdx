# Equipment evolution

Judo has almost no equipment, and the one thing it does have is contested in a
way that only makes sense once you realise the judogi is not clothing.

Both contestants grip the jacket. It is therefore the interface through which
the entire sport happens, and its specification is a performance rule rather
than a uniform rule. The IJF regulates weave, thickness, sleeve length, the gap
at the wrist and the width of the lapel, and enforces those tolerances with a
gauge at weigh-in — because a jacket cut slightly tighter is genuinely harder
to grip, and a competitor wearing one is harder to fight.

The visible change is colour. Until the late 1990s both contestants wore white
and were distinguished by a coloured sash, which on television was two
indistinguishable figures on a pale mat. The blue judogi was adopted for major
competition from 1998 after sustained resistance, particularly from Japan,
where the objection was that white was part of what judo was and changing it
for a camera got the priority backwards.

That argument is worth holding onto, because it is the same argument the sport
had about leg grabs fifteen years later and about yuko twenty years later.
Judo's equipment history is short, but the dispute inside it — whether the
sport exists for its practitioners or for its audience — runs through
everything else on this page.

The mat is the other piece of equipment, and its evolution is a safety story:
progressively better shock absorption, which is what allows throws to be
practised thousands of times.
