# The jacket both fighters hold

Judo has almost no equipment. It has a mat and a uniform, and the uniform is
part of the fight.

Both contestants <Term id="grip">grip</Term> each other's jacket, and every
throw starts from that grip. A jacket cut a little tighter is harder to hold,
and the person wearing it is harder to fight. So the federation fixes the
weave, the thickness, the sleeve length, the gap at the wrist and the width of
the lapel, and checks them with a gauge. The rule reads like a dress code and
works like a rule of play.

## Blue against white

Until the late 1990s both contestants wore white. A coloured sash was the only
difference between them. On television that meant two figures in the same
white clothes, holding each other on a pale mat. Viewers could not tell them
apart, and could not follow the referee's decisions.

[From 1998 one contestant wears blue](#ijf-1997-blue-judogi) at major
competitions. The change took years, and it was opposed inside the sport,
most of all in Japan. The objection was that the white judogi was part of
judo, and that changing it for a camera put the audience ahead of the sport.
The same objection has come back at every later rule change.

## The mat

The <Term id="tatami">tatami</Term> has one job, which is to absorb a body
thrown from standing. Mats have absorbed shock better and better over time.
That is what lets a judoka practise a throw thousands of times.
