# Governing body politics

The IHF's most consequential recent act was an accident, and that is worth
saying plainly.

In 2016 the federation removed the requirement that a substitute for the
goalkeeper wear a distinguishing shirt and act as a goalkeeper. The stated
purpose was to simplify a clumsy substitution procedure. The effect, within two
seasons, was that elite teams began attacking seven against six with an empty
net as a standard tactic.

The federation has spent the decade since deciding whether it approves. The
empty-net attack is exciting, produces long-range goals into an unguarded net,
and increases variance considerably — and it also means a large part of elite
handball is now played without one of its seven positions on court.

Nothing else on this site is quite like it. Most rule changes here were made to
produce an effect, and the arguments are about whether the effect was worth it.
This one produced an effect nobody was aiming at.

The federation's other 2016 changes were more deliberate and more typical of
this site's patterns. The four-pass limit after a passive-play warning converted
a referee's judgement into a count, which is the same instrument basketball's
shot clock and wrestling's passivity clock are. The blue card separated a
sending-off from the disciplinary process that follows it.

Handball's structural politics is European. The sport's professional strength
is concentrated in Germany, France, Scandinavia and the Balkans, its club
competitions are European, and the IHF's global development ambitions run
against a domestic economy that is regional.
