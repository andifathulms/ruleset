# Governing body politics

Shooting's governance has been driven for thirty years by a single question:
how do you televise a sport in which nothing visible happens?

The answer has been a sequence of format changes, each moving further from the
measurement. Finals were added in 1988 to create an ending. Decimal scoring
gave those finals enough resolution to separate competitors. In 2013 finals were
reset to start from zero and made progressively eliminating, so a medal is
decided by two people shooting head to head at the end.

Each step is defensible and the cumulative effect is that the sport's most
precise data — sixty shots of qualification, measured to a tenth of a ring —
now determines only who is admitted to the final. Competitors have said so
consistently and the federation has not disputed the description, only the
priority.

The other permanent political fact is the sport's relationship with firearms
regulation. Shooting's Olympic position has been questioned in the aftermath of
shootings in several countries, and the sport is unusually exposed to arguments
about the object it uses rather than the competition it runs. Its steady drift
towards air events at ten metres indoors is partly a response to that, and
partly a coincidence of what makes a good televised final.

The 2018 restructure — three men's events removed, three mixed-team events
added — was the standard IOC parity requirement inside a fixed quota, and it
cost the sport several events that had been contested for a century.
