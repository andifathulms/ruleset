# Who scores a shot

In international shooting, a machine scores the shot.

For about a century an official examined a paper target with a plug gauge and
decided whether the hole touched a line. It was slow, and disputes were
routine. An <Term id="electronic-target">electronic target</Term> registers
the position of the shot and shows the value. Nobody has to interpret
anything.

<Photo id="shooting-electronic-scoring" />

The officials are left with procedure. A
<Term id="range-officer">range officer</Term> controls the line, gives the
commands and keeps the time. A shot fired before or after the command, or
after the time has run out, is penalised. So is a
<Term id="cross-fire">cross-fire</Term>, a shot that hits another
competitor's target.

Safety comes above everything else. A shooter who handles a gun outside the
set procedure is out of the competition, whatever the score.

Equipment inspection is the largest job. The stiffness of the jacket and the
weight of the trigger are measured with gauges, and a shooter whose kit fails
does not compete.

The electronic target did not show that the officials had been wrong. It
showed that the scale was too coarse. The best shooters were posting the same
whole-ring totals, and the sport had to add a decimal place to see a
difference that had always been there.
