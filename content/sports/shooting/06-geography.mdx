# Where the law allows it

Firearms law draws the map of shooting more than wealth, climate or tradition
do.

China, South Korea, India, the United States, Italy, Germany, Russia, Hungary,
Serbia and Norway have been strong for a long time. Each has laws under which
sport shooting is licensed and organised through clubs, and a network of
ranges that follows from that. In Germany a town's shooting club parades at
its Schützenfest. In Britain the National Rifle Association has had its ranges
at Bisley in Surrey since 1890, with targets half a kilometre away.

## The ten-metre route

A country with strict firearms law can still be strong. The
<Term id="air-events">air events</Term> use low-powered guns, and many
countries treat those differently from firearms proper. India's rise has come
largely in air rifle and air pistol for that reason, and China's programme is
concentrated there too.

## Shotguns

The shotgun events have an older map. It follows the hunting and clay shooting
traditions of Italy, Britain, the United States and the Nordic countries.

## A fragile position

Where a shooter lives can decide whether they may train at all. A swimmer
without a pool is unlucky. A shooter in a country with strict licensing may
be unable to practise, whatever their means.

The same link makes the sport's public standing fragile. Its place at the
Olympic Games has been questioned after mass shootings in several countries.
The drift towards low-powered indoor events at ten metres is partly an answer
to that.
