# Officiating and technology

Table tennis is officiated almost entirely by eye, at a speed that makes that
remarkable. The umpire sits level with the net and calls the score, the edge
and the net; an assistant umpire sits opposite, and the assistant's principal
job is the service.

That a second official exists purely to watch one action tells you how much
weight the service law carries. Judging it means deciding whether the ball rose
16 centimetres, whether the palm was flat and stationary, and whether the ball
was visible to the receiver — three judgements about a movement lasting under a
second, made from the side.

**Video review** arrived late and remains limited. It is used at some top-tier
events for edge balls and for whether the ball touched the table, both of which
are questions of fact and both of which a camera answers well.

What no camera has settled is the service, and the reason is now familiar from
the rest of this site: the service law asks for a judgement, not a measurement.
"Visible to the receiver" is a claim about someone else's line of sight. Table
tennis has the same structural problem badminton had before 2018, and it has
not taken badminton's way out of replacing the judgement with a fixed number,
because there is no obvious number to replace it with.

The other thing officials watch is the racket itself. Coverings must be from
the approved list, correctly coloured and within thickness limits, and rackets
are inspected before matches — an equipment check that exists because two
separate laws in this sport are about what the opponent can see.
