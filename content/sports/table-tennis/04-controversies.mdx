# Hidden bats, hidden serves and a ball for television

## The bat nobody could read

Through the 1970s and the early 1980s, some players glued two very different
<Term id="rubber">rubbers</Term> to one bat. One side gave heavy
<Term id="spin">spin</Term>. The other side, with long pimples or an
anti-spin surface, killed it. Both sides were the same colour. The player
<Term id="twiddling">twiddled</Term> the bat during the rally, and the
opponent could not tell which side had hit the ball.

None of this was against the laws. It won points with information the
opponent had no way to get. [From 1986 the two sides of a bat must be
different colours](#ittf-1986-two-colour-racket), and the colours were
settled as one red and one black.

## The serve nobody could see

Sixteen years later the same problem appeared in the
<Term id="service">service</Term>. Top servers had learned to hide the moment
the bat met the ball behind the shoulder or the
<Term id="free-hand">free</Term> arm. The receiver could not see how much
spin was on the ball or which way it turned, and had to guess. A hidden serve
was the most effective way to win a point outright.

[Since 2002 the ball must be visible to the receiver for the whole
service](#ittf-2002-service-visibility), and the server may not screen it
with the body or with clothing.

The skill at the centre of the game is reading spin. Twice the federation
wrote a law because that skill was being beaten by hiding and not by better
play.

## Too fast to watch

The larger ball did not come from an incident. It came from a broadcast
problem. At the top level the ball moved faster than the television cameras
of the time could show clearly, and faster than a spectator could follow.
The federation said its aim was to make the sport watchable. [The 40
millimetre ball was adopted straight after the Olympic Games of 2000 in
Sydney](#ittf-2000-forty-millimetre-ball).

[A year later games were cut from 21 points to
11](#ittf-2001-eleven-point-scoring), and the service began to change hands
every two points and no longer every five. Matches under the old scoring were
long and their length was hard to predict, which made a television slot
impossible to promise. Match statistics from before September 2001 are
counted in a different unit and cannot be compared with those after it.
