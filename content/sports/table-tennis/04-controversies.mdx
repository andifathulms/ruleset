# Controversies that forced rule changes

**The unreadable bat.** Through the 1970s and early 1980s players mounted
radically different rubbers on the two sides of a bat in matching colours,
twiddled it during rallies, and left opponents unable to tell which surface had
struck the ball. Nothing about it was against the laws. It was a legal way of
winning points on information the opponent could not obtain, and in 1986 the
federation required the two sides to be different colours. The rule is
unusually blunt about what it is for.

**The hidden serve.** The same problem, sixteen years later, in a different
place. Servers had developed actions that screened the moment of contact behind
the free arm or shoulder, so the receiver could not see how much spin was being
applied or in which direction. The 2002 law requires the ball to be visible
throughout. Two laws, sixteen years apart, both written because the sport's
central skill — reading spin — could be defeated by hiding rather than by
outplaying.

**Sydney 2000, and the camera.** The trigger for the larger ball was not an
incident but a broadcast. At the top level the ball was travelling faster than
period television could usefully show and faster than a crowd could follow, and
a sport that cannot be watched cannot be sold. The federation enlarged the ball
within months of the Games.

**Solvent fumes.** Speed glue was not a controversy in the sense of a single
event; it was an accumulating one. A performance gain that required players to
inhale volatile solvents before every match, and that trickled down to junior
and school level, became indefensible. The ban took effect in 2008.
