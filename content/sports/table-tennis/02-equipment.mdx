# Equipment evolution

Table tennis has changed its equipment more aggressively than any other sport
on this site, and unlike athletics it has done so mostly on purpose.

**The rubber.** Pimpled rubber gave the sport spin. Sponge backing, arriving in
the 1950s, multiplied it — a Japanese player won the 1952 world championship
with a thick sponge bat against opponents using plain pimples, and the
federation has been specifying covering thickness ever since. Long pimples and
anti-spin surfaces then created the opposite problem: rubbers that reversed or
killed the incoming spin, unreadable when mounted in the same colour as their
opposite. The 1986 two-colour law exists because of them.

**Speed glue.** From the 1970s, players discovered that re-gluing a rubber with
volatile solvents swelled the sponge and added speed and spin for a few hours.
It became universal and had to be redone constantly, which meant elite players
were inhaling solvent daily, often in unventilated rooms, often as children.
Banned with effect from 2008.

**The ball, twice.** From 38 mm to 40 mm in 2000, deliberately, to slow the
game for television. From celluloid to plastic in 2014, not deliberately —
celluloid is flammable, was falling out of manufacture and was becoming
difficult to ship. The second change reduced spin again, which cut against what
the first change had been trying to achieve.

Set against football, whose ball specification has not needed to move in
decades, table tennis shows what happens when a governing body specifies its
implement tightly: it has to keep amending it, and each amendment moves the
sport.
