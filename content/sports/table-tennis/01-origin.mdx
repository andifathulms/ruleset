# Origin and invention

Table tennis is the only sport on this site that began as an imitation of
another one. It appeared in late-Victorian England as an after-dinner
parlour game — books for a net, a cigar box lid for a bat, a champagne cork
or a rubber ball — and its whole point was that it was lawn tennis indoors,
on a table, in winter.

The name it is still commonly known by came from a manufacturer. Ping-Pong was
a trademark registered around 1901 for one company's version of the equipment,
and the sport's governing bodies have used "table tennis" ever since precisely
to avoid it. That a sport's popular name is somebody's brand is not unique, but
it is unusually clean here.

Two things made it a sport rather than a diversion. The first was the celluloid
ball, adopted around 1900, which bounced consistently enough to allow rallies.
The second was pimpled rubber on the bat, which allowed spin — and spin is what
separates table tennis from the parlour game it descends from. Everything
interesting in its rulebook follows from that one material.

The International Table Tennis Federation was founded in 1926 and held its
first world championships the same year. The sport entered the Olympic
programme in 1988.
