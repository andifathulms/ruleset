# Origin and invention

Korfball is the only sport on this site that began with a social requirement
and worked backwards to a game.

In 1902 Nico Broekhuysen, an Amsterdam schoolteacher, attended a summer
teaching course at Nääs in Sweden and encountered a game called ringboll, in
which players threw a ball at a ring on a pole. He wanted something his
co-educational class could play together, and the version he wrote when he
came home — *korfbal*, from the Dutch for basket — was built so that boys and
girls on the same court would both matter.

Almost every law follows from that. Mixed teams are required. A player may only
defend an opponent of the same sex. Physical contact is banned outright.
Dribbling does not exist, so nobody can simply be stronger and go past. The
defended-shot rule means a defender wins by standing in the right place rather
than by out-reaching someone.

None of these is a safety concession or a modern accommodation; they are the
1902 code. Korfball reached its position on mixed-sex play roughly a century
before the sports around it began adding mixed events, and it did so not as a
statement but because a schoolteacher needed a game that worked for a class of
thirty children of both sexes.

It spread quickly through the Netherlands and into Belgium, and the
International Korfball Federation was formed in 1933 to settle a common
rulebook between them. It was played as a demonstration sport at the Antwerp
Games in 1920 and the Amsterdam Games in 1928 — both in the corner of Europe
where it was actually played — and it has never been on the Olympic programme
since.
