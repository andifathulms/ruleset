# The stick and its pocket

Everything in lacrosse is done with the stick. The
<Term id="crosse">crosse</Term> was once made of wood, with a netted hoop of
leather and gut at the end. A modern one has a composite head and synthetic
mesh.

<Photos ids="lacrosse-original-stick lacrosse-plastic-head" />

The rules set the width of the head, the height of its side walls and the
depth of the <Term id="pocket">pocket</Term>. The pocket rule matters most. A
deeper pocket holds the ball more firmly, and a defender then finds it much
harder to knock the ball out with a legal <Term id="check">check</Term>. If
pockets were free to deepen, the game would tip toward the team that has the
ball. So the depth is measured at inspection, and sticks do fail.

## Two games, two sets of protection

The men's game allows <Term id="body-check">body checking</Term>, and players
must wear a helmet, gloves and shoulder pads. The women's game does not allow
body checking. For most of its history it required only eyewear. The reasoning
was that more protection invites the contact it is meant to protect against.

Head injuries in the women's game come mostly from sticks and balls. [From
2017 the rules allow headgear built to a set
standard](#lacrosse-womens-headgear), and leave the choice to the player.
[Boxing](/sports/boxing/) had the same argument about headguards in 2013 and
went the other way: it removed protection that had been compulsory. Both
sports said they acted for the safety of the players.

## Sixes

<Term id="sixes">Sixes</Term> does not allow the
<Term id="long-stick">long sticks</Term> that defenders carry in field
lacrosse. That lowers the cost of a team and removes one specialist job.
