# A new game for the Olympics, and a team without a state

## Sixes

Lacrosse was an Olympic medal sport in 1904 and 1908. At the London Games of
1908 only two teams entered, Canada and Great Britain. After that it appeared
only as a demonstration.

<Photo id="lacrosse-london-1908" />

The way back was blocked by the size of the game. <Term
id="field-lacrosse">Field lacrosse</Term> is ten a side on a large pitch, with
specialist positions and costly equipment. It would need more athlete places
and a bigger venue than the Olympic programme was going to give.

So in 2021 the federation, World Lacrosse, [published a new
format](#lacrosse-2021-sixes). <Term id="sixes">Sixes</Term> is six a side on
a smaller field. It has a <Term id="shot-clock">shot clock</Term>, short
quarters, no long sticks and no face-off specialist, and a match lasts about
forty minutes. Two years later lacrosse was added to the programme for the
2028 Games.

[Rugby](/sports/rugby/) took a similar road with sevens, and
[cricket](/sports/cricket/) with Twenty20. Lacrosse did not have a short form
to adopt. It wrote one.

## The passport

The Haudenosaunee Confederacy is a full member of World Lacrosse, and its
team, the Nationals, plays as a nation. The sport accepts that. Governments
often have not. More than once the team could not travel to a competition
because the host country would not accept Haudenosaunee passports.

<Photo id="lacrosse-six-nations-arena" />

The Olympics raised a separate problem. The International Olympic Committee
recognises National Olympic Committees, and the Haudenosaunee are a
confederacy with no state and no such committee. World Lacrosse pressed the
case. In 2025 the IOC agreed arrangements that allow the Nationals to take
part in the 2028 Games.

No rule of play was at stake. The federation judged that a sport whose
inventors could not compete in it had a problem of legitimacy, and it used its
influence on that.
