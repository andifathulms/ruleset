# Where it is played

Lacrosse has one of the narrowest competitive maps on this site and one of the
most specific reasons for it.

The United States and Canada dominate, and the Haudenosaunee Nationals are
consistently among the top three teams in the world — a confederacy of
territories with a population smaller than most cities, competing at the top
of an international sport it invented.

Beyond North America: England, Australia, Japan, and a growing group including
Israel, Ireland and several European nations.

The barrier has been institutional rather than physical. Field lacrosse needs
ten players a side with specialist positions and expensive protective
equipment, which requires a club system to develop, which requires a sport that
already exists in the country. That is a circular problem and it kept the map
static for a century.

**Sixes is the deliberate attempt to break it.** Six a side, no specialists, no
long sticks, a smaller field and shorter matches means a national federation
can put a competitive team together without a deep domestic structure. Whether
it works will be visible at the 2028 Games and afterwards.

The Haudenosaunee position remains the sport's defining geographic fact and its
most difficult. The team's ability to travel has depended on other countries
accepting their passports, and Olympic participation required an accommodation
of a kind the movement rarely makes — because the IOC recognises National
Olympic Committees, and the Haudenosaunee are a confederacy rather than a state.
