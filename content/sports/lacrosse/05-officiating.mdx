# Officiating and technology

Lacrosse's officiating problem is contact at speed with a carried ball, and the
sport has largely borrowed its solutions rather than inventing them.

Penalties are served in a box, so an offending team plays short — the ice
hockey model rather than football's free kick. That makes a foul immediately
and visibly costly, and it means officials are managing a running penalty
system rather than only awarding restarts.

The **shot clock**, added internationally in 2019, is the other borrowed
instrument. It converts a judgement about whether a team is genuinely
attacking into a countable interval, exactly as basketball's did in 1954 and
wrestling's passivity clock did in 2018. This site has now recorded three
sports arriving at the same device for the same problem, sixty-four years
apart.

What remains difficult is the check. In the men's game a defender may check an
opponent's stick and body within defined limits, and deciding whether a check
was legal — its target, its force, whether it was from behind — is a fast
judgement about a physical action, which is the same class of problem football
has with the tackle and judo has with technique legality.

The women's game has a different and harder version: no body checking at all,
and a protected sphere around the head, so officials are judging proximity
rather than contact.

There is no significant video review at international level, which puts
lacrosse behind most sports here — a consequence of a small federation and a
sport that has only recently had the broadcast infrastructure that review
requires.
