# Where it came from

Football began as an argument about two things: carrying the ball and hacking
an opponent. In the middle of the nineteenth century English schools and clubs
each played their own code, and the codes disagreed on both questions.

<Photo id="football-shrovetide" />

In the autumn of 1863 a group of clubs met at the Freemasons' Tavern in Great
Queen Street, London, to agree on one set of laws. The first meeting was on
26 October. Ebenezer Cobb Morley proposed forming the Football Association and
kept the minutes. The meetings turned on carrying and hacking. The laws that
came out of them allowed neither, and the clubs that wanted both walked out.
What they took with them became [rugby](/sports/rugby/).

<Photos ids="football-laws-1863 football-freemasons-plaque" />

Football and rugby are two answers to the same argument, and both rule books
still show it.

## Who holds the laws

The Football Association did not keep the laws for long. In 1872 Scotland
played England at Hamilton Crescent in Glasgow, the first international
match. In 1886 the four British associations formed the International Football
Association Board to hold the laws together.

<Photo id="football-international-1872" />

The Board still holds them. FIFA runs the sport's competitions, and a change to
the Laws of the Game is an act of the Board. The four British associations
have one vote each, FIFA has four, and a change needs six. Neither side can
change the laws alone. The Board publishes an edition of the Laws every year,
with a circular that lists the changes for that season.

## Paid players

Football settled the question of paying players early. The Football
Association accepted professionalism in 1885, decades before
[athletics](/sports/athletics/), [tennis](/sports/tennis/) or rugby union faced
the same question. Three years later the Football League played its first
season, and Preston North End won it.
