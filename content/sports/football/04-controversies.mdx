# Controversies that forced rule changes

**Italia '90.** The tournament is the standard trigger cited for the back-pass
rule: sustained time-wasting in which the ball was played back to a goalkeeper
who picked it up, and the lowest scoring rate of any World Cup finals. Two years
later a goalkeeper could no longer handle a deliberate back-pass, and the
goalkeeping position was rewritten around the change. It is the clearest case in
this collection of a rule change altering a position rather than a procedure.

**Newcastle's offside trap.** Sixty-five years earlier the Board had done the
same thing for the same reason. A defensive method had become reliable enough to
suppress scoring across a whole league, and the Board changed Law 11 from three
defenders to two. Both changes are legislation against a way of playing that was
working too well.

**Golden goal, and its withdrawal.** Introduced in 1993 to make extra time more
attacking; abolished in 2004 on the reading that it had made extra time more
cautious, because conceding once ended the match with no chance to reply. A
governing body reversing itself and effectively saying so is rare enough to be
worth recording as its own entry rather than as a footnote to the first.

**Every televised refereeing error since about 1970.** VAR has no single
trigger, and that is the interesting thing about it. What forced it was
cumulative: broadcast technology showed the audience, in slow motion and from
eight angles, decisions the referee had one look at in real time. The rule
changed because the gap between what the crowd knew and what the official knew
had become impossible to defend.
