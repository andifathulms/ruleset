# Officiating and technology

Football resisted officiating technology longer than any comparable sport, and
the argument was never really about accuracy. The Laws give the referee
authority over matters of fact, and the concern was that a review system would
transfer that authority to a screen — which, in a game with continuous play and
no natural stoppages, also means transferring control of the clock.

**Goal-line technology** came first and was uncontroversial, because a goal has
the one completely unambiguous definition in the Laws: the whole ball across the
whole line. There is nothing to interpret, so a machine can answer it.

**VAR**, written into the Laws in 2018, is a different kind of thing. It reviews
four categories — goals, penalty decisions, direct red cards, mistaken identity
— and only for a clear and obvious error or a serious missed incident. That
threshold is doing enormous work, because most of what VAR is asked to review is
not a fact but a judgement: whether contact was careless, whether an arm made
the body unnaturally bigger, whether a foul was in the area.

The result is that football has automated its unambiguous decisions and left its
ambiguous ones exactly where they were, while making them visible to everyone in
slow motion. Offside is the awkward middle case: it is a question of fact, so it
can be measured, and measuring it to the centimetre has produced decisions that
are correct and widely felt to be wrong. That is not a technology failure. It is
a rule written in 1925 for a human eye, now being enforced to a precision it was
never drafted for.
