# Who makes the calls

The referee's decision is final on matters of fact. For a long time that
sentence kept technology out of football. The worry was that a review system
would move the referee's authority to a screen. In a game with no natural
stoppages, it would also hand the screen control of the clock.

## The goal line

Goal-line technology came first and caused little argument. A goal has the one
completely clear definition in the Laws: the whole ball over the whole line.
There is nothing to interpret, so a machine can answer it.

## The video assistant

No single incident brought in the <Term id="var">VAR</Term>. Television
cameras had long shown viewers, in slow motion and from many angles, decisions
the referee saw once at full speed. The Board's case was that the gap between
what the referee could see and what the audience could see could no longer be
defended.

<Photo id="football-var-screen" />

After a two-year trial, [video assistant referees entered the Laws in
2018](#ifab-2018-var). A review is allowed in four cases: goals, penalty
decisions, direct <Term id="red-card">red cards</Term> and mistaken identity.
It is allowed only for a clear and obvious error or a serious missed incident,
and the referee still makes the decision.

<Photo id="football-var-review" />

Most of what the VAR is asked about is a judgement. Was the contact careless?
Did the arm make the body unnaturally bigger? A camera cannot settle these the
way it settles a ball on a line. Football has automated its clear decisions
and left its unclear ones where they were, now replayed in slow motion for
everyone.

## Offside to the centimetre

Offside sits between the two. It is a question of fact, so it can be measured.
Measuring it to the centimetre has produced decisions that are correct and
widely felt to be wrong. The law was written in 1925 for an assistant
referee's eye, and it is now enforced to a precision nobody had in mind.
