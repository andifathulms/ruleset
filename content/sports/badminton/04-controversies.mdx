# Controversies that forced rule changes

**London 2012, women's doubles.** Four pairs, from China, South Korea and
Indonesia, were disqualified from the Olympic tournament for not using best
efforts in group matches. They were not throwing matches for money. The
tournament had a group stage followed by a fixed knockout bracket, so a team
that finished second in its group met a weaker opponent than a team that
finished first, and losing deliberately was the rational move. Serving into the
net repeatedly in front of a booing crowd was simply the visible form of an
incentive the rules had created.

This is the cleanest example in this collection of a rule punishing behaviour it
had itself made optimal. The federation's response was to change the structure
rather than only the sanction: from 2014 the knockout bracket is drawn after the
group results are known, so there is no bracket to aim for. The best-efforts
obligation stayed in the code of conduct, but the reason to break it was
removed.

**The service judgement.** For decades the service law was written against the
server's own body, and service faults were consequently among the most disputed
calls in the sport — a player could be faulted at one tournament and not at the
next for an identical action. The 2018 fixed-height law replaced a judgement
about a person with a measurement of a court, which is the same move athletics
makes whenever it can.
