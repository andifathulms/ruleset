# Racket and shuttle

The shuttle decides how badminton is played, and the racket matters much less
than a newcomer expects.

A feathered <Term id="shuttle">shuttle</Term> slows down so sharply in the air
that hitting it harder has a limited effect on how far it travels. Rackets
went from wood to steel to aluminium to graphite composite over the second
half of the twentieth century, and the laws never had to rein them in. Lighter
rackets made players quicker to swing and quicker to recover. They did not
make the court too small, which is the problem the javelin gave
[athletics](/sports/athletics/) in the 1980s.

<Photos ids="badminton-feather badminton-plastic" />

Top competition still uses sixteen goose or duck feathers set in a cork base.
Synthetic shuttles have existed for decades. They are cheaper, last longer and
are more consistent, and they are legal in general play. But the two
kinds fly differently, and the sport has chosen to keep the feel of the
feathered game at the top level.

The laws define the shuttle by what it does. A shuttle of the correct speed is
one that lands in a set place when hit full-force from the back line. The
laws fix its mass and the length of its feathers, and leave the material
open, which is why a synthetic shuttle can be legal at all.
