# Controversies that forced rule changes

**Whether Spirit of the Game survives money.** The sport's central and
permanent controversy. Self-officiation works when the players share a culture
and the stakes are moderate. At a world final, with a professional contract or
a national programme attached, a player is being asked to call a foul against
their own interest in front of a crowd. The introduction of observers in elite
competition is the rule change that came out of it, and the argument about
whether observers are referees has not ended.

**Referees in professional leagues.** North American professional ultimate
adopted full referees, which resolved the spectacle problem and removed the
sport's defining feature. It is the clearest case on this site of a sport
splitting over an officiating question rather than over a competitive one.

**The stall count.** Ten seconds counted aloud by the defender, which means the
clock limiting a possession is operated by an opponent with an interest in
counting fast. Disputes over a fast count are routine and are resolved,
inevitably, by the two players involved.

**Contact at speed.** A non-contact sport played by athletes sprinting for a
disc in the air produces collisions anyway, particularly on a bid — a diving
attempt. What counts as a dangerous play, and whether the player who was moving
fastest is responsible, has been rewritten repeatedly and is the safety
question the sport actually has.

**Mixed division seniority.** Ultimate runs open, women's and mixed as full
championship divisions rather than treating mixed as a novelty, which almost no
other sport does. Whether mixed should be the flagship division is argued
internally, and the answer affects what the sport looks like if it is ever
proposed for a programme that counts medal events.
