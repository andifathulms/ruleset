# Origin and invention

The disc came first, and the sports were reverse-engineered from a toy.

Walter Frederick Morrison made a plastic flying disc in 1948, sold the design
to Wham-O, and the company marketed it from 1957 as the Frisbee. For a decade
it was a toy with no game attached, which is a genuinely unusual starting point
— every other sport on this site began with an activity and then found its
equipment.

Ultimate was written in 1968 by students at Columbia High School in Maplewood,
New Jersey, principally Joel Silver, Buzzy Hellring and Jonny Hines. The
foundational rules are recognisable today: you may not run with the disc, you
score by completing a pass in the end zone, any incompletion is an immediate
turnover, and there are no referees.

That last one was not a considered position at first. There were no referees
because it was a game between students on a school car park and nobody had
thought to appoint any. What happened next is the interesting part: as the
sport grew and could have appointed officials, it wrote the absence into the
rulebook instead. Spirit of the Game, formalised in the mid-1970s, makes
self-officiation a rule rather than a circumstance.

The founding generation was explicitly reacting against organised competitive
sport as they had experienced it, and the sport carries that inheritance into
its own world championships — where the most decorated teams are still
expected to referee each other honestly in a final.

The World Flying Disc Federation was formed in 1985 to govern ultimate
alongside disc golf, freestyle, guts and the field events, and received IOC
recognition in 2015 without any of its sports being added to the programme.
