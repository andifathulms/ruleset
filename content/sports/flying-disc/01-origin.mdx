# Where it came from

The flying disc was a toy for ten years before anyone made a sport of it.

## The toy

Walter Frederick Morrison made a plastic flying disc in 1948. Around 1950 he
was promoting it in a space helmet, as a flying saucer called the Pluto
Platter. He sold the design to the Wham-O company, which sold it from 1957 as
the Frisbee. There was still no game to play with it.

## A school car park, 1968

Ultimate was written in 1968 by students at Columbia High School in
Maplewood, New Jersey. The main authors were Joel Silver, Buzzy Hellring and
Jonny Hines. [Their rules](#disc-1968-ultimate-codified) are easy to recognise
today. A player may not run with the <Term id="disc">disc</Term>. A team
scores by completing a pass in the <Term id="end-zone">end zone</Term>. Any
pass that is not caught is a <Term id="turnover">turnover</Term>. And there
are no referees.

<Photo id="flying-disc-maplewood-plaque" />

Nobody chose that last rule at first. The game was played between students
on a school car park, and nobody had thought to appoint a referee. The
students were also reacting against organised competitive sport as they had
known it.

## Writing the absence down

The sport grew, and it could have appointed officials. It did the opposite.
In the mid-1970s it wrote
[Spirit of the Game](#disc-spirit-of-the-game) into the rulebook, and playing
without a referee became a rule. The best teams in the world are still
expected to referee each other honestly in a final.

Ultimate then spread from American colleges to Europe, and national bodies
began to write their own versions of the rules.
[The World Flying Disc Federation](#disc-1985-federation) was formed in 1985
to keep one rulebook. It governs ultimate together with disc golf, freestyle,
guts and the field events.
