# Governing body politics

WFDF's politics are about one question: how much of the founding culture can
survive being taken seriously.

The federation was formed in 1985, holds world championships across several
disc sports, joined the international federation system, and received IOC
recognition in 2015. Recognition is the step before candidacy, and ultimate has
been on the World Games programme — the multi-sport event for sports that are
not Olympic — since the early 2000s.

The Olympic argument runs into something no other candidate sport has to
explain. A sport seeking inclusion is expected to be able to resolve its
matches definitively, and ultimate's rulebook says a disputed call that the
players cannot settle results in the disc going back to the thrower. A final
decided that way is difficult to broadcast and difficult to defend.

So the federation has moved carefully. Observers were introduced at elite level
in the 2010s: neutral officials who resolve deadlocks, judge lines and time,
and can penalise misconduct, but who do not call fouls. WFDF's position is that
this supports self-officiation. Its critics inside the sport say an official
empowered to overrule is a referee that has not been named one, and that the
line will not hold.

Some professional leagues, mostly in North America, went the whole way and
appointed referees. By this site's reckoning that is a different sport: once an
official calls fouls, the rule the game was built around has been removed and
everything else is just an invasion game played with a disc.

The other unresolved question is disc golf. It has far more participants than
ultimate worldwide, its own large professional structure, and almost no
presence in the multi-sport system that WFDF has spent forty years working
inside.
