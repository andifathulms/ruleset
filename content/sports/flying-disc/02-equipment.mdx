# One disc and some cones

Ultimate needs one <Term id="disc">disc</Term> of 175 grams, about 27
centimetres across. That size and weight have been stable for decades.

## How a disc flies

A disc is held up by lift and kept steady by its spin. It goes where it is
tilted more than where it is aimed. Released with the outer edge down, it
curves one way. With the edge up, it curves the other way. Released flat, it
flies straight until the spin runs out, and then it falls away to one side.

So the throws are named for their grips and for the shapes they draw in the
air: <Term id="backhand">backhand</Term>, <Term id="forehand">forehand</Term>
or flick, <Term id="hammer">hammer</Term>, scoober, blade.

<Photo id="flying-disc-flick" />

Wind matters more in ultimate than in most field sports. A wind in the
thrower's face shortens a throw, and it also makes some throws impossible.

## Nothing else

Ultimate has no goals, no nets, no posts and no protective equipment. The
only choice about footwear is which studded boots to wear. The
<Term id="end-zone">end zone</Term> is just a part of the field, so a full
international field can be marked with cones.

## Disc golf

Disc golf has the most players of all the disc sports, and it treats
equipment very differently. A player carries a bag of discs with different
rims that fly in different ways: drivers, mid-ranges and putters. The target
is a basket hung with chains. The equipment rules look much more like those of
[golf](/sports/golf/), with a list of approved discs and limits on speed and
on the width of the rim.
