# Equipment evolution

One object, standardised, and a set of sports built around its aerodynamics.

The competitive ultimate disc is 175 grams and about 27 centimetres across, a
specification that has been stable for decades. A disc generates lift and is
gyroscopically stabilised by its spin, which means it does not travel where it
is aimed so much as where it is angled: released with the outer edge down it
curves one way, with the edge up it curves the other, and flat it flies
straight until it runs out of spin and falls off.

That is why ultimate's throws are named for their shapes and their grips —
backhand, forehand or flick, hammer, scoober, blade — rather than for a
technique inherited from throwing a ball. It is also why wind is a tactical
condition in ultimate to a degree it is not in most field sports: a headwind
does not merely shorten a throw, it changes which throws exist.

Disc golf, the largest of the disc sports by participation, took the
specialisation much further. A disc golfer carries a bag of discs with
different rim profiles and stability characteristics — drivers, mid-ranges,
putters — and the sport's equipment regulation looks far more like golf's than
like ultimate's, with an approved-disc list and limits on speed and rim width.

Ultimate itself has almost nothing else. No goals, no nets, no posts, no
protective equipment, and cleats as the only footwear question. The end zone is
a region of the field, so a full international pitch is marked with cones.
