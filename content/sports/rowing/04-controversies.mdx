# Controversies that forced rule changes

Rowing has fewer of these than any sport on this site, and the reason is
structural rather than virtuous: crews race in separate lanes with no contact,
so there is very little for a controversy to be about.

**The conditions.** The recurring dispute is lane advantage. A crosswind makes
some lanes faster than others, and championship regattas have been decided by
draws that gave one crew shelter and another the exposed lane. The federation
has responded by re-racing in extreme cases and by scheduling around
conditions, but it has never imposed a limit, because there is no threshold
that would make a headwind race and a tailwind race the same event.

Instead it took the more radical step of declining to call the resulting times
records at all. That is not a response to an incident; it is a position adopted
before the problem could produce one, and it is the only case of its kind here.

**Amateurism.** Rowing's nineteenth-century definition excluded manual
labourers on the stated ground that physical work was an unfair advantage. It
is the most explicit case of amateurism as class exclusion in any sport on this
site, and dismantling it took most of a century.

**The lightweight category.** Introduced in 1996 to widen the sport, reduced in
2017 and removed from the Olympic programme thereafter. Weight-making practices
in lightweight rowing had been criticised for years, and the federation cited
quota arithmetic. Both accounts are recorded.

**Blade shape.** The asymmetric blade of the 1990s was worth measurable speed
and every crew adopted it within two seasons. In a sport with records it would
have been a crisis. Here it was a purchase.
