# Boats, seats and blades

A racing <Term id="shell">shell</Term> today has little in common with the
boats of the first regattas, and almost none of the changes needed a new rule.

The biggest change came in the 1870s. Until then a rower sat on a fixed bench
and pulled with the back and arms. The <Term id="sliding-seat">sliding
seat</Term> rolls on rails while the feet stay fixed, so the legs push on
every stroke. It changed how the stroke is rowed.

<Photos ids="rowing-sliding-seat-1871 rowing-seat-and-stretcher" />

Later, hulls went from wood to composite materials, and the riggers that hold
the oars moved further out from the side of the boat. In the 1990s the shape
of the <Term id="blade">blade</Term> changed. The old symmetrical blade gave
way to an asymmetric one known as the big blade. It was measurably faster, and
every crew was using it within a couple of seasons.

## A boat anyone can buy

In another sport, a faster blade might have been banned.
[Cycling](/sports/cycling/) defined the shape of a bicycle, and
[swimming](/sports/swimming/) banned a suit material. Rowing chose to control
who can get the equipment. Its rule book
[sets a minimum weight for each class of boat](#worldrowing-2017-boat-weight-and-materials)
and says equipment must be on sale to every competitor. A crew may not race a
prototype that nobody else can buy. The rule came when composite hulls had
begun to separate crews with large budgets from crews with small ones.

The rule is easier to live with because rowing keeps no world records. A blade
worth several seconds would have broken a record book. Rowing has only
<Term id="world-best-time">world best times</Term>, which it does not treat as
comparable, so a faster boat breaks nothing.
