# Equipment evolution

Rowing's equipment changed enormously and produced almost no rule changes,
because the sport regulated access rather than technology.

The sliding seat arrived in the 1870s and transformed the stroke by bringing
the legs into it. Hulls went from wood to composite, riggers moved outboard,
and blade shape changed substantially in the 1990s when the symmetrical blade
gave way to the asymmetric "big blade" — a change worth measurable speed that
every crew adopted within a couple of seasons.

None of that provoked a ban or a reset, and the reason is the rule that says
equipment must be **commercially available to all competitors** rather than
unique to one crew. A boat may not be a prototype nobody else can buy.

That is a genuinely different answer from the ones elsewhere on this site.
Cycling defined a bicycle's shape and banned positions. Swimming banned a
material and let the records stand. Athletics wrote a shoe specification.
Rowing let the technology develop and required that everyone be able to buy it.

It works partly because rowing has no records to protect. If the times were
records, a blade shape worth several seconds would have created exactly the
crisis the javelin and the supersuit created. Because they are best times and
the federation says they are not comparable anyway, an equipment improvement
does not sever anything.

The two positions reinforce each other, and the sport arrived at both without
apparently framing it as a single decision.
