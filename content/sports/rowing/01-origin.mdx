# From ferry work to racing

Rowing was a job before it was a sport. On the Thames in London, watermen
rowed passengers across the river for a living, and they raced each other for
pride. In 1715 that became a fixed annual race for a prize, a coat and a
silver badge. Doggett's Coat and Badge is still rowed, and it is one of the
oldest sporting events held anywhere.

<Photos ids="rowing-doggett-chelsea rowing-doggett-winners" />

The famous amateur races came in the next century. Oxford and Cambridge first
raced each other in 1829, and the regatta at Henley began in 1839. The
international federation was founded in 1892 and is the oldest international
sports federation. It was known as FISA for most of its life and renamed
itself World Rowing in 2020.

<Photos ids="rowing-henley-1895 rowing-boat-race-2012" />

Early Olympic regattas were rowed over different distances, so their results
could not be set side by side. In 1912 the federation
[fixed the championship distance at 2000 metres](#worldrowing-2000-metre-standard),
the length of the course crews race today.

## The carpenter at Henley

The gentlemen who ran British rowing in the nineteenth century wrote rules to
keep working men out. They barred professionals, and they also barred manual
labourers. The stated reason was that physical work gave a man an unfair
advantage in a boat. The amateur of those years was a man like Max Schmitt,
an attorney, whom Thomas Eakins painted in his
<Term id="single-scull">single scull</Term> on the river in Philadelphia in
1871.

<Photo id="rowing-max-schmitt" />

In 1928 Bobby Pearce of Australia won the Olympic single sculls. Later that
year he entered the Diamond Sculls at Henley and was ruled ineligible. Pearce
was a carpenter, and the rules excluded anyone who was "by trade or employment
for wages a mechanic, artisan or labourer".

<Photo id="rowing-bobby-pearce" />

Rules of this kind lasted well into the twentieth century before they were
removed.
