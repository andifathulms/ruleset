# Origin and invention

Rowing is a working practice that became a race, and the race is very old. The
Doggett's Coat and Badge, contested by Thames watermen since 1715, is the
oldest continuously held sporting event in the world, and it exists because
ferrying passengers across a river was a job that people were competitive about.

The sport's institutions are correspondingly old. FISA — now World Rowing —
was founded in 1892 and is the oldest international sports federation of any
sport on this site. The Oxford and Cambridge Boat Race dates from 1829 and
Henley from 1839.

Two decisions from that early period still shape everything. The distance was
standardised at 2000 metres in 1912, and it has not moved. And the sport
concluded, at some point in its long institutional life, that the conditions it
cannot control make its times incomparable — which is why it recognises world
best times rather than records.

Rowing's other founding preoccupation was amateurism, and it was the most
aggressive version of it anywhere. Nineteenth-century British rowing excluded
not only professionals but manual labourers, on the explicit ground that
physical work conferred an unfair advantage. That is amateurism as class
exclusion with the reasoning written down, and it took until well into the
twentieth century to remove.
