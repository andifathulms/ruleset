# What the officials measure, and what they do not

Rowing gives its officials very little to decide. Crews race in separate
<Term id="lane">lanes</Term> and may not touch, so an umpire has three things
to watch: the start, the steering, and whether the boats meet the rules.
Umpires follow each race in a launch, as they have since the days of steam.
In most races they do nothing at all.

<Photo id="rowing-umpire-launch-eva" />

The finish is measured closely. A photo-finish camera and electronic timing
separate crews to a hundredth of a second.

<Photo id="rowing-photo-finish-2005" />

The conditions are not measured at all. Nobody records the wind, the current
or the temperature of the water for the result, and no result is cancelled or
adjusted because of them. [Athletics](/sports/athletics/) measures the wind
and refuses a sprint record above 2.0 metres per second. Rowing's view is that
no such limit could make a race into a headwind and a race with a tailwind the
same event.

So the federation changed what its times claim to be. It
[recognises world best times and no world records](#worldrowing-best-times).
The times are logged, published and celebrated, and nobody pretends that two
of them rowed on different days measure the same thing.

## The lane draw

Wind still decides races. A crosswind makes some lanes faster than others, and
championship medals have turned on which crew drew the sheltered lane. In
extreme cases the federation has re-rowed a race, and it schedules racing
around bad weather. It has never set a wind limit.

<Photo id="rowing-lanes-2009" />

The <Term id="repechage">repechage</Term> comes from the same thinking.
Conditions differ from one <Term id="heat">heat</Term> to the next, so a crew
that loses its heat gets a second race before it is out.
