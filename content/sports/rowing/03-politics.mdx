# Governing body politics

Rowing's governance is dominated by two things: a very long institutional
memory, and a fixed Olympic athlete quota that forces every addition to be an
exchange.

The quota is the immediate pressure. Rowing sends a large number of athletes
per event — an eight is nine people — so its Olympic footprint is expensive in
quota terms, and the IOC's requirement for parity by sex had to be met inside
that number rather than beyond it. The result was a sequence of swaps: the
lightweight four out and the women's coxless four in for 2020, and the
lightweight category removed entirely thereafter.

Lightweight rowing was introduced in 1996 to widen the sport beyond very large
athletes, which is the same reasoning volleyball used when it invented the
libero. It is now gone from the Olympic programme, and this site records that
the federation's stated reason was quota arithmetic while the sport's own
weight-making practices had been under scrutiny for years. Which of those was
operative is argued.

Beach sprint's arrival for 2028 is the other governance move and a shrewder
one. Flatwater rowing needs a purpose-built 2000-metre buoyed course, which
almost no host city has and which is expensive to build for two weeks. Beach
sprint needs a beach. A federation under programme pressure invented a format
that removes its own infrastructure requirement.

The amateurism history is the deep background. Rowing's nineteenth-century
British institutions excluded manual labourers explicitly, and that legacy is
still visible in where the sport is strong and who takes it up.
