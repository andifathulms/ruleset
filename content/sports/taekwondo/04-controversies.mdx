# Controversies that forced rule changes

**Beijing, 2008.** Judging across the taekwondo competition was widely
disputed, and the sport's continued Olympic status became a live question. Four
judges pressing buttons within a fraction of a second of each other produced
results that competitors could not verify and spectators could not follow, and
there was no mechanism to check them.

The response, within a year, was to install sensors. That is the most decisive
answer to a judging crisis anywhere in this collection — and it worked, in the
narrow sense that scoring disputes about body contact essentially stopped.

**What the sensors produced.** Immediately, a different problem. If a point
exists when a sensor registers enough force, the rational strategy is the
cheapest contact that clears the threshold, which turned out to be a low,
repetitive kick to the trunk. The sport became visually monotonous within a
season.

The federation's answer was to re-price: head kicks worth substantially more,
spinning and turning techniques worth more again. That is the Code of Points
instrument — deciding what the sport should look like and paying for it —
applied to a combat sport.

**The corners.** Competitors ahead on points used the square area's corners and
size to avoid engaging. The octagon in 2011 removed the corners and shrank the
space.

**Holding a lead.** Still not solved by 2022, when the contest stopped being
cumulative and became best of three rounds. A competitor can now outscore an
opponent across a match and lose, which is exactly what archery did in 2011 and
attracts exactly the same objection.
