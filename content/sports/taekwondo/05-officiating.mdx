# Officiating and technology

Taekwondo has automated more of its central judgement than any other sport on
this site, including tennis, and the comparison with tennis is the useful one.

Tennis automated a question about **where an object was** — a ball, a line, a
fact with a correct answer. Taekwondo automated a question about **how hard a
person was hit**, which is a physical quantity but was previously an opinion.
Before 2009 a point existed because judges agreed; after 2009 it exists because
a sensor exceeded a threshold.

That is why this sport appears in the adjudication lens as combat rather than
judged, and why it is the only sport here that changed what kind of sport it
is. The instrument did it.

What the officials kept is instructive. The referee still runs the contest and
awards **gam-jeom**, the penalty that scores directly for the opponent — and
because penalties can decide contests, that is now where the disputes live.
Video review, formalised in 2017, exists almost entirely for penalties and for
techniques the system did not register.

There is also a threshold problem no other sport has. A sensor scores above a
set force, and that force is set by the federation, differently for different
weight categories. So the sport has a dial that determines how hard a kick must
be to count — and turning it changes the results. No official is involved in
that decision at all; it is made in advance, in a specification.

That is the same structural fact as modern pentathlon's points tables and
gymnastics' difficulty values: a number written in an office doing work an
official does elsewhere.
