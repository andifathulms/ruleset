# Where it is played

The Pyrenees, and everywhere Basque and Spanish emigration went.

**The Basque Country**, on both sides of the Spanish–French border, and
**Navarre** are the sport's heartland, and the density is remarkable: a fronton
in almost every village, publicly owned, free to use, and in constant use. That
infrastructure is why pelota survived when the other European handball
traditions did not.

**Spain** and **France** more broadly play it, particularly in the
south-west of France, where the trinquete and the long-wall specialties have
their own strong tradition.

**Latin America** is the sport's second home, carried by Basque and Spanish
emigration: **Mexico**, **Argentina**, **Cuba**, **Uruguay**, **Chile** and
**Venezuela** all have real structures, and Mexico in particular has a deep
cesta punta tradition and produced many of the professionals who filled the
American frontons.

**The United States** had jai alai as a substantial industry in Florida and
Connecticut from the 1930s, sustained by parimutuel betting, and it has
effectively disappeared.

**The Philippines** had a significant jai alai scene through the twentieth
century, on the same betting model and with a similar outcome.

The pattern is the one this site keeps finding. Fistball follows German
emigration, boules follows French administration, soft tennis follows Japanese
rule — and pelota follows the Basque and Spanish diaspora, plus one American
industry that existed because of a gambling statute and ended when the statute
stopped mattering.
