# From a line to a wall

Pelota began when players stopped hitting a ball to each other and started
hitting it against a wall.

Its ancestor is *jeu de paume*, one of a family of old European games in which
a ball was struck by hand across a net or a line. [Tennis](/sports/tennis/)
grew from the same root. In the Basque Country the game moved from the line to
the wall. The wall game survived, and almost every other European handball
tradition did not.

## A wall in every village

It survived because of a building. A <Term id="fronton">frontón</Term> is a
high wall with a marked floor in front of it. Basque and Navarrese villages
built one beside the church or the town hall, as they might build a fountain.
It was public, free and always there. A sport with a court in every village
does not need clubs to keep it alive.

## Many games from one

Through the nineteenth century the game split into many. Longer walls made a
faster game and invited tools for hitting the ball. The solid
<Term id="pala">pala</Term> came, then the lighter paleta, then the stringed
<Term id="share">share</Term> for closed courts. Last came the
<Term id="cesta">cesta</Term>, a long curved basket that catches the ball and
slings it faster than any other ball in sport.

## One match in Paris

At the Paris Games of 1900 [pelota was played for
medals](#pelota-1900-olympic). Two teams entered, one Spanish and one French,
and one match was played. It came back three times as a demonstration, in
1924, 1968 and 1992, and has never been a medal sport again.

<Photo id="pelota-paris-1900" />

[The international federation was formed in 1929](#pelota-1929-fipv). The
game was played in different local forms across the Basque Country, Spain,
France and Latin America, and the federation did not choose one of them. It
recognised <Term id="specialty">specialties</Term>, each a combination of a
court and a way of striking the ball, and gave each its own competition.
Pelota is still several games that share a wall.
