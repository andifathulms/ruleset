# Origin and invention

Pelota is what happened to medieval handball when a wall turned out to be
better than an opponent.

The ancestor is *jeu de paume* and the family of European hand-ball games
played across a net or a line — the same root that produced tennis, and which
this site records elsewhere as a sport contested at the 1908 Olympics and now
nearly extinct. In the Basque Country the game moved from playing over a
dividing line to playing against a wall, and the wall version survived while
almost every other European handball tradition did not.

The reason it survived is infrastructure. A **fronton** — a high wall with a
marked floor, often beside the church or the town hall — became standard
municipal furniture in Basque and Navarrese villages, free to use and
permanently there. A sport with a public court in every village does not need
clubs to persist.

Through the nineteenth century the game diversified rather than converging.
Longer walls made a faster game and invited implements: the solid **pala**, the
lighter **paleta**, the stringed **share** for enclosed courts, and eventually
the long curved wicker **cesta**, which catches and slings the ball and
produces the fastest projectile in any sport.

Basque pelota was contested for medals at the 1900 Paris Games, where two
teams entered and one match was played, and returned three times as a
demonstration — 1924, 1968 and 1992.

The international federation, formed in 1929, made the decisive institutional
choice: rather than pick a code, it recognised **specialties**, each a
combination of a court and an implement, contested separately. Pelota remains
several sports that share a wall.
