# Who runs it, and who bets on it

The federation runs the world championships. Private companies run the game
that people watch.

Professional hand pelota in the Basque Country is organised by promotion
companies. They contract the players, set the pairings, schedule the matches
and sell them to television. The federation has no part in it.
[Sumo](/sports/sumo/) and [muaythai](/sports/muaythai/) have the same split
between an international body and the place where the audience and the money
are.

## The bookmakers in the stands

Betting is part of a match. In a Basque frontón the
<Term id="corredor">corredores</Term> work the crowd while the ball is in
play. They call the odds aloud as the match swings. To settle a bet they throw
a ball with a slit in it, holding the ticket, up into the stands. The players
can hear all of it from the court. It is legal and public, and it is regulated
as gambling. It also brings the risk that every sport with live betting
carries, that someone will try to fix a result.

## Florida, 1935 to 1988

In 1935 Florida [made betting on jai alai legal](#pelota-1935-parimutuel), as
it had for horse and dog racing. Jai alai is the American name for cesta
punta. Promoters built frontons, brought professionals from the Basque Country
and Latin America, and ran matches every day. The programme was designed
around the bets.

<Photo id="pelota-miami" />

In 1988 [the players went on strike](#pelota-1988-jai-alai-collapse) over pay
and conditions. The frontons used replacement players, the standard fell, and
much of the crowd did not come back. Then a state lottery, and later casinos,
gave people in Florida other ways to bet. Accounts differ on whether the
strike or the lottery did more harm. Both happened in the same years. American
jai alai, which had employed hundreds of players, shrank to almost nothing.

No rule of pelota changed. The game had been imported because betting on it
was legal, and it faded when that stopped being special.
