# Officiating

A judge on the court, in the middle of a ball travelling faster than anything
else in sport.

The calls are boundary calls. Did the ball reach the front wall? Did it land
inside the marked lines? Did it bounce twice before it was returned? On a
fifty-four-metre wall with a cesta, those judgements are made about an object
moving at around three hundred kilometres an hour, by an official standing on
the court.

**Interference** is the other recurring call, and pelota's version is
unavoidable rather than deliberate: two or four players share an undivided
court with no net and no lanes, and a player moving to the ball necessarily
moves through space an opponent wants. Judging whether an obstruction was
avoidable is the sport's equivalent of racquetball's hinder, and it is decided
the same way — by an official's reading of what a player could have done.

**The basket** adds a call unique to the sport. The ball must be caught and
slung in **one continuous motion**; holding it in the cesta, even briefly, is a
fault. That is a judgement about the duration of contact, made at speed, on an
implement designed to make the contact last as long as physics allows.

**In mano**, whether the ball was struck or scooped is the equivalent.

There is no instrumentation anywhere in the sport — no line technology, no
video review in most competition. A judge stands on a court and watches the
fastest ball in the world.
