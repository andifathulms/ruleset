# Hand, bat and basket

The hand came first. Every tool in pelota was invented to do something to the
ball that a hand cannot, and the hand game is still the one most people watch.

## The hand

In <Term id="mano">mano</Term> there is no glove. A player has tape, and small
adhesive pads built up over years on the parts of the hand that strike the
ball. The ball is hard: a core of rubber or wound thread under a stitched
leather cover.

<Photo id="pelota-mano-2019" />

Hands swell and bruise to the bone. They lose feeling, and the damage is
permanent. The state of a professional's hands decides whether he can play at
all, and hand injury ends more careers than anything else. [The rules of the
hand game](#pelota-bare-hand) allow no equipment that would help. A glove
would make it a different specialty, and the federation already runs those
separately. So players manage: tape, pads, fewer matches, and a career that
ends when the hands do.

## The bats and the racket

The pala is a heavy bat of solid wood. The paleta is a lighter one. Each has
its own ball, leather or rubber, which is why the bat games are separate
events. The share, a racket with strings, is used only in the closed court,
the <Term id="trinquete">trinquete</Term>.

## The basket

The cesta is strapped to the forearm. It catches the ball and slings it in one
continuous motion, and in <Term id="cesta-punta">cesta punta</Term> the ball
has been recorded at about three hundred kilometres an hour, the fastest in
any sport. Baskets are woven by hand from chestnut and reed and fitted to one
player. Very few people still make them.

<Photo id="pelota-chistera" />

A ball at that speed is a danger to players and, in the past, to the crowd.
Helmets are now compulsory in the basket games, and a net separates the
spectators from the court.
