# Two moments that changed the rules

## A throw of 104.80 metres

Berlin, 20 July 1984. Uwe Hohn threw the javelin 104.80 metres. In many
stadiums a throw of that length would have reached the running track on the
far side, or the crowd beyond it.

The distance was a safety problem. There was a second problem, and it was
harder. A javelin throw only counts if the point strikes the ground first. The
javelins of the time often landed flat, and when a javelin lands flat nobody
can say which part touched first. The judges were giving opinions about a
measurement.

The federation [redesigned the men's javelin](#iaaf-1986-javelin-redesign) so
that it would fly less far and land on its point. The new rule took effect on
1 April 1986, and the <Term id="record">world record</Term> list started
again that day. Hohn's mark was never beaten. It was retired together with the
javelin that made it. The [record with the new
javelin](#javelin-mens-wr) is 98.48 metres, thrown by Jan Železný in 1996,
and it is still about six metres short of Berlin.

## One false start

Sprinters were once allowed one <Term id="false-start">false start</Term>
each. Some learned to use it. A deliberate early move forced a restart and
unsettled a rival. In 2003 the rule changed: the whole field was allowed one
false start between them, and whoever made the next one was out.

[From 2010 there is no allowance at all](#iaaf-2010-false-start). The first
runner to make a false start is disqualified. The federation's stated reason
was television. Repeated restarts made it impossible to know how long a
sprint final would take inside a broadcast. Many people read the change
differently, as the end of the job begun in 2003. The federation gave the
scheduling reason.
