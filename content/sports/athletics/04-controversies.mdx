# Controversies that forced rule changes

**Uwe Hohn, Berlin, 20 July 1984.** 104.80 m. The throw is the trigger for the
1986 redesign in the plainest sense — it is the specific incident PRD §3 asks
for — but it is worth being precise about what the problem was. The distance
alone was a stadium-safety issue in the arenas the sport actually used. The
harder problem was that javelins thrown that far were increasingly landing flat,
and a flat landing cannot be judged: nobody can say whether the tip struck
first, so the measurement itself becomes a matter of opinion. A measured sport
can survive throws that are too long. It cannot survive throws it cannot
measure.

Hohn never threw with the new implement in competition at that level. His record
was not beaten; it was retired along with the object that produced it.

**The false start, three times.** Each athlete was once allowed one false start.
From 2003 the field collectively got one, and the next offender went out — a
rule introduced because athletes had learned to use a deliberate false start to
unsettle a rival. From 2010 the first false start disqualifies. The federation
gave scheduling as its reason. The change is at least as easily read as
finishing the anti-gamesmanship job started in 2003, and both readings sit in
the entry rather than one being chosen.

**The shoes.** No single incident, which is precisely why it is interesting. A
diffuse technological shift produced an unusual density of records across
several years, the federation legislated the technology and not the record book,
and the marks stand. Set this next to the javelin and the swimming supersuits
and the pattern is that governing bodies reset the record book when the break is
sharp and datable, and leave it alone when the change arrives gradually.
