# The javelin and the shoe

In a throwing event the <Term id="implement">implement</Term> is part of the
rules. Its mass and its dimensions are fixed, so that a throw in one stadium
can be compared with a throw in another. Twice, a piece of equipment has
changed the results faster than the athletes did. The sport answered the two
cases in opposite ways.

## A javelin that glided

Through the 1950s the American thrower Bud Held developed javelins with a
larger surface that glided better. Distances began to rise faster than
technique alone could explain. In 1964 Terje Pedersen became the first man to
throw beyond 90 metres, with an aerodynamic design. The federation tightened
the specification. Distances came down, and then climbed again.

<Photo id="athletics-javelin-1965" />

By 1984 the record stood at 104.80 metres, and the javelin was close to
outgrowing the stadium. [The 1986 redesign](#iaaf-1986-javelin-redesign)
changed one number. The men's javelin stayed at 800 grams and stayed within
the same length. Its centre of gravity moved four centimetres forward. The new
javelin tips nose-down earlier in flight. It flies about a tenth less far, and
it lands point first. The record list was started again from nothing.

Throwers soon found a way back. Javelins with roughened and dimpled tails
appeared within a few years and added several metres. They were [banned from
1992](#iaaf-1991-javelin-surface), and the marks set with them were cancelled.

The women's event went the same way. Petra Felke threw 80.00 metres in 1988,
and the mark stood for ten years. In 1999 [the women's 600-gram javelin was
redesigned](#iaaf-1999-womens-javelin-redesign) on the same principle, and the
women's record list was restarted too.

## A shoe that was allowed to stay

From 2016, racing shoes with thick foam soles and a carbon plate produced an
unusual run of distance records. Not every athlete could get them on equal
terms.

<Photo id="athletics-vaporfly" />

World Athletics [wrote limits into the rules in
2020](#wa-2020-shoe-regulations). A road shoe may have a sole up to 40
millimetres thick, with one rigid plate inside it. Track shoes have lower
limits. Every record set in the earlier shoes was allowed to stand.

So the same federation met the same kind of problem twice. With the javelin
it restarted the record list. With the shoe it kept the list and limited the
technology from then on. The sources do not settle why the two were treated
differently.
