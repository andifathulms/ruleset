# Equipment evolution

The javelin is the clearest case in this collection of a rule written against an
implement, and it happened twice.

Through the 1950s the American thrower Bud Held developed javelins with greater
surface area and better gliding characteristics, and distances began to climb
faster than technique alone would explain. Terje Pedersen passed 90 metres in
1964 with an aerodynamic design; the federation restricted the specification;
distances came back down and then climbed again. By 1984, when Uwe Hohn threw
104.80 m, the implement was outrunning the arena that was supposed to contain it.

The 1986 redesign is unusually surgical. The mass stayed at 800 grams and the
length stayed within the same band; what moved was the centre of gravity, four
centimetres forward. That makes the implement pitch nose-down earlier, which
both shortens the throw by roughly a tenth and restores a clean tip-first
landing. The federation solved a safety problem and a measurement problem with
one change to one number, and accepted that it was destroying the comparability
of its own record book in order to do it.

Then it happened again in a smaller way. Roughened and dimpled tail surfaces
appeared within a few years and recovered part of the lost distance; they were
banned with effect from 1992, and the marks set with them were annulled rather
than carried forward. And the same operation was performed on the women's
600-gram implement in 1999.

**Shoes are the counter-example.** Carbon-plated racing shoes from 2016 onward
produced a run of records at least as anomalous as anything the javelin ever
did. World Athletics wrote limits into the rules in 2020 — a maximum sole
thickness, one rigid plate — and left every record standing. Same federation,
same kind of problem, opposite decision about the record book. Whether the
difference is the physics or the commercial position of the shoe industry is not
something the sources settle.
