# Where it came from

Nobody invented running, jumping or throwing. People raced and threw things
long before anyone wrote a rule about it. What athletics had to invent was a
way to measure them.

<Photo id="athletics-amphora" />

The events are inherited. Some come from the ancient Olympic programme, some
from Highland and rural games across northern Europe, and some from military
training. A jar given as a prize in Athens about 530 BC shows runners in a
foot race. It proves the race is ancient. Nobody recorded how long it took.

The nineteenth century added the things that make one result comparable with
another: a standard distance, a standard <Term id="implement">implement</Term>,
a standard surface and, in time, a standard clock. This took a while. At the
first modern Olympics, in Athens in 1896, the 100 metres had a fixed distance,
but some runners started in a crouch and some started standing.

<Photos ids="athletics-caber athletics-athens-1896" />

The caber shows what was left behind. It is still tossed at Highland games,
and it never became a standard event, because a caber differs from one field
to the next and its result cannot be compared.

The International Amateur Athletic Federation was founded in 1912. Its first
large task was to ratify <Term id="record">world records</Term>, which meant
deciding when two performances count as the same thing done twice.

## Amateurs only

For most of its history the federation was for amateurs, and the word stayed
in its name until 2001. Amateurism was an eligibility rule. It decided who was
allowed to compete, and it had nothing to say about how.

Jim Thorpe won the pentathlon and the decathlon at Stockholm in 1912. The next
year he was stripped of both titles, because he had been paid to play
[baseball](/sports/baseball/). Paavo Nurmi was the leading distance runner of
the 1920s. In 1932 the federation suspended him for professionalism, and he
never ran the Olympic marathon he was preparing for.

<Photos ids="athletics-thorpe athletics-nurmi" />

The rule fell apart slowly through the 1970s and 1980s. First came trust-fund
arrangements, then open payment. It was the largest change
in the sport's history, and it altered no record, because it changed who could
enter and left the events as they were.

The name followed. The body became the International Association of Athletics
Federations in 2001, and World Athletics in 2019.
