# Equipment evolution

Rugby's equipment history is short, and its most interesting feature is what
the sport has refused to add.

The ball went from a pig's bladder in a leather case to a synthetic oval with a
grippy surface, which made passing and kicking more reliable without any rule
having to move. Boots and studs are specified for safety. That is nearly all of
it.

**Protective equipment is deliberately limited.** Padding is permitted only
within strict thickness limits, and the reasoning is explicit and worth
recording: hard protection encourages harder collisions. American football
provides the natural experiment — helmets and pads made the collisions
survivable and therefore made them harder, and the long-term neurological cost
of that is now well documented.

Rugby has looked at that and declined to follow.

**Headgear** is the item that most needs explaining. It is permitted, it is
worn, and it does not prevent concussion — it protects against lacerations and
cauliflower ear. World Rugby says so plainly, which is unusual candour about
equipment that looks protective and is not.

That leaves the sport with only one instrument for its central safety problem:
the Laws themselves. It cannot pad its way out of head impact, so it is
lowering the legal tackle height instead — changing what players may do rather
than what they may wear.

Set against boxing, which removed headguards in 2013 on contested safety
grounds, and against American football, which added armour and got harder
collisions, rugby's position is the most coherent of the three.
