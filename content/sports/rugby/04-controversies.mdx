# The tackle and the head

The biggest thing happening in rugby has no single date. Through the 2010s
evidence grew about what repeated blows to the head do over a career. The
concern covers diagnosed concussions and also the ordinary contact of every
match. Former professional players went to court, saying the sport had failed
to protect them. The sport has answered in three steps.

<Photo id="rugby-tackle" />

## Taking the decision off the pitch

Concussion used to be judged on the pitch, in seconds, by people watching a
match. Several players in well-known cases carried on after a visible
concussion. Since 2012 [a player with a suspected concussion leaves the
field](#rugby-2012-head-injury-assessment) for a structured examination. A
temporary replacement comes on, and the player returns only after passing.

## Punishing the high tackle

Head contact in the tackle is the main way players are concussed, and
referees had punished it unevenly. In 2019 World Rugby published [a framework
for the high tackle](#rugby-2019-high-tackle-framework). A
<Term id="high-tackle">high tackle</Term> now earns a penalty, a yellow card
or a red card according to the height of the contact, the danger and anything
that lessens the blame. The aim was a predictable sanction, because a
predictable sanction changes how players tackle.

## Lowering the tackle itself

The third step changes the action. [The legal tackle height was
lowered](#rugby-2023-tackle-height) in community rugby in several unions, to
the base of the breastbone and in some competitions to the waist. The change
was adopted in 2023 and applied from 2024, with trials moving up through the
game. A lower tackle means fewer clashes of heads between tackler and carrier.

A <Term id="tackle">tackle</Term> is what makes rugby the game it is. The law
now says where on the body it may begin.
