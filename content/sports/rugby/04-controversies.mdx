# Controversies that forced rule changes

**1895, and the split.** Northern English clubs wanted to compensate working
men for wages lost playing on Saturdays. The union refused, and the clubs left.
Rugby league exists because of a disagreement about payment, and the two codes
have had different laws ever since.

That is the clearest example on this site of an amateurism rule not merely
excluding people but permanently splitting a sport in two.

**1995, and the end of amateurism.** A hundred years later, union accepted
professionals. Unofficial payment had become widespread, a rival professional
circuit was being organised, and the 1995 World Cup demonstrated the commercial
value the amateur rule was suppressing.

**The head.** Not a single incident, and the largest thing happening to this
sport now. Evidence on the cumulative effects of repeated head impact — not
only diagnosed concussions but ordinary contact across a career — grew through
the 2010s, and former professionals brought litigation alleging the sport
failed to protect them.

Rugby's response has been sequential and unusually structural. A head injury
assessment protocol in 2012 removed the on-pitch judgement about whether a
concussed player continues. A published sanction framework in 2019 escalated
high-tackle penalties by contact height and danger. And from 2024 the legal
tackle height itself is being lowered.

**Whether it will work** is contested by players and coaches, who argue that
tackling lower produces more contact with hips and knees and may relocate
injuries rather than reduce them. The sport is changing its defining action on
an evidence base it does not fully agree about, which is an honest description
of a difficult position rather than a criticism.
