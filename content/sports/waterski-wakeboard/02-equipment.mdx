# Equipment evolution

Three pieces of equipment matter, and the athlete owns two of them.

**The skis and boards** are specialised to the point of mutual uselessness. A
slalom ski is a single ski with a deep fin, designed to hold an edge against
enormous lateral load. Trick skis are short and **finless**, so they can be spun
freely — a slalom skier cannot do tricks on their ski and a trick skier cannot
slalom on theirs. Jump skis are long. A wakeboard is a single board with
bindings and moulded fins, ridden sideways.

**The rope** is the least visible and most consequential item. It is
inelastic — any stretch would destroy the geometry the sport depends on — and it
is manufactured in standard lengths with defined shortening increments. Those
increments are what slalom scores are quoted in: "10.75 off" means the rope has
been shortened by 10.75 metres from full length. The rope is not merely
equipment; it is the measuring instrument.

**The boat** is the equipment the competitor does not own. It is approved by
specification, fitted with electronic speed control, and driven by somebody
else along a surveyed line. Hull design and wake characteristics have improved
steadily over eighty years, and every improvement makes a pass easier — which
is why comparing scores across eras compares boats as much as skiers.

For wakeboarding the boat matters even more directly: ballast systems shape the
wake, and the wake is the ramp. A wakeboarder's jump is made possible by an
object designed by a boat manufacturer and trimmed by a driver.
