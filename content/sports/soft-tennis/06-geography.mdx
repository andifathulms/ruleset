# Where it is played

Three countries play soft tennis seriously, and many others play it a little.

Japan is the home of the game. Soft tennis was invented there, and it is a
mainstream school and university sport. Its national competition is far
deeper than anything international. Most people who have ever played soft
tennis were Japanese schoolchildren.

<Photo id="soft-tennis-japan-2024" />

South Korea and Chinese Taipei are the other two powers. Both took up the game
during the period of Japanese rule. In Korea it drew crowds early. At the
national tournament for the Dong-A Ilbo's Flag in 1923, spectators packed the
ground around the court. The next year the national championships opened with
a ceremony. Today all three countries have national programmes, and they share
the Asian Games medals between them.

<Photos ids="soft-tennis-dong-a-1923 soft-tennis-korea-1924" />

Outside those three, the game is played in China, the Philippines, Thailand,
Indonesia and India, and in a scattering of federations in Europe and the
Americas. Most are small. Many began through Japanese cultural or education
programmes.

<Photo id="soft-tennis-india-2019" />

Cost is not what holds the game back. The ball and racket are cheap, and most
countries already have tennis courts. What has not travelled is the reason to
play. Soft tennis solved a supply problem, and where [tennis](/sports/tennis/)
balls are easy to buy, that problem does not exist.

Soft tennis is not an Olympic sport, and nobody is pushing for it. Tennis has
been on the Olympic programme since 1896, with a gap, and fills the place any
racket-and-ball case would need. The federation works instead on schools and
communities. A slow, soft, cheap ball is a good way to teach children a racket
sport, which is what it was made for.
