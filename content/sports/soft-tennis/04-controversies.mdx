# Controversies that forced rule changes

Soft tennis has one of the quietest legislative histories on this site, and the
reason is worth recording as much as the absence.

**The substitution itself.** The only decision that ever mattered, made around
1884 for cost reasons, and never reversed. When imported tennis balls became
affordable, Japan had already built a different sport with its own technique
and its own school structure, and there was no constituency for going back.

**Doubles or singles.** Codifying doubles as the principal format was a
recognition of practice rather than a controversy — doubles puts twice as many
students on the same number of courts, which is why the school system had
always favoured it. It does mean the sport's flagship event is the one tennis
treats as secondary, which affects how it reads to anyone encountering it from
outside.

**Scoring.** The four-point game predates international codification and has
not been rewritten. That is genuinely unusual: badminton, volleyball, table
tennis and fistball all rewrote their scoring between 1999 and 2006 for
broadcast, severing their own statistics. Soft tennis, with far more to gain
from television exposure than any of them, did not.

**Internationalising a Japanese sport.** The 1974 federation had to write one
rulebook across Japan, Korea and Taiwan, where the game had developed for
decades under separate national bodies. It was a reconciliation rather than a
dispute, and it is the moment the sport stopped being one country's.

There has been no safety controversy, no equipment arms race and no doping
scandal of any scale — which for a sport of this age is a notably clean record,
and follows from a ball that cannot hurt anybody.
