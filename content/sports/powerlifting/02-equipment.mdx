# Equipment evolution

Powerlifting's equipment history is the clearest case on this site of a support
garment turning into a machine.

Belts and wraps came first and were uncontroversial: a belt increases
intra-abdominal pressure, knee wraps and wrist wraps stabilise a joint. Then in
the late 1970s and 1980s came the squat suit and the bench shirt, made of dense
polyester and later canvas, cut deliberately smaller than the lifter.

These do not support the lift. They store elastic energy at the bottom of the
movement and return it on the way up, which means an equipped lift is a
different mechanical event from a raw one. A bench shirt's effect is large
enough that a lifter's equipped bench can exceed their raw bench by a margin no
other sport's equipment comes close to producing. Multi-ply gear — layers
stitched together — pushed it further still.

Two things follow, and both are recorded on this site as rules.

First, federations disagreed about how much to allow, and the disagreement is
one of the reasons their records are not comparable. Single-ply, multi-ply and
raw are effectively three sports.

Second, the IPF eventually stopped trying to resolve it and split its own
competition instead: a classic division from 2012 with its own championships
and its own records. That is the same instrument cycling used for the hour
record — separate the conditions and keep both books — and the opposite of
what swimming did with the supersuits, which was to ban the equipment and let
the marks stand.

Equipped lifters make an argument worth recording: using a bench shirt well
takes years, and the division is a discipline rather than an asterisk. Nothing
about the physics is in dispute; what the physics means is.
