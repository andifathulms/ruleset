# Governing body politics

Finswimming's governance problem is that it is a swimming sport and swimming's
federation has nothing to do with it.

CMAS governs finswimming as one activity among many underwater pursuits.
World Aquatics governs swimming, diving, water polo, artistic swimming and open
water. The two sports share a pool, a lane, a touchpad, a start and a
timekeeping convention, and are institutionally unconnected.

That matters for a specific reason. When a sport wants a place on a multi-sport
programme, it competes against sports in its own category, and finswimming's
category is already fully occupied by a federation with five Olympic
disciplines and a century of standing. A pool sport arriving from the diving
federation has no obvious route in.

CMAS's own priorities are also not primarily competitive. It certifies divers,
sets technical standards, and runs scientific and environmental programmes; a
racing discipline is a smaller part of its work than finswimming's athletes
would like. This is a milder version of the tension recorded on this site for
lifesaving, whose federation is a drowning-prevention body first.

Where finswimming has done well is the World Games, which is the intended home
for exactly this kind of sport: organisationally mature, genuinely
international, and with no plausible Olympic path.

The sport's geography reflects a further political inheritance. Its historic
strength is in Russia, Ukraine, Italy, Hungary, China, Colombia and Greece —
substantially the countries where state or club diving federations invested in
racing, rather than the countries that are strongest in swimming.
