# The fin that could not be banned

A <Term id="monofin">monofin</Term> is one large blade with a place for both
feet. The swimmer moves like a dolphin: a wave runs from the chest through the
hips and legs to the blade. This <Term id="dolphin-kick">dolphin kick</Term>
pushes far more water than two separate feet can, and it is why a finswimmer
covers a distance faster than a swimmer does with any stroke.

Early monofins were flat, wide blades on a pair of foot pockets. Modern blades
are shaped, and made from composites and glass fibre. They differ in stiffness
and profile, and a blade is chosen to suit the swimmer and the distance.

## Three sports, three answers

Monofin design moved quickly once people were racing. With no limit at all,
the blade would have decided the races.

Other sports have met the same problem.
[Swimming](/sports/swimming/) faced polyurethane suits in 2009, banned them,
and let the records stand. Several are still unbeaten.
[Athletics](/sports/athletics/) had a javelin that flew too far, redesigned
it, and started the record list again.

Finswimming could do neither. Ban the fin and there is no sport. Force every
swimmer onto one standard fin and a real skill is lost, because matching a
blade to your own body is part of the craft.

So [CMAS limited the size of the fin](#finswimming-monofin-regulation). The
surface area and the dimensions have a maximum. The materials are mostly left
open.

## Two fins or one

<Term id="bi-fins">Bi-fins</Term> are two ordinary fins, one on each foot.
They give a different speed and need a different technique. If the two kinds
raced together, one of them would be pointless. The sport made them separate
disciplines, each with its own <Term id="record-book">record book</Term>.
[Powerlifting](/sports/powerlifting/) did the same for lifting with and
without supportive equipment.

## Breathing gear

The rest of the kit depends on the discipline. A surface swimmer wears a wide
<Term id="snorkel">snorkel</Term> at the centre of the face, so the head
never has to lift or turn. An <Term id="apnea">apnea</Term> swimmer has
nothing. An <Term id="immersion">immersion</Term> swimmer carries a
<Term id="cylinder">cylinder</Term> of compressed air through the whole race.
The rules for that cylinder, its type and its valves, come from technical
diving.
