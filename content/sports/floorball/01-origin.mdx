# Origin and invention

Floorball began as school equipment and became a sport by accident.

Plastic floor hockey sets — light sticks and hollow balls, made for physical
education classes — were manufactured in the United States and exported to
Sweden in the late 1960s and early 1970s. They were toys for a gym lesson. What
happened next did not happen in the country that made them: Swedish, Finnish
and Swiss players started organising clubs and competitions around the
equipment, and by the late 1970s each had a codified game.

The three codes were not the same. Rink sizes, team sizes and restart
conventions differed, which meant three countries had independently invented
closely related sports that could not play each other. The International
Floorball Federation, formed in 1986 by the three national associations, exists
because of that problem, and its founding job was to choose between them.

What it produced is the sport described here: five outfield players and a
goalkeeper, a forty-by-twenty rink with low boards and rounded corners, three
periods of twenty minutes, free hits rather than face-offs, and a stick that
may never be raised above the waist.

Two of its decisions are the interesting ones. It gave the goalkeeper no stick,
which no other stick sport has ever done. And it did not write an offside rule,
which it has never added — field hockey abolished its own in 1998 and ice
hockey still has one, while floorball simply never had the thing to remove.

The IFF received IOC recognition in 2011 and floorball joined the World Games
programme in 2017. It has never been at an Olympic Games.
