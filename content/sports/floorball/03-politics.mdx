# Governing body politics

The IFF has done everything a small federation is supposed to do, and it has
not been enough.

It resolved three competing national codes into one rulebook in 1986. It grew
from three founding members to a large international membership across Europe,
Asia and Oceania. It obtained IOC recognition in 2011, joined the World Games
programme in 2017, and runs men's and women's world championships in
alternating years with genuine broadcast production in its core countries.

The obstacle is the one this site keeps recording for sports of this size, and
in floorball's case it is unusually stark: the competitive concentration.
Sweden and Finland have won almost everything the sport has ever offered, with
the Czech Republic and Switzerland the only consistent challengers. A sport
making a universality argument with those results has a problem it cannot argue
its way out of.

The federation's response has been developmental rather than rhetorical —
school programmes, small-sided formats, and growth in Asia, particularly
Singapore, Japan and Thailand, and in Australia and New Zealand. Participation
is genuinely broad. Elite results are not.

There is a second political question that floorball shares with the other
indoor sports here: it competes for the same halls, the same school time and
the same funding as handball, basketball and volleyball, all of which are
already on the Olympic programme. A sport can be cheap, safe and popular in
schools and still lose every argument about facilities to an incumbent.
