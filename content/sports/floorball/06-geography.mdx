# Where it is played

Two countries dominate, four more compete, and the participation map is much
wider than the results map.

**Sweden and Finland** are the sport. Both have substantially professional
leagues, deep club structures, floorball in the school curriculum, and
participation numbers that place it among the largest team sports in each
country. Between them they have won almost every world championship contested,
in both the men's and women's game.

**The Czech Republic and Switzerland** are the only consistent challengers, and
Switzerland's involvement is foundational — it was one of the three
associations that created the federation in 1986.

Beyond that core, floorball has real presence in Norway, Denmark, Latvia,
Estonia, Germany and Poland, and a growing one in Asia — Singapore in
particular has invested in it as a school sport, with Japan, Thailand and
Malaysia also competing — as well as in Australia and New Zealand.

The gap between participation and results is the sport's defining geographic
feature. Floorball is played by very large numbers of people across a wide
range of countries, because it is cheap, safe and fits in any sports hall. It
is won by two. A federation making an international case has both of those
facts to explain at once, and they point in opposite directions.

The reason for the concentration is not mysterious: Sweden and Finland are the
only places where a floorball player can be a full-time athlete, so everyone
good enough eventually plays there.
