# Controversies that forced rule changes

Floorball has had a legislatively quiet history, and the reasons are worth
recording as much as the events.

**The founding negotiation.** Three national codes with different rink sizes,
team sizes and restart conventions had to become one in 1986. Nothing about
that was a scandal, but it was the moment the sport decided what it was, and
several things that could have been in it — an offside rule, a goalkeeper with
a stick, permitted body contact — were decided against then rather than
removed later.

**The blade curve.** The one genuine equipment arms race the sport has had.
A more deeply curved blade lets a player lift and shoot a very light ball with
more control than the rules intended, and the permitted curve is capped and
measured. It is a small version of the same problem the javelin, the swimsuit
and the composite bat produced elsewhere on this site, caught early.

**Waist height.** The rule that makes the sport non-dangerous is also the rule
players are most often penalised under, because a natural follow-through goes
above the waist and has to be trained out. It is enforced strictly and is the
commonest call in the game.

**Concentration.** Not a rule change but the sport's central competitive
problem: two countries win everything. The IFF's format decisions — the
structure of world championship divisions, promotion and relegation between
them — are all responses to it, and none has changed the result.

What floorball has not had is a safety crisis, a doping scandal of any scale,
or a schism. For a sport nearly forty years old with a large playing base, that
is an unusually clean record, and it is largely because the founding rulebook
removed the mechanisms that generate those problems elsewhere.
