# Equipment evolution

The sport is named after a floor and defined by a ball with holes in it.

**The ball** is hollow plastic, 72 millimetres across, 23 grams, and pierced
with 26 holes. The holes are aerodynamic rather than decorative: they cut air
resistance and stabilise the flight, which is what makes a ball this light
controllable at speed in a small hall. A solid ball of the same mass would be
unusable. It is one of the more elegant pieces of equipment design on this
site, and it came out of a toy.

**The stick** weighs around 350 grams — roughly half a field hockey stick and
much less than an ice hockey one — and is made of composite plastic. The blade's
curve is limited by rule, which is the sport's equivalent of the bat and
implement regulations elsewhere on this site: without a cap, a deeper curve
would let a player lift and shoot the light ball in ways the rules did not
intend.

**The goalkeeper carries nothing.** They wear padding, a helmet and gloves, and
play from their knees, catching the ball and throwing it back into play. Every
other stick sport hands its goalkeeper a stick — usually a specialised one —
and floorball took it away.

**Outfield players wear no protection at all.** No pads, no gloves, no helmet,
no mouthguard requirement in most competition. That is only possible because of
the two rules that cap the sport's violence: no body checking, and no stick
above the waist.

The rink itself is equipment. Boards fifty centimetres high with rounded
corners keep the ball live and keep it moving, and the sport's continuous flow
is as much a product of that geometry as of any rule about restarts.
