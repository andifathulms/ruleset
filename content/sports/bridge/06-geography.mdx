# Where it is played

Old money, old clubs, and one country where it is genuinely a mass sport.

**Europe** is bridge's heartland: **Italy**, **the Netherlands**, **Norway**,
**Sweden**, **Denmark**, **Poland**, **France**, **England** and **Germany** all
have deep club structures and strong national teams, and Italian and Dutch
partnerships have dominated long stretches of the sport's history.

**The United States** has the largest single national organisation and a dense
tournament circuit.

**China** has invested seriously and is now a leading nation, and bridge has
significant participation there in a way it does not elsewhere in Asia —
though **India**, **Indonesia**, **Japan**, **Chinese Taipei** and **Singapore**
all compete, and it was Indonesia's hosting that brought bridge to the Asian
Games in 2018.

The distinctive geographic fact is **age**. In most of bridge's strongholds the
playing population is markedly older than in any other sport covered on this
site, and shrinking. Clubs that ran full sessions for decades are closing, and
the pipeline of new players is thin because the game's entry requirement — four
people, two partnerships, a shared learned system — is difficult to satisfy
casually.

Where bridge is growing it is generally because a national body has put it into
schools deliberately, which China and several European federations have done.
Online platforms have added players and have not replaced the club structure
that teaches them.
