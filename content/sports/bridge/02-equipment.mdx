# Cards, boards and screens

Bridge needs a pack of 52 cards. Everything else at a bridge table exists to
control what the players can learn from each other.

The <Term id="board">board</Term> holds each player's thirteen cards in a
slot. The deal stays whole after it is played, and it can be passed to the
next table. Without boards there would be no duplicate bridge, and without
duplicate the cards would decide too much.

A <Term id="bidding-box">bidding box</Term> replaces spoken bids with printed
cards laid on the table. The stated reason is clarity. The effect is that a
bid has no tone. A bid made after a long pause, or briskly, or unwillingly,
can tell a partner something the laws forbid them to know. A card on the table
looks the same however it was played.

<Photo id="bridge-bidding-boxes" />

A <Term id="screen">screen</Term> goes further. At the top level a barrier runs
diagonally across the table, so partners cannot see each other at all. Bids
pass underneath on a sliding tray. Each player can see one opponent and
cannot see their partner. Screens came in after cheating scandals in which
pairs were accused of signalling by gesture, expression and the way they
placed their cards.

<Photos ids="bridge-screen-table bridge-screen-from-above" />

The <Term id="convention-card">convention card</Term> does the opposite job.
It is a written summary of a pair's agreements about what its bids mean, and
it stays on the table [for the opponents to read](#bridge-full-disclosure).

<Photo id="bridge-convention-cards" />

So the equipment divides in two. Some of it keeps your partner from learning
things. The rest makes sure your opponents do.
