# The director and the hand that was not played

A tournament <Term id="director">director</Term> rules on what already
happened at a table, and often on what would have happened instead.

<Photo id="bridge-director-2014" />

Most of the work is routine. A <Term id="revoke">revoke</Term>, a lead out of
turn, a bid out of turn, a bid that is too low: the laws set out how each one
is put right. Applying them is technical work.

## The innocent partner

The hard rulings are about <Term id="unauthorised-information">unauthorised
information</Term>. A player hesitates, or asks a question, or fidgets. Their
partner has now learned something they had no right to learn, even if nobody
meant to send it. The law then falls on the partner. Of the actions open to
them, they must choose the one least suggested by what they were not supposed
to know.

If the partner did something else, the director has to decide whether a
player without that information could reasonably have done the same. Directors
ask expert players which actions were "logical alternatives" for a player of
that standard. If the answer is no, the director adjusts the score. The new
score is the result that would probably have happened at that table if the
hesitation had never occurred.

So a player can lose points for making the right bid, because they had a
reason for it they were not entitled to have. Rulings like this feel unjust
to the side that loses them, and appeals committees hear disputes about
them.

## Rulings nobody needs

The <Term id="screen">screen</Term> moves part of the director's job into the
furniture. Partners who cannot see each other cannot send a look or a gesture,
and a whole class of infringement never needs to be judged.
