# Governing body politics

Bridge is well governed, internationally recognised, demographically in
trouble, and was told by a court that it is not a sport.

The World Bridge Federation, formed in 1958, runs a serious world championship
cycle and holds IOC recognition. Bridge reached the Asian Games in 2018. On
paper that is a strong institutional position for a game with no physical
component.

The 2017 European court ruling is the sharpest thing in its politics. Asked
whether duplicate bridge qualified as a sport for a tax exemption, the court
said no, on the ground that it does not involve a significant physical element.
The federation's answer — that bridge is recognised as a sport by the IOC and by
many national bodies, and that a tax definition is not a general verdict — is
reasonable and did not prevail. This site records the same question being
litigated for cheerleading in the United States, with the same lack of a
settled answer.

The federation's real problem is not definitional. Bridge's playing population
is old and shrinking across most of the countries where it is strong, and the
reason is structural: the game needs four committed people at the same time, in
two partnerships, each of which must learn a shared bidding system before
either can play properly. That is a difficult product to sell against
alternatives available instantly and alone.

Online play grew sharply and brought its own crisis: screens and bidding boxes
cannot be built into a website, and a partnership communicating by any other
channel is undetectable in a way it is not across a table. Several
high-profile cheating cases followed, and the sport has had to build detection
systems it did not previously need.
