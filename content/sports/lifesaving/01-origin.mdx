# Rescue first, racing second

Lifesaving began as a way to stop people drowning. The racing came afterwards.

Drowning was a mass cause of death in the nineteenth century. Societies formed
across Europe and the English-speaking world to teach people to swim and to
rescue. Racing arrived as a training tool. A club that timed its members
carrying a weight across a pool could compare them, improve them and attract
more. [The first international federation was founded in France in
1910](#lifesaving-1910-federation) to organise that tradition.

## The surf clubs

The other half of the sport came from another place. In Australia and New
Zealand, surf lifesaving clubs patrolled ocean beaches. They built their own
contests around the equipment they used on patrol: the
<Term id="rescue-board">rescue board</Term>, the
<Term id="surf-ski">surf ski</Term> and the sprint along the beach. A club
carnival at Piha in New Zealand, around 1938, held a chariot race on the sand.
At the Australian championships of 1947 a Queensland club marched past with
its banner.

<Photos ids="lifesaving-piha-1938 lifesaving-march-past-1947" />

These events were shaped by surf. They have the same purpose as the pool
drills and little else in common with them.

## One federation

For most of the twentieth century the two traditions had separate governing
bodies and held rival world championships. [In 1993 they merged into the
International Life Saving Federation](#lifesaving-1993-ils-merger). The new
body kept both programmes. That is why the sport has pool events that can be
held anywhere and ocean events that need a particular kind of beach.

Lifesaving is on the World Games programme and has never been on an Olympic
one. The federation says openly that the competition is there to serve the
work of preventing drowning.
