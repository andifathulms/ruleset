# Origin and invention

Lifesaving is the only sport covered here that was invented as a public health
measure and became a competition afterwards.

Drowning was a mass cause of death in the nineteenth century, and national
societies formed across Europe and the English-speaking world to teach people
to swim and to rescue. Competition arrived as a training device: if a club
could time its members carrying a weight across a pool, it could compare them,
improve them and recruit more. The first international federation, founded in
France in 1910, organised that tradition.

The other half of the sport came from somewhere else entirely. Australian and
New Zealand surf lifesaving clubs, patrolling ocean beaches, developed their
own competitive culture around the equipment they actually used — the rescue
board, the surf ski, the beach sprint. Their events are not pool drills moved
outdoors; they are a different sport with the same purpose, shaped by surf
instead of by lane ropes.

Those two traditions were governed separately for most of the twentieth
century, ran competing world championships, and merged in 1993 into the
International Life Saving Federation. The merged body kept both, which is why
the sport today has a pool programme that can be held anywhere and an ocean
programme that needs a particular kind of beach.

Lifesaving entered the World Games programme at Akita in 2001. It has never
been on an Olympic programme, and the federation's own literature is unusually
candid that the competition exists to serve the drowning-prevention work rather
than the other way round.
