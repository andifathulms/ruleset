# Officiating

Lifesaving officials judge two things at once, and the second one is unusual:
did the competitor go fast, and would the rescue have worked?

**The clock** is the ordinary half. Pool events are timed as swimming is, with
touchpads and lane judges.

**The technique** is the other half, and it carries the sport's characteristic
sanction. A manikin whose airway goes under the surface is a disqualification.
A carry with a non-approved hold is a disqualification. A dropped manikin, a
surfacing during an underwater section, an improper release at the wall — all
disqualifications. There is almost no scale of penalty in this sport: either
the rescue was valid or it was not.

**The judged event** has no clock at all. In the simulated emergency response,
a team is confronted with a staged incident they have not seen and assessed on
their reading of the scene, their response, and their resuscitation, against
the protocols in force. It is scored by officials with rescue qualifications
rather than sports ones, and the marking standard changes when the underlying
medical guidance does.

**Beach flags** is judged on the sand: whether a competitor's start was legal
from a face-down position, and whether contact between two divers for the same
baton was incidental or impeding. It is the sport's most contested call and its
most physical moment.

What lifesaving does not have anywhere is instrumentation of technique. A
manikin's airway position could be sensed; it is judged by eye, by an official
standing at the poolside, for the same reason every small federation here gives.
