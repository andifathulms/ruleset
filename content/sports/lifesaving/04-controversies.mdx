# Controversies that forced rule changes

**Technique that was fast and wrong.** The recurring one, and the sport's
defining tension. Any timed event creates pressure to optimise, and in this
sport optimisation eventually produces a carry, a tow or a release that is
quicker and would not save anybody. The federation's answer is a standing rule
that technique must reflect current rescue practice, enforced by
disqualification rather than by a time penalty.

**Changing medical guidance.** When resuscitation protocols are revised by the
bodies that set them, the sport's judged emergency event has to be revised too
— and competitors who trained the previous sequence are training the wrong
answer. It is the only sport on this site whose correct technique is
periodically redefined by people who have nothing to do with sport.

**Merging two sports.** The 1993 unification had to decide what counted.
Pool and ocean events measure the same purpose in conditions that have nothing
in common, and the federation chose to keep both rather than pick. The
consequence is a world championship in two halves whose results cannot be
compared, and a World Games programme that is mostly the pool half because that
is the half that travels.

**Beach flags contact.** Two competitors diving for the same buried baton
collide, and the sport's most argued call is whether one of them impeded the
other. It is also the event with the highest injury rate, in a sport whose
entire purpose is preventing harm.

**Ocean conditions.** Surf, current and sandbanks differ between heats, so an
ocean result partly reflects the draw. The sport records the times and does not
present them as records, which is the same position rowing takes.
