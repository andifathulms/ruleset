# A federation that puts prevention first

The International Life Saving Federation is a humanitarian body that also runs
a sport, and it says so.

Its main work is to prevent drowning. It sets standards for lifeguards, runs
education programmes, publishes statements on water safety and works with
health bodies. Drowning kills hundreds of thousands of people a year, most of
them in low- and middle-income countries. The federation says that number
matters more to it than the medal table.

The sport serves that work. Competition trains lifeguards beyond the standard
their qualification asks for. It brings volunteers into clubs, and it gives
people a reason to stay after they have passed an award.

The federation was born from a compromise. The European pool tradition saw the
sport as standard rescue drill. The surf tradition of Australia and New
Zealand had a large, wealthy and well-organised club culture built on ocean
patrol. The two had reasons to distrust each other.
[The merger of 1993](#lifesaving-1993-ils-merger) kept both programmes, and
that was a piece of diplomacy as much as a sporting choice.

The Olympic question is an awkward one. The federation is recognised by the
IOC and has a place at the World Games, and an Olympic place would bring
attention to the prevention work. But the ocean half of the sport needs a surf
beach, and the pool half looks to a newcomer like swimming with obstacles. The
federation has never acted as if an Olympic place were its reason to exist.
