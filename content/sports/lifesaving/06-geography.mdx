# Where it is played

The competitive map and the need map point in opposite directions, and the
federation knows it.

**Competitively**, the sport belongs to Australia and New Zealand, whose surf
lifesaving club culture is large, wealthy and deeply embedded — beach patrol is
a mass volunteer activity there in a way it is almost nowhere else — and to
Germany, Italy, France, the Netherlands and Belgium, whose pool tradition
descends directly from the 1910 federation. Japan and Canada compete strongly.
Those countries win essentially everything.

**The drowning burden** is overwhelmingly elsewhere: South and Southeast Asia
and sub-Saharan Africa account for the large majority of the world's drowning
deaths, in populations with limited access to swimming instruction and almost
no organised lifesaving sport at all.

The federation's prevention work is directed at the second map and its
competition happens on the first. That is not hypocrisy — it is the ordinary
shape of a sport that requires pools, patrolled beaches and volunteer club
structures, all of which are expensive — but it does mean the countries that
win the world championships are not the countries the organisation exists to
help.

Within the sport there is a further split by environment. The ocean programme
is concentrated in countries with surf beaches and patrol traditions; the pool
programme travels anywhere with a fifty-metre pool, which is why it forms the
bulk of the World Games competition and why a European nation can be a world
power in half of a sport.
