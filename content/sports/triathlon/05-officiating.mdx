# Officiating and technology

Triathlon's officiating history is a case study in solving a problem by
deleting it.

For twenty years the sport's central officiating task was judging drafting: was
this rider inside the zone behind that one, and for how long, and were they
overtaking? It required marshals on motorcycles making repeated judgements
across an entire bike course, and it was the sport's principal source of
disputes.

In 1995 the federation legalised drafting at elite level, and the officiating
problem disappeared entirely. That is the same move hockey made when it
abolished offside in 1998 — a rule that could not be applied consistently was
removed rather than refined — and both federations found the sport worked fine
afterwards.

Age-group racing kept the rule and kept the problem, and it is still enforced
by marshals with a penalty box.

What officials police at elite level now is mostly **transition**: helmets
fastened before the bike leaves the rack, mounting and dismounting behind the
line, equipment inside the designated area. These are procedural questions with
correct answers, they are enforced strictly, and they have decided races.

Timing is fully instrumented with transponders, and the sport publishes leg
splits. Those splits are not comparable across 1995 and are not comparable
between courses, and triathlon — unlike rowing — has never made a statement
about what its numbers mean.
