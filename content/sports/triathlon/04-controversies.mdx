# Controversies that forced rule changes

**Drafting, 1995.** Not an incident, a decision — and the largest single change
to any sport on this page.

Before it, riding in another competitor's slipstream was a penalty offence, and
enforcing it meant motorcycle marshals judging a gap between moving bicycles
for 40 km. It was expensive and inconsistent, and races were regularly decided
by a penalty somebody disputed.

Legalising drafting removed the enforcement problem completely and produced a
race that arrives at the final transition together. It also removed cycling
ability as a factor. An Olympic triathlon is now decided by a 10 km run, and
the sport has been arguing about whether that is a triathlon ever since.

**The two rulebooks.** Keeping drafting banned in age-group racing was a safety
decision about mass fields of inexperienced riders, and it is defensible. It
also means that almost every person who competes in triathlon is competing in a
different sport from the one they watch, with different rules and different
bicycles.

**The water.** Open-water swim legs depend on water quality the organiser does
not control, and several major events — including recent Olympic ones — have had
swims shortened, postponed or cancelled after rainfall. The rules now specify
thresholds and a contingency: the event becomes a duathlon.

That means a result in the sport's own records can describe a two-discipline
race, which is a comparability problem triathlon has never named.
