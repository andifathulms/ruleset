# Where it is played

Tennis has the widest elite geography of any racket sport on this site and one
of the narrowest participation bases relative to its visibility, and the gap
between those two things is money.

Champions have come from Switzerland, Spain, Serbia, the United States, Russia,
Japan, Australia, Germany, Romania, Poland, Italy and China in recent decades,
which is genuinely global. But the pathway to producing them is not. A
competitive junior needs court time, individual coaching, restringing and
international travel from an early age, and that combination costs more than
any other sport here except cycling and equestrian.

The result is a sport whose elite is internationally diverse and
socioeconomically narrow almost everywhere. National federations that have
broken that pattern have done it by building public court infrastructure — the
Spanish and more recently Italian systems are the usual examples — rather than
by talent identification.

The four majors are in Australia, France, Britain and the United States, which
is a nineteenth-century distribution that the sport has never revisited. Three
of the four are in countries with a combined population smaller than several
that have never hosted one.

The surface question has a geography too. Clay is the dominant surface in
continental Europe and South America, hard courts in North America and Asia,
grass almost nowhere. A player's home surface shapes their game permanently, and
because tennis never standardised, that is a genuine competitive fact rather
than a preference.
