# Equipment evolution

Tennis has the strangest equipment history on this site: the change that
transformed the sport required no rule, and the change that required a rule
lasted about a year.

**The frame.** Rackets were wood for a century, then aluminium, then graphite
composite, and the effect was enormous — larger heads, far greater stiffness,
and a game that moved from touch and placement to power and spin. The serve
became a weapon. Rallies shortened. Wimbledon in the 1990s was widely held to
have become unwatchable because of it. No rule changed at any point. The ITF
specified racket length and little else, and the transformation happened
entirely inside the specification.

**The strings.** In 1977 someone discovered that a double-strung racket, with
loose strings threaded through tubing rather than woven, produced spin no
conventional racket could match. Unranked players began beating established
professionals with it within weeks. The ITF amended the rules the following
year to require interlaced stringing, which meant writing down for the first
time what a racket face actually was.

The contrast is the point. A gradual transformation that changed the sport
completely provoked no rule; an abrupt one that upended a few months of results
provoked an immediate one. That is the pattern across this whole site — swimming
tolerated the suits until forty-three records fell in eight days, athletics
tolerated the javelin until one throw reached the far end of the stadium.

The ball has been specified by bounce height since the 1920s and has not needed
to move. Surfaces remain unstandardised on purpose.
