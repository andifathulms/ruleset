# Origin and invention

Tennis descends from a game played with the palm of the hand in French
monastery cloisters from the twelfth century — *jeu de paume*, real tennis,
still played today on a handful of courts and briefly an Olympic sport in 1908.
The scoring system tennis uses now came from there, which is why it makes no
sense: 15, 30, 40 is inherited from a game with different architecture, and the
best explanation anyone has is a clock face.

Lawn tennis is a Victorian repackaging. Walter Clopton Wingfield patented a
portable version in 1874 — a net, rackets, rubber balls and a set of rules in a
box, sold for garden parties. The All England Croquet Club adopted it, added
"and Lawn Tennis" to its name, and held the first Wimbledon championship in
1877, which promptly standardised a court shape Wingfield had drawn as an
hourglass.

Two inheritances still shape the sport. **The scoring**, which nobody would
design and which tennis has spent fifty years patching rather than replacing.
And **the surface**, because lawn tennis was played on grass and the sport
never subsequently insisted that everyone play on the same thing — which is why
tennis is the only scored sport on this site where the ground itself varies
between its major events.

The professional and amateur games were separate until 1968, when the Open era
began. Tennis returned to the Olympics as a full medal sport in 1988, sixty-four
years after being dropped over exactly that dispute.
