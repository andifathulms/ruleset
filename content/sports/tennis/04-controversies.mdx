# Controversies that forced rule changes

**Isner–Mahut, Wimbledon 2010.** Eleven hours and five minutes, played across
three days, the final set ending 70 games to 68. It is the most extreme single
match in the history of any sport on this site and it happened forty years
after tennis had invented the device that would have prevented it. Wimbledon
simply did not apply the tiebreak to the deciding set.

The response took nine more years, and then produced three different answers at
three different tournaments before the sport agreed on one in 2022. That
sequence — a clear trigger, an available solution, and fifty-two years between
the invention and its consistent application — is the best illustration on this
site of what fragmented governance costs.

**The spaghetti racket, 1977.** Loose double-stringing produced spin nobody
could read, and players outside the top hundred started beating professionals
with it. The ITF banned it within a year. It is the fastest response to a
technology in this collection, and the contrast with the graphite revolution —
which changed the sport far more and provoked no rule at all — is the thing
worth noticing.

**Every line call ever argued.** Tennis's officiating history is a long series
of disputes about whether a ball was in, conducted in public by players who
could see the mark and officials who had a fraction of a second. Electronic
review arrived in 2006 and let players challenge; in 2025 tour-level tennis
removed line judges entirely and let the machine make every call.

**Amateurism.** The sport was removed from the Olympic programme after 1924 over
who was allowed to be paid, and stayed away for sixty-four years. No other rule
change on this site cost a sport that much time.
