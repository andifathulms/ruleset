# Who makes the calls

For most of its history a tennis match was run by a
<Term id="chair-umpire">chair umpire</Term> and a team of line judges. The
line judges called each ball in or out. The umpire could overrule them. Both
were judging a ball that travels at up to 200 km/h and touches the ground for
a few thousandths of a second.

<Photo id="tennis-line-judges" />

Players could often see the mark and argued in public. From the 1990s
television showed viewers the correct answer while the players still had no
right to ask for it.

## The challenge

[In 2006 players were given that right](#itf-2006-electronic-review). A
tracking system worked out where the ball had landed and showed it on a
screen to the crowd. Each player had a limited number of wrong challenges per
set. The official stayed, and the machine checked the official.
[Football](/sports/football/), [volleyball](/sports/volleyball/) and
[judo](/sports/judo/) later added review in the same way, keeping their
officials.

<Photo id="tennis-hawk-eye" />

## The machine alone

[In 2025 the professional tours went a step further and removed the line
judges](#itf-2025-electronic-line-calling). Electronic line calling now makes
every call as the ball lands, and there is nothing left to challenge. The
Grand Slams adopted it one by one. Many people in tennis were sorry to lose
the human part of the game, and line judges lost their work. Neither
complaint was about accuracy.

<Photo id="tennis-centre-line-judge" />

Tennis could do this because its main question is simple. Was the ball inside
the line? That is a fact about where an object was, and a camera can answer
it. No one has to judge what a player meant to do.

The chair umpire keeps the decisions that need judgement:
<Term id="hindrance">hindrance</Term>, the code of conduct, time violations
and the crowd.
