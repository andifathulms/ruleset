# Controversies that forced rule changes

**Melbourne, 1956.** The match remembered as Blood in the Water was played on
6 December 1956, weeks after the Soviet army had crushed the Hungarian
uprising. Hungary led the Soviet Union 4–0 when the referees stopped it, and
the photograph of Ervin Zádor leaving the pool with a cut head became the
image of the Games. Hungary went on to win gold, and many Hungarian athletes did not
go home. It changed no rule. It is here because it is what most people know
about the sport, and because it shows how much of the game is a fight.

**The game nobody could see.** Water polo's real controversy is structural.
Holding, sinking and pulling back happen underwater, out of the referee's
view, and for most of the sport's history the advantage went to whoever was
best at fouling unseen. Each wave of rule changes — free movement after a
whistle in 1949, timed exclusions from 1971, the shorter clocks of 2005 and
2019 — has tried to make the game faster and more open. Researchers who have
studied them read them as attempts to make the sport more attractive to watch;
the federation's own reasons for the older ones are not on record.

**Exclusions that decide matches.** Since the 1970s a major foul has sent a
player out for a set time, and the length of that time has been cut again and
again — sixty seconds in 1971, then forty-five, thirty-five, twenty, briefly fifteen,
and since 2025 eighteen. Because a power play is the likeliest way to score, the exclusion
clock is effectively the sport's most important tactical rule, and changing it
changes how every team plays.

**2024–25.** The rule package of November 2024 and its partial reversal in May
2025 are the sport's most recent controversy. The dispute was about process as
much as content: whether a change presented as a trial should have been
adopted without one.
