# Blood in the water, and the fight nobody sees

## Melbourne, 6 December 1956

Hungary met the Soviet Union in the Olympic pool, a few weeks after the Soviet
army had crushed the Hungarian uprising. Hungary led 4–0 when the referees
stopped the match. Ervin Zádor of Hungary climbed out of the pool with a cut
head, and the photograph of him became the image of those Games. The match is
remembered as Blood in the Water.

Hungary went on to win the gold medal, and many Hungarian athletes did not go
home afterwards. The match changed no rule. It is still the one thing most
people know about water polo, and it shows how much of the game is a fight.

## The game under the surface

The lasting trouble in water polo is quieter. Holding, sinking and pulling
back happen under the water, out of the referee's sight. For most of the
sport's history the advantage went to the team that fouled best without being
seen.

<Photos ids="water-polo-centre-forward water-polo-scramble" />

Wave after wave of rule changes has tried to open the game up. Players were
allowed to keep moving after the whistle in 1949. Timed exclusions came in
1971. The clocks were shortened [in 2005](#fina-2005-thirty-second-possession)
and again [in 2019](#fina-2019-twenty-second-reset). Researchers who have
studied these changes read them as attempts to make the sport better to
watch. One study of the 2005 rules found shots and goals both up by about
eighteen per cent. The federation's own reasons for the older changes are not
on record.

## The exclusion clock

Since 1971 a <Term id="major-foul">major foul</Term> has sent the offender out
of the water for a set time. That time has been cut again and again: sixty
seconds at first, then forty-five, thirty-five and twenty. For six months it
was fifteen, and [since 2025 it has been
eighteen](#wa-2025-twenty-eight-eighteen).

The number matters because a <Term id="power-play">power play</Term> is the
likeliest way to score. Each change to the exclusion clock changes how every
team attacks and defends.
