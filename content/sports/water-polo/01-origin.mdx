# Football in the water

Water polo began as a show for a swimming gala. In 1877 the Bon-Accord club
of Aberdeen asked William Wilson, a swimming-club official from Glasgow, to
invent a game that would liven up its gala. He drew up rules for what he
called aquatic football. The first match was played in open water on the
River Dee, from one bank to the other. A revised version was played in
Paisley Baths that October.

## The pool gives it a shape

Indoor baths gave the game fixed ends, fixed goals and a set area. By 1885 the
Swimming Association of Great Britain had sent round an agreed set of rules,
and the game spread across Europe in that form. A rougher American version
was played at the St Louis Olympics in 1904. The European game outlasted it.

Even so, matches went on being played in rivers for years, with floating
goals and a field marked out on the current.

## At every Olympics since 1900

Water polo has been at every Olympic Games since 1900, the longest unbroken
run of any team sport. Great Britain won four of the first five tournaments.
After that the strength of the game moved east, and Germany beat Hungary 5–2
in the 1928 final in Amsterdam.

<Photos ids="water-polo-great-britain-1912 water-polo-amsterdam-1928-final" />

One thing has stayed the same from the start. The water is too deep to stand
in, so every player treads water the whole time. The struggle for position
goes on below the surface, where spectators and referees cannot see it
clearly. Most of the sport's arguments since have come from that.
