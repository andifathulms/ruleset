# Origin and invention

Water polo began as a swimming club's entertainment and took twenty years to
become a ball game rather than a brawl.

In 1877 William Wilson, a Glasgow swimming-club official, was asked by
Aberdeen's Bon-Accord club to devise a game to enliven its gala, and drew up
rules for what he called aquatic football. The first version was played in
open water on the River Dee, bank to bank; a revised one was played in Paisley
Baths that October.

The pool tightened it. Indoor baths gave fixed ends, fixed goals and a defined
area, and by 1885 the Swimming Association of Great Britain had circulated an
agreed set of rules. The game spread across Europe on that pattern, and when
an American variant with rougher rules was played at St Louis in 1904, it was
the European game that survived.

<Photos ids="water-polo-paris-1900 water-polo-seine-1912" />

Water polo has been on every Olympic programme since 1900, the longest
unbroken run of any team sport at the Games. Great Britain won four of the first five
tournaments, and then the sport moved east.

<Photos ids="water-polo-great-britain-1912 water-polo-amsterdam-1928-final" />

One feature has never changed and explains the rest of this page. The water
is deep enough that nobody can stand, so every player is permanently treading
water, and the fight for position happens below the surface where neither the
spectators nor the referees can see it clearly.
