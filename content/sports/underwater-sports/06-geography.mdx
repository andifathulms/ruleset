# Where it is played

Two sports, two overlapping maps, both drawn by diving-club culture and by the
availability of pools.

**Underwater hockey** is strongest in the countries whose sub-aqua club
traditions produced it and adopted it early: **Great Britain**, where it was
invented, and above all **New Zealand**, **Australia**, **South Africa**,
**France**, **Colombia**, **Turkey** and **Canada**. New Zealand and Australia
have been dominant for long stretches, which for a sport invented in Portsmouth
is a reversal worth noting.

**Underwater rugby** is a northern European sport almost exclusively.
**Germany**, where it was invented, and **Norway**, **Sweden**, **Denmark**,
**Finland** and **Colombia** are the powers — Colombia again being the outlier,
as it is in ultimate, finswimming and roller speed skating, and for the same
reason: a small, concentrated national programme in a sport nobody else is
funding heavily.

The constraint on both is pools, and on underwater rugby it is severe. The
sport needs at least three and a half metres of depth, and municipal pool
construction has trended shallower for decades — cheaper to build, cheaper to
heat, better suited to swimming lessons. A country's underwater rugby scene
depends on how many deep pools it happens to have inherited.

That is an unusual kind of geographic determinism. Most sports on this site
spread where somebody invested or where a diaspora carried them. This one
spreads where the twentieth century happened to leave deep water indoors.
