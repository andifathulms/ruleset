# Referees who breathe under water

The referees are in the water, wearing <Term id="scuba">scuba</Term>, and they
move with the play. An official at the surface cannot see a game played on the
floor of a pool. In underwater rugby that official cannot see most of the
field at all. So the referees dive, and carry the air that the players are
forbidden.

Even this does not show them everything. In underwater rugby the play fills a
deep space, and bodies hide each other in every direction. Several referees
in the water work with officials at the surface. The number of officials and
where they are placed have been changed more than once to close the gaps.

A whistle is no use under water. Play is stopped with hand signals and with
devices that make a sound the players can hear. The link between the referees
below and the officials above is planned as part of the competition.

## The route to the air

The calls are of two kinds.

Ordinary fouls are the kind every team game has. Attacking a player who does
not have the ball is one. So is obstruction, and so is dangerous use of the
stick in hockey.

Air offences are the grave ones. Nothing may stop an opponent from
<Term id="surfacing">surfacing</Term> to breathe.
<Term id="holding-under">Holding a player down</Term>, pulling a player back,
or touching a mask or snorkel are the most serious offences in the rules. The
reason is plain. These games are a struggle for a ball at depth, and one bad
decision could drown somebody.

<Photos ids="underwater-sports-rugby-at-goal underwater-sports-rugby-tackle-sydney" />

Underwater rugby allows a player to wrestle the one who has the ball. In water
this is much gentler than on land, and it can still trap a person at the
bottom. The line between a fair contest and a dangerous hold has been
rewritten many times. Finding that line in a game is the hardest judgement a
referee makes.
