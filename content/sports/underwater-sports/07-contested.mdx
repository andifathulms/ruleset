# What is still argued about

**Whether anyone can watch.** Players call both games rich in tactics and
hard on the body. A spectator at the side of the pool sees splashing.
Underwater cameras and live streams help. They do not solve the problem,
because a pool is not built for filming and the viewers are watching a game
they have never seen played. No change to the rules can reach this.

**Deep pools.** Underwater rugby needs at least three and a half metres, and
new pools are built shallower. The growth of the game depends on town
planners who are not thinking about it. The permitted depth is a range, too,
so the size of the playing space differs from one pool to another, and the
sport chooses its championship pools with care.

**Contact and air.** Where does a fair fight for the ball end and
<Term id="holding-under">holding under</Term> begin? The rule has been
rewritten many times, and it will be rewritten again.

**What the referees cannot see.** Even on scuba, the referees cannot watch the
whole <Term id="water-column">water column</Term> at once. More officials,
better positions and help from the surface are the answers so far. None is
complete.

**A place in the federation.** CMAS mostly deals with diving standards and
technical work. It also governs [finswimming](/sports/finswimming/),
freediving, underwater orienteering and underwater photography. These have
little in common with two team games. How much attention a small federation
should give those games is a real question inside it.

**Spearfishing.** CMAS also governs competitive spearfishing. Several
countries restrict or ban it to protect fish stocks, and the same federation
runs programmes for the marine environment. The objection comes from outside
the sport, and the federation has not settled it.
