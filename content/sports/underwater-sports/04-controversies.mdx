# Controversies that forced rule changes

**Getting to the air.** The safety rule that defines both sports: nothing may
prevent an opponent surfacing to breathe. Holding a player down, interfering
with a mask or snorkel, or blocking a route to the surface are the gravest
offences in the rulebook, and they exist because the ordinary business of these
games — wrestling for a ball at depth — is one bad decision away from
drowning somebody.

**Contact in underwater rugby.** The sport permits wrestling the ball carrier,
which in water is much gentler than on land and can still trap a person at
depth. The boundary between legitimate contest and dangerous restraint has been
rewritten repeatedly and is the sport's central officiating judgement.

**Officiating what nobody can see.** Referees are in the water, on scuba, and
in underwater rugby they still cannot see the whole volume. The number of
officials, their positioning and the use of surface officials have all been
adjusted to close gaps in coverage.

**Pool depth.** Underwater rugby specifies a depth range, and the volume of the
playing area varies within it. That makes venue selection a competitive
variable, and it is why the sport is careful about championship pools.

**Spearfishing.** Not a rule change inside the team sports, and the federation's
most contested activity. Competitive spearfishing is restricted or prohibited
in several countries on conservation grounds, and whether it belongs under the
same body as marine environmental work is argued from outside the sport.
