# Things that must not float

The players need very little kit. The two objects they play with share one
property: they sink.

The hockey <Term id="puck">puck</Term> is lead and weighs about 1.3
kilograms. It usually has a plastic coat. The weight keeps it on the floor,
where the game is played, and makes it steady enough to pass with a short
stick. A light puck would drift and rise, and there would be no game.

<Photo id="underwater-sports-glove-stick-puck" />

The rugby <Term id="ball">ball</Term> is filled with saltwater. That makes it
denser than the pool water, so it sinks at a steady rate. The sinking keeps
the play deep. A ball that floated would pull every contest up to the surface
and flatten the game.

<Photo id="underwater-sports-rugby-ball" />

The hockey <Term id="stick">stick</Term> is about thirty centimetres long and
is held in one hand, with a protective glove on that hand. Under the
international rules a stick must fit inside a box 350 mm long, 100 mm wide and
50 mm deep. It pushes the puck and does not strike it. The skill everyone
admires is the <Term id="flick">flick</Term>: moving a lead puck accurately
with a short lever, often upside down, and without air.

<Photo id="underwater-sports-stick-dimensions" />

Everything else is diving equipment. Players wear a mask, a
<Term id="snorkel">snorkel</Term> and fins. Hockey players add a mouthguard,
and both games use caps with ear protectors. No player carries anything to
breathe from.

<Photos ids="underwater-sports-hockey-kit underwater-sports-rugby-caps" />

The referees do. They work in the water on <Term id="scuba">scuba</Term>.
They carry more equipment than the players, and they can stay in the field of
play longer than any player can.
