# Equipment evolution

Very little kit, and two objects designed around a single physical property:
they must not float.

**The puck** in underwater hockey is lead, around 1.3 kilograms, usually
sheathed in plastic. Weight is the point — it keeps the puck on the pool floor
where the game is played, and gives it enough inertia to be flicked accurately
by a short stick. A light puck would drift and rise, and the sport would not
exist.

**The ball** in underwater rugby is filled with saltwater, which makes it
denser than the pool water around it so that it sinks at a controlled rate.
This is the most elegant equipment decision on this site: the negative buoyancy
is what keeps play at depth. A ball that floated would drag every contest to
the surface and turn a three-dimensional game into a two-dimensional one.

**The stick** in hockey is about thirty centimetres, held in one hand, with a
protective glove on that hand. It is a pusher rather than a striker, and the
sport's signature skill is the flick — moving a lead puck accurately with a
short lever, from an inverted position, without air.

Everything else is diving equipment: mask, snorkel, fins, mouthguard and ear
protection. No player carries a breathing apparatus of any kind.

The **referees** do. Officials work in the water on scuba, which makes them the
only officials covered on this site who need more equipment than the athletes,
and the only ones who can stay in the field of play longer than the competitors
can.
