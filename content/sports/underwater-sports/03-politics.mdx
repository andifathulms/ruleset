# Governing body politics

These sports sit inside a federation whose main business is not sport, and
their difficulty is one this site has met before in a more extreme form.

CMAS governs underwater activity broadly — diving standards, technical and
scientific work, and a set of competitive disciplines of which the two team
sports are only part. Finswimming, freediving, underwater orienteering,
underwater photography and competitive spearfishing all sit under the same
body, and they have almost nothing in common with each other.

That breadth means the team sports compete for attention inside their own
federation before they compete for it outside.

The visibility problem is the binding one. Underwater hockey and underwater
rugby are, by the account of anyone who plays them, tactically rich and
physically demanding, and a spectator at the side of a pool sees splashing.
Underwater cameras and live streaming have improved this and cannot solve it,
because a pool is not built for filming and the audience needs to understand a
game they have never seen played.

There is also a facility politics. Underwater rugby needs a pool at least three
and a half metres deep, and modern municipal pools are increasingly built
shallow. A sport whose venue requirement is being designed out of new
construction has a growth problem that no federation can legislate away.

Competitive **spearfishing** is the discipline that draws external objection.
It is governed by the same federation, it is restricted or banned in a number
of countries on conservation grounds, and it sits awkwardly beside an
organisation whose other work includes marine environmental programmes.
