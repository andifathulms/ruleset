# Three rules that changed the game

## The end of offside

By the 1990s hockey had a problem in midfield. Offside, on a small pitch with
a fast ball, crowded the middle of the game. It was also the hardest call an
umpire had to make. In 1998 the federation [removed
offside](#fih-1998-offside-abolished) completely.

Coaches said defending would become impossible. Within a season defences had
reorganised, and the change is now seen inside the sport as a success.
[Football](/sports/football/), [rugby](/sports/rugby/) and
[lacrosse](/sports/lacrosse/) all still have an offside rule. In hockey there
is no <Term id="offside">offside</Term>, and an attacker may wait anywhere.

## Less waiting

A <Term id="free-hit">free hit</Term> used to mean a long pause: play
stopped, players moved into place, and then someone passed. From 2009 the
[self-pass](#fih-2009-self-pass) let the player taking it simply carry on
with the ball.

In 2015 the match changed shape. Two halves of thirty-five minutes became
[four quarters of fifteen](#fih-2014-quarters), with the clock stopped for
penalty corners and goals. A team that was ahead could no longer win by
running down the clock. The quarters also gave three fixed breaks, which suit
television. [Basketball](/sports/basketball/) made the same change to its
international game in 2000.

## Eyes on the circle

Almost everything decisive happens in the circle, with a hard ball moving
fast and bodies between the umpire and the play. In 2011 hockey brought in
[video referral](#fih-2011-video-referral), and limited it to that area.
