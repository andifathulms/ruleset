# Equipment evolution

Hockey's decisive equipment change was not equipment at all. It was the ground.

From 1976 Olympic hockey was played on artificial turf. On grass the ball
bobbles and the game rewards close control, deft touch and the ability to
manage an unpredictable bounce. On a synthetic surface the ball runs true and
fast, which made possible techniques that had barely existed: the drag flick,
sustained reverse-stick play, and passing at speeds a grass field would not
carry.

Every skill in the sport moved. So did the balance of power. India and Pakistan
had dominated hockey for half a century with a game built for grass, and
neither has won an Olympic title since 1984. That is the clearest instance on
this site of a rule change — and it was a rule change, mandated by the
federation — redistributing a sport geographically.

The cost consequence was not legislated for and was probably not foreseen. A
water-based pitch is expensive to build and requires irrigation, and hockey
went from a sport playable on any field to one requiring a facility. Nations
that could not build them fell away, and the FIH has spent the last decade
approving surfaces that need less water partly to reverse it.

**Sticks** went from wood to composite, which raised drag-flick speeds
substantially and produced limits on stick shape and on the curvature of the
head. **Protective equipment** followed injury: mouthguards, shin pads, and
face masks permitted for penalty corner defence, where a defender runs at a
flick struck from fifteen metres.
