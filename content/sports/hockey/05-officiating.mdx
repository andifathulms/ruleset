# Officiating and technology

Hockey concentrates its officiating problem into two semicircles and points
almost all of its technology there.

Two umpires cover the pitch, one per half, which is fewer than the area
suggests — the reason it works is that a goal can only be scored from inside
the circle, so the decisions that matter are geographically constrained.

**Video referral**, from 2011, is limited to incidents in the circle. A team
refers, keeps the referral if successful, and the video umpire answers
questions of fact: was the ball played by an attacker's stick, did it touch a
foot, did it cross the line, was there a foul in the build-up to a goal. It is
the same structure cricket uses and it works for the same reason.

What the umpires kept is **obstruction**, and it is hockey's equivalent of
football's handball. Obstruction is placing your body between an opponent and
the ball without playing it, and judging it means deciding whether a player was
playing the ball or shielding it — a judgement about purpose, not position, and
therefore not automatable.

**Dangerous play** is the other, and it is harder here than in most sports
because the ball is solid and travels fast. Whether a raised ball was dangerous
depends on who was near it and how, which is a judgement made in a fraction of
a second.

The removal of offside in 1998 deserves recording as an officiating decision as
much as a sporting one. It deleted the sport's most disputed call by deleting
the rule, which is a solution available to any federation and taken by almost
none.
