# Governing body politics

Kickboxing's politics are a fragmentation problem with an Olympic recognition
sitting awkwardly on top of it.

WAKO was formed in 1976 and is the body the Olympic movement recognises —
provisionally from 2018, fully from 2021 — and which holds the World Games
place. It is a genuine international federation with a large membership and a
substantial championship programme.

It is not the only one. Kickboxing has several international sanctioning
organisations, each awarding its own world titles, alongside commercial
promotions running their own rulesets, where most of the sport's money and
visibility are. A fighter can be a world champion under a body the Olympic
movement does not acknowledge, and there is no mechanism by which those claims
get reconciled.

This site records the identical structure in powerlifting, where federations
split over drug testing and never reunified, and a related one in sumo and
muaythai, where a national professional institution sits outside the
international federation. Kickboxing has the powerlifting version: rivalry
between peers rather than a single dominant outsider.

The internal fragmentation compounds it. WAKO itself runs six competitive
disciplines that disagree about whether the legs may be kicked and whether
knees are legal. That is defensible as breadth and it makes the sport
extraordinarily hard to present as a single Olympic proposition, because a
programme place would have to choose one code — and choosing would disenfranchise
most of the federation's own athletes.

Recognition has been held since 2021. No programme place has followed.
