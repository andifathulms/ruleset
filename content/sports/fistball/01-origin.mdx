# Origin and invention

Fistball is old enough that its codification is the recent part.

Ball games in which a ball is struck with the arm and allowed to bounce are
attested across Europe for centuries. The Italian *pallone col bracciale*,
played with a spiked wooden cylinder worn on the forearm, was a major
professional spectator sport into the nineteenth century, with purpose-built
arenas. Antonio Scaino's treatise of 1555 is generally cited as the first
written rules of a ball game of this family, and the German *Faustball* that
this page describes belongs to the same lineage.

The modern sport was codified in the German gymnastics movement in the late
nineteenth century, with rules published in the 1890s. That inheritance is
visible in what the sport kept: it is played outdoors on grass, on a long
field, with a tape rather than a net, and with a ball that is allowed to land
— all of which are properties of a game that grew up outside rather than one
designed for a hall.

Emigration did the rest. German-speaking communities carried fistball to
Brazil, Argentina, Chile and what is now Namibia, where it took root and
developed for decades in isolation from the European game. By the time the
International Fistball Association was formed in 1960, there were two
continents playing under rules that had drifted apart, and the federation's
first job was to reconcile them.

It has never been on the Olympic programme. It is on the World Games one, where
a sport with clubs in a dozen countries and a century of continuous play is
exactly the intended constituency.
