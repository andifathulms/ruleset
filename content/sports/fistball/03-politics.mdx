# Governing body politics

The IFA's history is a reconciliation followed by a long, patient campaign.

The reconciliation came first. Fistball had developed on two continents in
isolation — German, Austrian and Swiss clubs on one side, and South American
clubs founded by German-speaking emigrants on the other — with rules that had
drifted apart over decades. The federation formed in 1960 to produce a single
rulebook, and South America has been a full competitive partner rather than a
periphery ever since. Brazil, Argentina and Chile all contest world titles.

The campaign is the ordinary one for a sport this size: recognition, a World
Games place, and no realistic Olympic prospect. The sport's honest position is
that it has strong clubs in about a dozen countries and very little presence
outside them, and that is not a case anybody can argue into a programme that
selects on global reach and broadcast value.

The 2000 move to rally scoring belongs to the politics rather than to the
sport. It was made for the same reason volleyball, table tennis and badminton
made theirs within a few years either side: a match of unpredictable length
cannot be scheduled, and a sport that cannot be scheduled cannot be televised
or fitted into a multi-sport programme. Fistball paid the same price they all
paid, in severed statistics, for a benefit that was always going to be smaller
because the sport is smaller.

Namibia is the federation's most unusual member, and the reason is colonial:
fistball arrived with German settlers and stayed after they no longer governed.
