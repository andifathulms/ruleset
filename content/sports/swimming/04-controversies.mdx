# Controversies that forced rule changes

**Rome, July 2009.** Forty-three world records in eight days, in an event that
normally produces a handful a year. The suits were banned within days by a
congress sitting at the same championships. The records stayed.

Fifteen years later the consequence is still on the page. The men's 200 m
freestyle record set by Paul Biedermann in Rome has not been beaten. The
post-2010 half of that chart is empty, and this site draws it empty rather than
extending the axis or joining the segments, because an empty panel is what
actually happened.

The 100 m freestyle is the more hopeful version of the same story: César Cielo's
46.91 from Rome stood until Pan Zhanle finally went under it in 2024. It took
fifteen years for swimming to get back to where its equipment had briefly taken
it.

**Seoul, 1988.** David Berkoff swam roughly the first third of the 100 m
backstroke underwater, because underwater dolphin kick is faster than backstroke
and the rules did not say how far you could go. FINA imposed a limit — ten
metres, later fifteen — citing the hypoxia risk that had ended underwater
breaststroke in 1957. It is also true that a backstroke race with no visible
swimmers is unusable on television. The sources do not separate the two motives,
so this site records the cause as disputed rather than choosing the flattering
one.

**The dolphin kick nobody could see.** Breaststroke swimmers were performing an
extra downward kick off the walls, underwater cameras showed it was near
universal, and surface officials could not detect it. In 2015 FINA legalised it.
A rule that cannot be enforced is enforced arbitrarily, and this is one of the
few cases where a federation said so by acting.
