# Where it is played

Swimming's map is a map of pools, and pools are a map of money and climate. This
is the most infrastructure-determined sport on the site after gymnastics, and
unlike gymnastics the barrier applies at the very first step: a person cannot
learn the sport at all without access to water and instruction.

Elite competition is concentrated in the United States, Australia, western
Europe, Japan and China. What those have in common is not a swimming culture in
any romantic sense; it is a density of 50-metre pools, a school or club system
that teaches children to swim as a matter of course, and enough coaching to
convert that into competition.

The consequence is a participation funnel unlike any other sport here. In
football the barrier is that everyone plays. In swimming the barrier is that
most of the world's population never gets the chance to, so the competitive
pool — the phrase is unavoidable — is small, well resourced and stable.

This intersects with the rules in one direct way. When polyurethane suits were
permitted between 2000 and 2009, a swimmer's equipment depended on their
national federation's supplier contract, and swimmers tied to a slower
manufacturer were competing on unequal terms through no choice of their own.
That was part of the federation's stated case for the 2010 ban: an equipment
arms race in a sport whose access is already unequal made the inequality visible
at the elite level, where it had previously only operated at the entry level.
