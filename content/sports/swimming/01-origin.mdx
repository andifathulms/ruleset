# Where it came from

Swimming became an organised sport in London. The National Swimming Society
was holding races there in the 1830s, and the Amateur Swimming Association
followed in 1869.

<Photo id="swimming-strand-1892" />

The international federation, FINA, was founded in 1908, the year London held
the Olympics. The swimmers at those Games raced in a pool dug into the infield
of the main stadium.

<Photos ids="swimming-london-1908 swimming-relay-1908" />

## Strokes are rules

<Term id="freestyle">Freestyle</Term> means any style. Swimmers chose the
crawl because it is the fastest, and no rule has ever required it. Duke
Kahanamoku won the 100 metres freestyle at Stockholm in 1912 swimming the
crawl, and that is what freestyle came to mean.

The other three strokes exist because a rulebook describes them. A swimmer is
doing <Term id="breaststroke">breaststroke</Term> because the rules say what
breaststroke is. So the history of these events repeats one pattern. A swimmer
finds a faster movement that the definition does not quite forbid, and the
federation has to decide whether to allow it.

<Photos ids="swimming-kahanamoku-1912 swimming-peaty-breaststroke" />

## How butterfly was born

In the 1930s some breaststroke swimmers began to bring both arms forward over
the water, where the old stroke brought them forward under it. The
breaststroke rules did not forbid this. It was faster.

The federation could have banned the new movement. In 1952 it made it a
separate event, and <Term id="butterfly">butterfly</Term> became the fourth
stroke.

<Photos ids="swimming-buyze-butterfly-1951 swimming-phelps-butterfly" />

The next loophole got the other answer. At the 1956 Games, breaststroke
swimmers covered large parts of the race underwater, which is faster than
swimming on the surface. Some were reported to have come up in distress. [From
1957 the head had to break the surface in every
stroke](#fina-1957-underwater-breaststroke).
