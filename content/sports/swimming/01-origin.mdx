# Origin and invention

Competitive swimming as a governed sport dates from the National Swimming
Society in London in the 1830s and the Amateur Swimming Association in 1869;
FINA was founded in 1908 to run it internationally. Almost everything
interesting about its rules comes from one structural fact: the strokes are
themselves rules.

Freestyle is a category, not a technique — it means any style, and the crawl won
because it is fastest. But breaststroke, backstroke and butterfly are defined by
regulation. A breaststroke swimmer is doing breaststroke because the rulebook
says what breaststroke is, and the entire history of those events is a history
of swimmers finding faster motions that the definition did not quite exclude,
and the federation deciding whether to exclude them.

Butterfly exists because of exactly this. It began in the 1930s as breaststroke
swum with an over-water recovery, which the breaststroke rules did not forbid.
Rather than ban it, FINA split it off into its own event in 1952. That is the
generous version of the move; the underwater breaststroke ban of 1957 and the
backstroke fifteen-metre limit are the restrictive version. Same mechanism,
different answer.
