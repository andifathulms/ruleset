# From cavalry training to an English rulebook

People have played ball games on horseback across Central Asia and Persia for
two thousand years. They were training for cavalry and a show for the court.
In Persia the game was called *chovgan*, and related games were played from
Constantinople to China and Japan.

## Manipur

The game that became modern polo survived in Manipur, in north-east India.
British tea planters and army officers met it there in the 1850s and took it
up. The Calcutta Polo Club was founded in 1862 and is the oldest polo club
still in existence.

<Photos ids="polo-manipur-1875 polo-sagol-kangjei" />

In 1874 the Hurlingham Polo Committee wrote the first English rules. [They
contain the rule the game still turns on](#polo-1874-line-of-the-ball): the
<Term id="line-of-the-ball">line of the ball</Term>, and
<Term id="right-of-way">right of way</Term> along it. In 1888 the United
States Polo Association [added the goal handicap](#polo-1888-goal-handicap),
which lets unequal teams play a fair match.

## Five Olympic Games

Polo [joined the Olympic programme in Paris in
1900](#polo-1900-olympic-entry). It was played at five Games: 1900, 1908,
1920, 1924 and 1936. Many of the teams were clubs, or mixed teams from more
than one country.

<Photos ids="polo-paris-1900 polo-london-1908" />

[It has not been played at the Games since
1936](#polo-1936-programme-removal), when five nations entered. No single
reason is recorded as the deciding one. Accounts mention the cost of moving
strings of horses between continents, the decline of the cavalry that had
supplied many of the players and horses, and the small number of countries
that could field a good team.

[An international federation was formed only in 1982](#polo-1982-fip),
forty-six years after the last Olympic match. [Tug of
war](/sports/tug-of-war/) went the same way round: out of the Games first, a
world federation later.
