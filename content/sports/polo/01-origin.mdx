# Origin and invention

Polo is very old, was codified by an occupying army, and left the Olympic
programme ninety years ago while remaining a substantial professional sport.

Mounted ball games are attested across Central Asia and Persia for two thousand
years, played as cavalry training and as court spectacle — *chovgan* in Persia,
and related games from Constantinople to China and Japan. The version that
became modern polo survived in Manipur, in north-east India, where British tea
planters and army officers encountered it in the 1850s.

They took it up, and they codified it. The Calcutta Polo Club was founded in
1862 and is the oldest surviving club in the world. The Hurlingham Polo
Committee wrote the first English rules in 1874, and those rules contain the
provision the sport still turns on: the **line of the ball**, and right of way
along it.

Polo entered the Olympic programme at Paris in 1900 and was contested at five
Games — 1900, 1908, 1920, 1924 and 1936 — often by clubs and
mixed-nationality teams rather than national selections. It has not been
contested since.

No single reason is recorded as decisive. The cost and logistics of moving
strings of horses across continents, the decline of the cavalry that supplied
much of the sport's manpower and mounts, and the small number of nations able
to field a competitive side are all cited.

An international federation followed in 1982 — forty-six years after the sport
left the programme, which is the same sequence tug of war followed, and for
recognisably similar reasons.
