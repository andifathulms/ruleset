# The horses come first

A polo player's most important equipment is alive. The animals are called
<Term id="pony">ponies</Term> by tradition, and they are full-sized horses.
Most are Thoroughbred crosses, bred to accelerate, to stop, and to stay calm
when ridden against another horse.

<Photo id="polo-pony" />

No horse can gallop for a whole match. So a player brings a
<Term id="string">string</Term> of several and changes between
<Term id="chukka">chukkas</Term>. A pony usually plays no more than two
chukkas, and not two in a row. A <Term id="high-goal">high-goal</Term> player
may ride six or eight horses in one game.

This is the difference from [equestrian](/sports/equestrian/) sport. There,
one horse is the registered competitor, with a passport and a name in the
result. In polo no single animal is the competitor. The string is widely
thought to account for most of a player's performance, and a string is
bought.

## Copies of one mare

In 2010 [clones were produced from an outstanding
mare](#polo-2010-cloned-ponies). They went on to play, and to win, at the top
of the Argentine game. In some matches one player rode several clones of the
same horse.

No rule allowed this and no rule forbade it. The rules of polo deal with
handicaps, right of way and equipment. They say nothing about where a horse
comes from. Supporters say a clone is a twin, that training still makes the
horse, and that cloning keeps great bloodlines alive. Critics say it turns a
living animal into a manufactured product and narrows the gene pool. No
governing body has ruled.

## Mallet, ball and helmet

The <Term id="mallet">mallet</Term> is a cane shaft with a hardwood head. The
player hits the ball with the side of the head. The ball was once made of
willow root and is now plastic, about eight centimetres across. Helmets are
compulsory. Knee guards and face guards are common.

Before a match the pony's tail is plaited and taped up, so that it cannot
catch a mallet or a rein.
