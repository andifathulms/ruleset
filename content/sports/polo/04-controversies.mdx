# Collisions, left hands and twenty-one ponies

## A rule about where a rider may be

Two horses of half a tonne each, galloping at the same ball, can collide. A
rule that punishes the contact afterwards comes too late. So the first English
rules [gave one rider priority before the horses
meet](#polo-1874-line-of-the-ball). The rider who follows the
<Term id="line-of-the-ball">line of the ball</Term> most closely has right of
way. <Term id="crossing">Crossing</Term> in front of that rider is a foul for
the danger it creates, even when nothing is hit.

<Photos ids="polo-line-of-ball polo-ride-off" />

Rights of way are familiar from [sailing](/sports/sailing/). Most ball games
rule on what a player may do to an opponent. Polo rules on where a player may
be.

## No left-handed play

[Since 1974 every player must hold the mallet in the right
hand](#polo-1974-right-handed-only). The reason is geometry. When a
left-handed and a right-handed player ride at the same ball on the same line,
they meet head-on, with horses and mallets coming together. When everyone
plays right-handed, two riders on the same line pass on the same side. The
rule is defended on safety alone.

## Florida, 2009

At a high-goal match in Florida in 2009, twenty-one ponies died. The cause was
a supplement that had been mixed incorrectly. It remains the sport's worst
incident, and it forced changes in how vets oversee the horses.

The wider argument about horses has grown since. After [modern
pentathlon](/sports/modern-pentathlon/) removed riding in 2022, the claim that
the public no longer simply accepts animals in sport reached polo too. Critics
point to how hard the ponies work, how many animals a professional career uses
up, and the use of drugs.

## The handicap

A player's <Term id="handicap">handicap</Term> is set by a committee. Results
do not set it. The rating decides how much a professional is worth to a team,
so it has a direct effect on earnings. Appeals and disputes about ratings
never stop.
