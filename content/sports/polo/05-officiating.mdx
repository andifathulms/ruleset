# Officiating

Two umpires on horseback, riding with the play, and a third official on the
line to break ties between them.

**The line of the ball** is what they are watching, and it is invisible. The
umpires must continuously establish where the ball's path runs, who is
following it most closely and therefore holds right of way, and whether an
opponent's approach crossed in front of them — all at a gallop, among eight
horses, on a field three hundred yards long.

That is a harder officiating problem than it sounds, because the offence is
**creating danger** rather than causing contact. A player penalised for crossing
the line may have touched nobody. The umpire is ruling on a collision that did
not happen and would have.

**The third man** sits on the sideline and is consulted when the two mounted
umpires disagree. Polo is one of very few sports on this site with a
built-in mechanism for officials being unable to agree, rather than a senior
official whose view prevails.

**Penalties** are graded by the danger of the foul rather than by its location,
and the most serious is an **automatic goal** — awarded outright, with no ball
struck. A penalty in polo can be the score rather than a chance at it, which
concentrates enormous weight on a judgement about an invisible line.

There is no video review in most competition, no instrumentation of the line,
and no obvious way to build either. The sport is officiated by two people on
horses, watching geometry.
