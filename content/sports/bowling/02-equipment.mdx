# Equipment evolution

Bowling's equipment history has two halves, and the second one is a surface
nobody can see.

**The ball.** Hard rubber gave way to polyester, polyester to urethane, and
urethane — around 1991 — to reactive resin. A reactive coverstock grips the dry
part of the lane far more aggressively than anything before it, hooks harder
into the pocket, and carries pins that older equipment left standing. Scores
rose immediately and permanently.

The regulations did not stop it, because they were written for a different
threat: weight, dimensions, hardness and balance were all specified, and a
coverstock's friction behaviour was not. Manufacturers innovated inside the
rules, which is the same story this site records for the composite softball
bat and the polyurethane swimsuit.

Modern competitive equipment goes further. A ball has an internal weight block
of a designed shape, drilled at a chosen angle relative to it, and a
coverstock that can be sanded or polished to alter friction. Bowlers carry
several and change during a block.

**The lane.** This is where bowling diverges from every other sport covered
here. The lane is dressed with oil in a pattern, applied by machine, invisible
to the bowler, which determines where the ball slides and where it grips.

When reactive equipment made scoring easy, the sport did not restrict the
balls. It changed the oil — adopting longer, flatter competition patterns with
far less margin for error than the forgiving house conditions recreational
bowling is played on.

That option existed because bowling is the only sport here whose field of play
is reapplied before every session and consumed during it.
