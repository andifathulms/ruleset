# Origin and invention

Knocking down pins is one of the oldest games there is, and ten-pin bowling is
a nineteenth-century American standardisation of it.

Pin games are attested across Europe for centuries in many forms — nine pins
arranged in a diamond, in a square, in a line — and they crossed to North
America with German and Dutch settlers. The move to ten pins in a triangle, on
a lane of fixed length, with a fixed ball specification, happened in the United
States through the nineteenth century, driven by the commercial bowling
industry that was building the lanes.

The scoring system dates from that period and has not changed since. Ten
frames, two deliveries each, a strike carrying the next two deliveries and a
spare the next one, with a maximum of exactly 300. It is a bounded perfect
score in a sport where a competitor can actually reach it — a distinction worth
holding on to, because gymnastics had a bounded perfect score too and abolished
it in 2006 for precisely that reason.

The international federation was founded in 1952 as the Fédération
Internationale des Quilleurs, bringing ten-pin and the European nine-pin games
under one body. It has since been World Bowling and is now the International
Bowling Federation.

Bowling was a demonstration sport at Seoul in 1988. The IOC discontinued the
demonstration category after 1992, bowling has bid for the programme repeatedly
since, and it has never been selected.

The sport's decisive modern event was neither institutional nor athletic. In
the early 1990s the ball changed, and everything the numbers meant changed with
it.
