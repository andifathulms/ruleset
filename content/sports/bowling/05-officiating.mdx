# Checking the lane, not the bowler

During a game of bowling almost nothing has to be judged. Pins fall or they
stay up, and a machine counts them. The one offence in play is crossing the
<Term id="foul-line">foul line</Term>. A sensor detects it, and the pins from
that delivery do not count.

<Photos ids="bowling-pinsetter bowling-foul-line-sign" />

The officials' real work comes before the game and around it.

**The oil.** Officials check that the pattern on the lane matches the one set
for the competition, and that fresh oil is laid on schedule. The pattern
decides how hard it is to score, so a mistake here changes the whole event.

**The ball.** Weight, size, balance, hardness and surface finish are all set
by rule. A surface can be changed by sanding or polishing, so officials check
what the surface is and when it was last altered.

**Who bowls where.** Each delivery moves the oil for the next bowler on that
pair of lanes, and a pattern wears unevenly. The order of the bowlers and the
lanes they use are therefore set by the organisers and not left to chance.
How patterns are designed and how often lanes are oiled again are both laid
down by rule, and both are argued about.

[Orienteering](/sports/orienteering/), where the result is an electronic
record, and [boules](/sports/boules/), where the players measure for
themselves, need as little judging during play.
