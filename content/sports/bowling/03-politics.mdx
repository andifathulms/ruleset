# Governing body politics

Bowling has the participation numbers every federation dreams of and none of
the standing that usually follows them.

Tens of millions of people bowl. Commercial centres exist in most cities on
earth. The sport was a demonstration event at Seoul in 1988, has bid for the
Olympic programme repeatedly, and has never been chosen — which puts it in an
unusual category: a genuinely global, genuinely popular sport that the Olympic
movement has consistently declined.

The reasons offered are identifiable and unflattering. Bowling is played
overwhelmingly as a commercial leisure activity rather than in clubs; its
professional structures are modest and concentrated in the United States and
East Asia; and it presents poorly as a spectacle, because the decisive variable
— the oil pattern — is invisible to a camera and to an audience.

That last point is worth dwelling on. A viewer watching elite bowling sees
people knocking down all the pins repeatedly and cannot see why it is hard.
Every other sport on this site can show a spectator its difficulty. This one
hides it in a coating.

The federation has also had a naming problem that reflects an identity one: the
Fédération Internationale des Quilleurs became World Bowling and then the
International Bowling Federation, while continuing to govern both ten-pin and
the European nine-pin games, which are a different sport played almost entirely
in Central Europe.

The most consequential decisions in bowling's recent history were not made by
the federation at all. They were made by ball manufacturers in the early 1990s,
and the sport has been responding to them ever since.
