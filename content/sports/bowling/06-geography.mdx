# Where it is played

Bowling's map is drawn by commercial property rather than by sporting
tradition.

**The United States** is where ten-pin was standardised, where the industry
that built the lanes grew, and where the professional structure remains. It has
by far the largest number of centres and bowlers.

**East and Southeast Asia** is the sport's other stronghold and in competitive
terms often its strongest: **South Korea**, **Japan**, **Chinese Taipei**,
**Singapore**, **Malaysia** and **Hong Kong** all produce world-class bowlers,
and Singapore and Malaysia in particular have won at world level far out of
proportion to their size. The sport arrived commercially and was then taken up
seriously.

**Europe** has ten-pin in Sweden, Finland, Denmark, England, Germany and the
Netherlands — and a completely separate pin-game culture in the centre of the
continent, where **nine-pin** in its several forms is a substantial club sport
in Germany, Austria, Croatia, Serbia and Slovenia, governed under the same
international umbrella and played by different people.

The distinctive feature of bowling's geography is that the facilities are
private businesses. A country gets bowling when someone decides there is money
in building centres, and it loses it when the property becomes worth more as
something else. This site records the same mechanism destroying racquetball in
the United States, where clubs converted courts to gym floor.

Bowling has been luckier, because a bowling centre cannot easily become
anything else — but the sport's presence in any country still depends on a
commercial calculation rather than on a federation.
