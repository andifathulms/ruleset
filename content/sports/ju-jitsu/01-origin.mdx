# Origin and invention

Ju-jitsu is the parent art, competing as a sport younger than its own child.

The Japanese systems that the name covers were battlefield methods for a
warrior who had lost or could not use a weapon: throwing, joint locking,
strangling, striking, and defending against all of those, often against an
armoured opponent. They were taught in schools — *ryū* — each with its own
curriculum, and there were many of them.

In the 1880s Jigoro Kano took a subset of that material, removed the techniques
that could not be practised at full resistance without injury, and built judo.
That subtraction is the whole point: judo works as a sport precisely because
Kano took out the parts that do not survive contact. It entered the Olympic
programme in 1964.

Competitive ju-jitsu is a much later reconstruction, assembled by European
federations from the 1970s, and it faced the opposite problem. If judo already
owns the throwing, and the grappling arts own the ground, what is a ju-jitsu
competition for?

Its answer is the Fighting system: a bout that passes through striking, then
throwing, then groundwork, in that order, so that no phase can be avoided. And
alongside it the Duo system, in which the techniques too dangerous to apply to
a resisting opponent are performed cooperatively with a team-mate and judged.

The Ju-Jitsu International Federation took its present form in 1998. It governs
a name that also belongs to classical Japanese schools that do not compete at
all, and to a Brazilian grappling sport far larger than itself.
