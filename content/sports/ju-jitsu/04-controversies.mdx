# Controversies that forced rule changes

**What a ju-jitsu competition is for.** The founding problem, and the Fighting
system's three phases are the answer. If a bout could be settled at one range,
the sport would collapse into judo, into kickboxing, or into submission
grappling — all of which already exist and are better established. Requiring
the bout to pass through all three ranges is a rule written to preserve the
sport's reason to exist.

**Techniques that cannot be contested.** A large part of the art is
self-defence against attacks that cannot be applied at full force to a
resisting opponent without injuring them. The Duo system is the response:
perform them cooperatively with a team-mate and have judges mark the realism
and control. It makes a substantial part of the curriculum contestable and it
imports every difficulty that judged sports have.

**Grappling's growth outside the federation.** Submission grappling expanded
enormously through the 2000s and 2010s under Brazilian and independent bodies.
The JJIF added ne-waza in 2015, which was a response to a constituency it did
not govern rather than to anything inside its own sport.

**Gi and no-gi.** Contesting both means maintaining two technical codes, and
which is the real ne-waza is argued exactly as it is in the Brazilian sport.

**The name.** Not a rule change and the sport's largest problem: a federation
whose sport shares its name with several unrelated activities cannot easily
tell anyone what it is, and this affects recruitment, recognition and every
conversation about inclusion.
