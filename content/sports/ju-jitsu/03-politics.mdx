# Governing body politics

The JJIF governs a word, and the word is not its own.

"Ju-jitsu", "jujutsu" and "jiu-jitsu" are used by classical Japanese schools
that do not compete, by hundreds of independent organisations with their own
gradings, by a very large Brazilian grappling sport with its own federations
and its own culture, and by this federation, which runs a codified competitive
code and holds international recognition.

That is a harder governance position than any other combat sport covered here.
Judo, taekwondo, wrestling, boxing and karate each have contested politics, but
in each case the international federation governs something recognisably
central to the activity. The JJIF's competitive sport is one claimant among
several to a name whose largest constituency is somewhere else.

The Ne-waza discipline, added in 2015, is the federation's most consequential
strategic move. Submission grappling had grown into one of the largest combat
activities in the world almost entirely outside the JJIF, and adding a ne-waza
competition brought a version of it inside. It did not bring the Brazilian
federations, their athletes or their events, which continue as before.

Institutionally, the sport has done well by the standards of its size: a World
Games place, an appearance at the 2018 Asian Games, and international
recognition. What it does not have is an Olympic place, and the obstacle is
partly that the IOC already has judo — a sport created by removing most of
ju-jitsu, admitted sixty years ago, and occupying the slot its parent would
need.
