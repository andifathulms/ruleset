# Governing body politics

Kabaddi's governance has two problems: too many federations, and one country.

**Too many federations.** The International Kabaddi Federation was formed in
2004, and other international bodies have since claimed the same mandate.
Competing organisations have held competing world cups. For a sport whose
strongest claim to institutional seriousness is its Asian Games place, having
more than one body assert the right to govern it is a substantial obstacle, and
it is one this site has recorded elsewhere in powerlifting for entirely
different reasons.

**One country.** India has dominated the sport completely — every men's Asian
Games title from 1990 for decades — and the professional league that now
effectively sets the rules is Indian. Iran has emerged as a genuine rival, and
Bangladesh, Pakistan, South Korea and Japan compete, but the sport's centre of
gravity has never moved.

The 2014 league is the most important political fact of kabaddi's modern
history and it is not a federation at all. It made the sport a career, raised
the standard, produced the raid clock and the do-or-die raid, and made an
Indian domestic competition the venue where kabaddi's laws are decided in
practice. International rulemaking has followed a broadcaster.

That is not unique — this site records the same pattern in the broadcast
scoring wave that caught badminton, volleyball, table tennis and fistball —
but it is unusually direct. In those sports a federation changed its rules
because broadcast pressure existed. In kabaddi, a league wrote rules and the
federations adopted them.
