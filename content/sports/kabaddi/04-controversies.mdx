# Who makes the rules

## Raids that went nowhere

The cant limits a <Term id="raid">raid</Term>, but a fit raider can chant for
a long time. A defence under no pressure can simply wait. Matches could turn
into raiders stepping in and stepping out again, with nothing happening.

The professional league that began in India in 2014 answered with two rules
at once. A raid would last no more than thirty seconds. And after two
<Term id="empty-raid">empty raids</Term> in a row, a team's third raid would
be do-or-die: score, or lose the raider. Both rules [moved into the
international game](#kabaddi-2014-raid-clock). They do the same job as the
shot clock in [basketball](/sports/basketball/): they stop a team from
stalling.

## A league writing the laws

Those changes were written for a television league and only afterwards
adopted by the federations. In other sports, a federation changed its own
rules under pressure from broadcasters. In kabaddi, a domestic competition
wrote the rules and the federations followed. Whether a commercial league
should set a sport's laws is still argued.

<Photo id="kabaddi-bengaluru-bulls" />

The league made kabaddi a career. It raised the standard of play, and it is
where the sport's full-time athletes are.

## Two bodies, two world cups

The International Kabaddi Federation was formed in 2004 to govern the game.
Other international bodies have since [claimed the same
role](#kabaddi-2004-ikf), and rival organisations have held rival world cups.
For a sport whose strongest claim to standing is its Asian Games place, that
is a serious problem.
