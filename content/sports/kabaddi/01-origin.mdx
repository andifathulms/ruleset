# Origin and invention

Kabaddi is very old, very widespread, and has almost no documented invention —
which is what you would expect of a game that requires nothing.

Contests in which one person enters a group's territory, tries to touch them
and escape are recorded across South Asia for centuries, under a range of
regional names: kabaddi, hu-tu-tu, ha-du-du, chedugudu, sadugudu. The
breath-holding element appears throughout, and the chant is the mechanism that
makes it verifiable — you cannot see whether someone is holding their breath,
but you can hear whether they have stopped chanting.

Codification came in India in the 1920s, when written rules were framed so
that clubs and regions could compete on common terms. That work turned a family
of folk games into one sport with a court, a team size and a scoring system,
and it is the version described on this page.

Institutional recognition followed slowly. Kabaddi appeared as a demonstration
sport at the Asian Games and became a full medal event at Beijing in 1990,
after which India won the men's title at every edition for decades. The
International Kabaddi Federation was formed in 2004.

The most consequential thing that has happened to the sport is much more
recent. A professional league launched in India in 2014, became one of the
country's largest television properties, and rewrote the rules — a raid clock
and a do-or-die raid — which were then adopted into the international game. A
folk sport that had been governed by federations for ninety years had its laws
changed by a broadcaster's format requirement, and this site records that as
the same kind of event as badminton's rally scoring.
