# Governing body politics

The IOF is a well-run federation with a structural problem it has never
solved: its sport is nearly impossible to watch.

An orienteering race happens across several square kilometres of forest, out
of sight, with competitors starting at intervals and taking different routes.
For most of its history the spectator experience was a start line, a finish
line, and no information about what happened in between.

Almost everything the federation has done competitively in the last thirty
years is a response to that. **Sprint orienteering** was created to be
watchable: fifteen minutes, in a town or a campus, where spectators and cameras
can see the runners and the route choices are visible on a street map.
**Relays** are mass-start with shuffled courses, so there is a race rather than
a procession. **GPS tracking** — banned as a navigation aid, mandatory as a
broadcast one — puts every competitor on a live map for the audience while
telling the runner nothing.

That has not produced Olympic inclusion, and orienteering has never been on
any Olympic programme, summer or winter. Ski orienteering's absence from the
Winter Games is the more striking of the two, given how few sports contest
those.

The federation's other quiet achievement is trail orienteering, a discipline
judged on accuracy rather than speed, designed so that wheelchair users and
walking competitors compete directly against each other. It is the only
discipline covered anywhere on this site that removes physical speed from a
sport on purpose, and it is a genuine piece of institutional imagination from a
federation with very little money.
