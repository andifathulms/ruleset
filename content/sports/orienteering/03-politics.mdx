# A race nobody could watch

An orienteering race happens across several square kilometres of forest, out
of sight. Runners start at intervals and take different routes. For most of
the sport's history a spectator saw a start line and a finish line and learned
nothing about what happened between them.

Almost everything the federation has changed in competition over the last
thirty years answers that problem.

## Three answers

The <Term id="sprint">sprint</Term> was created to be watched. It lasts about
fifteen minutes and is run in a town or on a campus, where spectators and
cameras can see the runners and follow their choices on a street map.

Relays use a <Term id="mass-start">mass start</Term>, with the course
combinations shuffled between teams, so the crowd sees a race.

GPS tracking is the third. A runner may not use GPS to navigate, but must
carry a tracker for the broadcast. It puts every runner on a live map for the
audience and tells the runner nothing. At the Jukola night relay in Porvoo,
Finland, in 2023, spectators stood watching the leaders move across a big
screen while the runners themselves were out in the dark.

None of this has brought a place at the Olympic Games. Orienteering has never
been on an Olympic programme, summer or winter, and ski orienteering has never
been part of a Winter Games.

## Trail orienteering

The federation also runs a discipline judged on accuracy, with speed taken
out. Trail orienteering was designed so that wheelchair users and walking
competitors compete directly against each other.

<Photo id="orienteering-trail-o" />

It draws the best from the rest of the sport too. Thierry Gueorgiou, many
times a world champion in foot orienteering, has taken part in a trail
orienteering event in Kristianstad.
