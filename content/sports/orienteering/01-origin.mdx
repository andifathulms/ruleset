# A military exercise that escaped

Orienteering began as army training. Through the nineteenth century,
Scandinavian armies taught their officers to cross unfamiliar country with a
map and a compass. The Swedish word *orientering* comes from that training,
and the sport took its vocabulary from it.

Civilians began to compete in Norway and Sweden in the 1890s. The sport grew
through the early twentieth century as part of Scandinavian outdoor life, the
same movement that produced widespread cross-country skiing and the legal
right to roam. In Sweden, Norway and Finland it became a sport for very large
numbers of people, and their clubs still lead the international results.

## One federation, one map

The sport had grown separately across Scandinavia and into central Europe.
Each country had its own way of drawing maps and describing
<Term id="control">controls</Term>, and that made international competition
impractical. [The International Orienteering Federation was formed in
Copenhagen in 1961](#orienteering-1961-iof) by ten national federations. Its
early work was to make things standard: control descriptions, start
intervals, and above all the map.

[An international map specification followed in
1969](#orienteering-map-specification). With it, a Finn can read a Portuguese
map, because the symbols mean the same thing everywhere. A world championship
can be held on ground that none of the runners has seen.

## Two rules that shaped the sport

A runner who knew a forest could beat a better navigator who did not, so
results measured where people lived. [The answer was to close the area
beforehand](#orienteering-unseen-terrain), publish nothing, and hand over the
map at the start. The <Term id="embargo">embargo</Term> now reads like the
definition of the sport.

The second rule is about length. Ground varies so much that a fixed distance
gave wildly different races. [Courses are planned to a target winning
time](#orienteering-winning-time), so ground of any difficulty can hold the
same kind of event.

Together the two rules mean that a time in orienteering says nothing outside
the race that produced it. The sport chose fairness across terrain, and gave
up any chance of a record to get it.
