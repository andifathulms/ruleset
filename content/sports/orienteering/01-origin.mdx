# Origin and invention

Orienteering is a military exercise that escaped.

Scandinavian armies trained officers in land navigation through the nineteenth
century — moving across unfamiliar country with a map and a compass, which is a
skill an army needs and a civilian population mostly does not. The Swedish word
*orientering* comes from that context, and the sport carried the vocabulary out
with it.

Civilian competition followed in Norway and Sweden in the 1890s, and the sport
grew through the early twentieth century as an expression of Scandinavian
outdoor culture — the same movement that produced widespread cross-country
skiing and the legal right to roam. It became a mass participation sport in
Sweden, Norway and Finland in a way it has never quite become anywhere else,
with club structures that still dominate the international results.

The International Orienteering Federation was formed in Copenhagen in 1961 by
ten national federations, and its early work was standardisation: control
descriptions, start intervals, and above all the map. An international map
specification followed later in the decade, and it is the reason a world
championship can be held in terrain that none of the competitors has seen — a
Finn can read a Portuguese map because the symbols mean the same thing.

Two further decisions gave the sport the shape described on this site. Courses
are planned to a target winning time rather than to a distance, so terrain of
any difficulty can host a comparable event. And competition areas are embargoed
beforehand, so nobody can have seen the ground.

Between them, those rules make orienteering a timed sport in which times mean
nothing outside the race that produced them. That was a choice, made in favour
of fairness across terrain, and it cost the sport any possibility of a record.
