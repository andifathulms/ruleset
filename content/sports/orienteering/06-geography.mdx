# Where it is played

Orienteering belongs to Scandinavia first. Sweden, Norway, Finland, Denmark
and Switzerland have dominated it for its whole history.

The reasons are cultural and legal as much as athletic. These countries have
a strong outdoor tradition, a right of public access to land, forests that
are mapped and easy to walk in, and clubs tied to schools. Sweden's largest
events draw as many people as most sports would call a national championship.

Beyond that core, the Czech Republic, Russia, France, Britain, Austria and
Latvia are competitive. The sport has a small but real presence in Australia,
New Zealand, Japan, Brazil and China.

## The ground itself

Terrain affects results more here than in most sports. Nordic forest, the
karst of central Europe, British moorland, Iberian granite and the streets of
a sprint each read differently and reward different skills. A runner who is
excellent in one may be ordinary in another.

World championships held again and again in Nordic forest produced Nordic
winners. The championship terrain is now rotated on purpose. The rotation is
a political matter, because it helps decide who is likely to win.

## The map comes first

The real barrier is the map. Somebody has to walk the ground and draw it to
the specification, and that takes skilled volunteers. A country without
orienteering usually has people willing to run in a forest. It has no mapped
forest for them to run in.
