# Where it is played

Scandinavia, and then everyone else at a distance.

Sweden, Norway, Finland, Denmark and Switzerland dominate the sport
completely and have for its whole history. The reasons are cultural and legal
as much as athletic: a widespread outdoor tradition, an established right of
public access to land, forests that are mapped and walkable, and club
structures embedded in schools. Sweden's largest events draw participant
numbers that most sports on this site would regard as a national championship.

Beyond that core, the Czech Republic, Russia, France, Britain, Austria and
Latvia are competitive, and the sport has a genuine but small presence in
Australia, New Zealand, Japan, Brazil and China.

Terrain is itself a geographic factor in results, more than in any other sport
covered here. Nordic forest, Central European karst, British moorland, Iberian
granite and urban sprint terrain each read differently and reward different
skills, and a competitor excellent in one may be ordinary in another. World
championship terrain is rotated deliberately for this reason, and the rotation
is a live political matter because it decides who is likely to win.

The sport's access barrier is unusual and worth naming: it needs a surveyed
map. Somebody has to walk the ground and draw it to specification, which takes
skilled volunteer labour and is the real reason orienteering is dense where it
is dense. A country does not lack orienteering because nobody wants to run in a
forest; it lacks it because nobody has mapped one.
