# Equipment evolution

One piece of equipment matters and the competitor does not own it.

**The map** is orienteering's technology, and it has been standardised more
carefully than the equipment of most sports on this site. The international
specification fixes a symbol set and a colour logic: white is forest you can
run through, green is undergrowth graded by how much it slows you down, yellow
is open ground, brown is landform, blue is water, and black is rock and
man-made features. It is drawn to magnetic north rather than true north, so a
compass needle and the sheet of paper agree without the competitor doing
arithmetic while running.

The refinement of that specification is the sport's real equipment history.
Early maps were repurposed national survey sheets at coarse scales with no
information about runnability. Purpose-surveyed orienteering maps, made at
1:15,000 or 1:10,000 by people walking the terrain, turned a rough approximation
into an instrument precise enough that a control can be placed on a boulder and
found.

**The compass** evolved from a plate compass to the thumb compass — a small
unit strapped to the thumb and held against the map, so the map, the needle and
the terrain are all in one line of sight and the runner never stops.

**Punching** moved from a needle punch and a paper card to an electronic chip
in the 1990s. That changed the sport's self-knowledge more than its
competition: every split between every pair of controls is now recorded and
published, and the sport can see exactly where a race was lost.

What is banned is as important. No GPS device may be used for navigation, no
altimeter, no pre-marked route. The sport has kept its instrument analogue
deliberately, because the thing it measures would otherwise cease to exist.
