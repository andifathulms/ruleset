# The map, the compass and the chip

The most important piece of equipment in orienteering belongs to the
organiser. It is the map.

## The map

The international specification fixes one set of symbols and one use of
colour. White is forest a person can run through. Green is undergrowth, in
shades that show how much it slows a runner. Yellow is open ground, brown is
the shape of the land, blue is water, and black is rock and things people
have built. The map is drawn to magnetic north, so the compass needle and the
paper agree and the runner does no arithmetic.

<Photos ids="orienteering-forest-map orienteering-sprint-map" />

Early maps were national survey sheets at coarse scales, borrowed for the
purpose. They said nothing about how easily the ground could be run. Maps
surveyed specially for the sport came later, made at 1:15,000 or 1:10,000 by
people walking the terrain. They are exact enough that a control can be
placed on a single boulder and found.

## The compass

The plate compass gave way in racing to the thumb compass. It is a small unit
strapped to the thumb and held against the map. Map, needle and terrain sit
in one line of sight, and the runner never has to stop.

## The chip

For most of the sport's history a runner proved a visit to a control with a
needle punch and a paper card. A card could be damaged or lost. A runner could
be disqualified for a mark that was there but could not be read, with no way
to prove otherwise.

[Electronic punching replaced the card in the
1990s](#orienteering-electronic-punching), and that kind of dispute
disappeared. <Term id="punching">Punching</Term> a chip also records the time
at every control. Every <Term id="split">split</Term> is now published, and
the sport can see exactly where a race was lost.

## What is kept out

No GPS device may be used to navigate, and no route may be marked in advance.
The sport has kept its tools simple on purpose. With electronic help, the
skill it measures would disappear.
