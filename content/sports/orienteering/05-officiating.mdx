# The chip is the referee

There are almost no officials out in the terrain. A runner is alone and
unobserved for most of the race.

<Photo id="orienteering-control-flag" />

The proof of what the runner did is the chip. It holds an electronic record
of which <Term id="control">controls</Term> were visited, in what order and
at what time. That record does the work of a referee, and it is complete.

A few judgements are left to people.

## Was the course valid?

A control may be put in the wrong place, or be missing, or fail to match its
description. The organiser must then decide whether that
<Term id="leg">leg</Term>, or the whole course, is void. It is the heaviest
decision in the sport. It is made after the race, sometimes after a world
championship, and from time to time it cancels results.

## Fair play

Interval starts exist so that each runner navigates alone. A runner who
catches another and simply follows has turned the event into a running race.
<Term id="following">Following</Term> on purpose is an offence, and so is
entering closed terrain before the race. Nobody sees either happen. They are
judged from evidence: splits that match too closely, a GPS trace, or an
admission.

Relays solve the problem by design. Teams get different combinations of the
same controls, so following another runner does not pay.

## Out of bounds

Areas marked <Term id="out-of-bounds">out of bounds</Term> on the map are
absolute. They are usually private land, crops or dangerous ground. A runner
who crosses one is disqualified even if it gained nothing.

Compare [tug of war](/sports/tug-of-war/), which is judged entirely by eye
because it has nothing to measure by instrument. Orienteering is judged
almost entirely by instrument because nobody is there to look.
