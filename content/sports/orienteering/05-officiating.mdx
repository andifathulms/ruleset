# Officiating

There are almost no officials in the terrain, and that is the point.

An orienteering race is self-administered by design. A competitor runs alone,
unobserved, for most of the event. The proof of what they did is the chip: an
electronic record of which controls were visited, in what order, and at what
time. That record is the referee, and it is complete.

What remains for humans is a small set of judgements:

**Course validity.** If a control is mis-sited, missing, or placed so that the
description does not match the terrain, the organiser must decide whether the
leg — or the whole course — is void. This is the most consequential decision in
the sport and it is made after the fact, sometimes after a world championship
has been run, and it periodically voids results.

**Fair play.** Deliberate following, and entering embargoed terrain
beforehand, are both offences judged on evidence rather than observation —
splits that match too closely, or a GPS trace, or an admission.

**Out of bounds.** Areas marked uncrossable on the map — private land, crops,
dangerous ground — are absolute, and a competitor who crosses one is
disqualified even where it gained them nothing.

The interesting comparison is with tug of war, elsewhere on this site, which is
adjudicated entirely by eye because there is nothing to instrument.
Orienteering is adjudicated almost entirely by instrument because there is
nobody there to look — and the residual human calls it has are all about
whether the competition itself was properly constructed, rather than about what
any competitor did.
