# From seamanship to racing

Sailing became a sport once sail was no longer the only way to cross water.
Yacht racing was organised in the Netherlands and Britain in the seventeenth
and eighteenth centuries. A private sailing yacht was then a display of
wealth, and the early racers were the people who could afford one.

<Photos ids="sailing-dutch-states-yacht sailing-english-yacht-rotterdam" />

Clubs came next. The Cumberland Fleet was founded in 1775 for racing on the
Thames. In 1851 the schooner America won the cup that still carries her name.

## Rules borrowed from the sea

The core of the rule book was never written for a game. Sailors at sea already
had conventions for avoiding collisions. A boat on
<Term id="port-tack">port tack</Term> gives way to one on
<Term id="starboard-tack">starboard tack</Term>. A
<Term id="windward">windward</Term> boat keeps clear of a leeward one. A boat
behind keeps clear of the boat ahead. Racing took these over almost whole.
That is why the book is called the Racing Rules of Sailing, and why most of it
is about what boats must do to avoid hitting each other.

## Places, not times

The wind drives the boat, and the wind changes across a course, across a race
and across a day. A time would mostly record the weather. So a
<Term id="regatta">regatta</Term>
[counts finishing places over a series of races](#sailing-series-scoring) and
lets each boat [drop its worst result](#sailing-discard). The system admits
that any single race holds a good deal of luck, and it has hardly changed in a
century.

<Photo id="sailing-star-fleet-2012" />

The international federation was founded in 1907 as the International Yacht
Racing Union. Sailing has been on every Olympic programme since 1900 except
that of 1904.
