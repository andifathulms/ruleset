# Origin and invention

Sailing became a sport when it stopped being the only way to cross water, and
its rules came from seamanship rather than from any game.

Yacht racing organised in Britain and the Netherlands in the seventeenth and
eighteenth centuries, and the institutions that still matter — the Royal Yacht
Squadron, the America's Cup from 1851 — date from the period when a private
sailing vessel was a statement of wealth. That is the sport's founding social
fact and it has never entirely left.

The rulebook's origin is different and more interesting. The **right-of-way
rules** — port gives way to starboard, windward keeps clear of leeward, a boat
astern keeps clear of one ahead — are not competition rules. They are
collision-avoidance conventions from ordinary seafaring, adopted more or less
whole into racing. That is why the document is called the Racing Rules of
Sailing rather than the laws of a game, and why most of its length concerns
what boats must do to avoid hitting each other.

The scoring system is the other inheritance, and it was right from the start.
Sailing has never recorded times, because with the wind as propulsion a time
records the weather. It counts finishing positions across a series and discards
the worst, which is a century-old acknowledgement that any single race contains
a great deal of luck.

The IYRU was founded in 1907. Sailing has been on every Olympic programme since
1900, apart from 1904.
