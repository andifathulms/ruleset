# Officiating and technology

Sailing officiates a collision-avoidance code across several square kilometres
of open water, on boats moving in different directions at speed, and until
recently it did most of it by asking the competitors afterwards.

**The protest** is the sport's traditional instrument and it is genuinely
unusual. A competitor who believes another broke a rule hails a protest during
the race and files it afterwards; a jury hears both parties, examines evidence
and decides, sometimes hours later. Results are provisional until the protests
are resolved.

That system has one striking feature. A boat that knows it has broken a rule
can **exonerate itself** by taking a penalty turn on the water immediately — a
competitor serving their own sentence without an official involved. Nothing
else on this site works that way. Golf's self-reporting is the nearest
relative, and this site would note that both are sports with strong amateur
traditions and codes of honour attached.

**On-water umpiring**, extended from 2017, moved high-level fleet racing
towards immediate decisions: umpires follow the fleet, signal penalties as
incidents happen, and the result is final when the racing ends.

Technology has arrived mostly for the audience. GPS tracking, on-board cameras
and real-time graphics have made a sport that was almost impossible to watch
legible for the first time, and the same tracking data now supports umpiring
decisions.

What has not been automated, and probably cannot be, is right of way. Whether a
boat had established an overlap before entering a mark zone is a judgement
about the geometry of two moving objects over time — the same class of question
as fencing's priority, and just as resistant.
