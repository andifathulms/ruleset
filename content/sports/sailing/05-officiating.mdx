# Who enforces the rules

For most of the sport's history, the sailors enforced the rules on each other.

## The protest

A race covers a wide stretch of open water, with boats moving in different
directions. No official can see everything. So a sailor who believes another
boat broke a rule calls out a <Term id="protest">protest</Term> during the
race and files it afterwards. A <Term id="jury">jury</Term> hears both sides,
looks at the evidence and decides, sometimes hours later. Until then the
results are provisional.

## The penalty turn

A boat that knows it has broken a rule does not have to wait for a protest. It
can clear itself at once with a
<Term id="penalty-turn">penalty turn</Term>. The sailor serves the penalty
with no official involved. The nearest thing in another sport is the
[golf](/sports/golf/) player who reports a penalty against himself or herself.

## Umpires on the water

Protest hearings were impossible for spectators to follow, and they left the
result open long after the racing ended. From 2017
[umpiring on the water was extended](#sailing-2017-protest-and-umpiring) in
Olympic and other top-level racing. Umpires follow the fleet in their own
boats and signal a penalty as soon as they see a breach.

Technology came mainly for the audience. GPS tracking, cameras on the boats
and live graphics made the sport possible to follow on a screen. The tracking
data now helps the umpires as well.

<Term id="right-of-way">Right of way</Term> itself still needs a human eye.
Whether one boat had drawn level with another before they reached a mark is a
judgement about two moving objects over time. No instrument settles it.
[Fencing](/sports/fencing/) has a problem of the same kind with its priority
rule.
