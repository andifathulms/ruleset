# Identical boats, replaced every few years

In an Olympic sailing event every boat is the same. A few years later the boat
itself may be gone from the Games.

## One design

Early yacht racing was partly a contest between designers, and the fastest
yacht often belonged to the richest owner. Olympic classes are
<Term id="one-design">one-design</Term>. Every boat in an event is built to
one specification, so the race is between sailors.

## Choosing the classes

The federation decides which classes are sailed at the Games, and it reviews
the list for every Olympic cycle. The choice has to balance several things:
how the sport is really sailed, what smaller nations can afford, the Olympic
limits on athlete numbers and the balance of men and women, and the interests
of the builders who make the boats. The votes are lobbied hard, because
medals, careers and businesses depend on them.

[The list for 2024 changed a great deal](#sailing-2024-equipment-cycle).
<Term id="foiling">Foiling</Term> boats and boards came in. The windsurfing
class was replaced by a foiling board. The Finn, a dinghy sailed at every
Games from 1952 to 2020, went out.

<Photos ids="sailing-nacra-foiling sailing-iqfoil" />

The federation's reason was that foiling had already changed sailing outside
the Games, and the Olympic programme was falling behind.

## The cost to sailors

A sailor spends about ten years learning one class: the boat, how it handles,
and the tactics that suit it. Then the event can disappear.
[Judo](/sports/judo/) removed techniques and
[weightlifting](/sports/weightlifting/) redrew its weight categories, and
athletes in both sports took those changes as shocks. In sailing the same
thing happens on a schedule.
