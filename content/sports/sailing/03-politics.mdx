# Governing body politics

World Sailing's central and recurring political act is choosing the Olympic
classes, and it is contested every cycle because medals, careers and
manufacturers all depend on it.

Each decision balances several things that pull apart: keeping the programme
relevant to how the sport is actually sailed, keeping costs low enough that
smaller nations can compete, meeting IOC requirements on gender balance and
athlete numbers, and dealing with class associations and builders who have
commercial interests in the outcome. The votes are political in the ordinary
sense and are lobbied accordingly.

The 2024 cycle is the clearest example. Foiling equipment came in, the Finn
went out, and the windsurfing class was replaced. Each of those decisions ended
or reshaped careers, and each was defended on grounds of relevance and
attracting a younger audience.

The other long-running tension is between the sport as raced and the sport as
broadcast. Sailing's scoring system exists to average out luck across a series;
the 2008 medal race concentrates the medals in one short race close to shore,
worth double points, because a regatta that ends with the gold already decided
is not televisable. Both positions are coherent and they contradict each other,
and the federation has chosen broadcast every time the question has come up.

Underneath everything is cost. Sailing is expensive, its Olympic participation
skews wealthy, and the federation's stated intent to widen the sport runs
against equipment decisions that are frequently expensive.
