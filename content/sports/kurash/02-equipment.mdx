# Equipment evolution

Three garments, one of which is deliberately not a gripping surface.

The **yakhtak** is the jacket, and it is the sport's apparatus rather than its
clothing. Grips on the jacket are most of kurash's technical vocabulary, in the
way they are in judo and in the belt-wrestling traditions the sport sits among.
Competitors wear blue and green.

The **belbog** is the belt, tied at the waist, and it is a legal gripping point.

The **ishton** are the trousers, and they are the interesting item, because
gripping them is prohibited. Nothing below the waist may be held at all — not
the trousers, not the legs. So a competitor is wearing a garment that is
explicitly outside the contest, which is unusual: in most jacket sports
everything you wear is fair game.

That prohibition is the sport's founding constraint expressed as equipment
regulation. Judo has arrived at a version of it by legislation, banning leg
grabs from 2013 after trialling the restriction from 2010, and its judogi
trousers remain grippable in ways kurash's are not.

There is no protective equipment. There is no footwear. There is nothing else.

The mat is the only other item the rules govern, and it matters less here than
in the neighbouring sports, because nothing happens on it: a bout that reaches
the ground is stopped and restarted standing.
