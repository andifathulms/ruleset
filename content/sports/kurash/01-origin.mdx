# Origin and invention

Kurash is an old wrestling tradition with a very new rulebook, and the gap
between those two facts is the sport's whole story.

Jacket wrestling contested standing, at festivals and celebrations, is attested
across Central Asia for a very long time, and Uzbek accounts place kurash's
antiquity in the thousands of years. Related traditions run right across the
region and the Caucasus under many names, and most of them share the same
shape: two people in jackets and belts, trying to throw each other, standing.

What is precisely dated is the modern sport. In 1998, Komil Yusupov produced a
codified international rulebook — weight categories, a scoring system of
**halal**, **chala** and **tanbeh**, bout lengths, a matted contest area — and
the International Kurash Association was formed in Tashkent.

The codification made a specific choice and stuck to it. There is no
groundwork, no submission and no grabbing below the waist. The sport is
entirely the standing phase, which is what the tradition had always been, and
the rulebook declined to import the ground phase that international jacket
wrestling had acquired elsewhere.

This was a national project. Uzbekistan, independent for seven years, set out
to make a domestic tradition into an international sport with a federation and
a calendar — a smaller version of what Chinese state authorities did with
wushu from the 1950s, conducted much faster.

It worked quickly. Kurash reached the Asian Games in 2018, twenty years after
the rulebook was written, which is a shorter path from codification to a
continental programme than any other sport covered here.
