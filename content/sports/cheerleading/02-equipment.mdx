# No helmet, no harness

A <Term id="flyer">flyer</Term> is thrown several metres into the air and
caught by other people, and she wears no protective equipment at all. There
is no helmet, no harness and no padding. The only mat is the competition
floor.

<Photo id="cheer-cornell-liberty" />

Other sports that produce head and spine injuries turned to equipment:
helmets in [cycling](/sports/cycling/), headguards in amateur
[boxing](/sports/boxing/), face masks in [softball](/sports/softball/).
Cheerleading turned to rules. [Its safety code](#cheer-safety-code) limits
how high a pyramid may be, which forms of
<Term id="toss">basket toss</Term> are allowed, and which
<Term id="released-stunt">released stunts</Term> each age may try. It also
says how many <Term id="spotter">spotters</Term> must stand by.

<Photo id="cheer-pyramid-2009" />

## Why the rules are so strict

Falls during stunting have caused spinal and head injuries. Studies in the
United States have repeatedly placed the rate of catastrophic injury among
the highest of any school or college activity. Most of the rulebook is an
answer to that record.

The limits get tighter as the athletes get younger. A skill that is legal
for a senior international team is banned for a school team.

## What equipment there is

The floor is the one piece of hardware the rules control closely. Routines
are performed on a matted floor, and at senior level the floor is sprung. The
rule is there for safety, because a large part of the injury record is about
what people landed on.

The megaphone was the first equipment of cheerleading. It survives as a
sideline prop and has no part in a competitive routine. Poms are used in one
category of <Term id="performance-cheer">Performance Cheer</Term> and nowhere
else. Uniforms are checked for modesty and for safety: nothing that can
catch, and no jewellery.
