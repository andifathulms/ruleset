# From the sideline to the mat

Cheerleading was invented to be an audience. In 1898, by the usual account, a
student at the University of Minnesota stood in front of a football crowd and
directed it to shout in unison. American college football drew large crowds,
and the universities wanted them organised and loud. Students were appointed
to lead them.

The cheerleaders were men, and they stayed men for four decades. It was a
university pursuit with some status, and several American presidents led
cheers as students. The work was megaphones and organised chanting. Nothing
about it was gymnastic.

<Photo id="cheer-usc-yell-leaders" />

Two things changed it.

The first was the Second World War. The men left, women took over the squads,
and when the war ended the squads did not go back. Within about ten years
cheerleading went from almost entirely male to overwhelmingly female.

The second was the skills. Through the second half of the twentieth century
squads added <Term id="tumbling">tumbling</Term>, then partner
<Term id="stunt">stunts</Term>, then <Term id="pyramid">pyramids</Term> and
tosses. A competitive circuit grew up around these skills in the United
States. It was largely commercial, and event companies ran it where another
sport would have a federation.

[The International Cheer Union was formed in 2004](#cheer-2004-icu) to give
the sport common rules across countries. It received provisional recognition
from the International Olympic Committee in 2016 and [full recognition in
2021](#cheer-2021-ioc-recognition).

The sport that the federation governs replaced the activity of 1898. The
sideline job still exists, and school and college squads still do it.
