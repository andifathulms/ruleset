# Governing body politics

The ICU governs a sport whose competitive infrastructure it does not own.

The federation was formed in 2004, obtained provisional IOC recognition in
2016 and full recognition in 2021, and holds a World Games place. On the usual
measures that is a successful two decades.

But the overwhelming majority of competitive cheerleading happens in the United
States, on a commercial circuit of all-star club competitions, and that circuit
— along with much of the apparel supply, the camp system and the event calendar
— is concentrated to an unusual degree in a single company. Antitrust
litigation has followed, brought by gyms and by families, alleging that the
concentration allowed prices to be set across events, uniforms and training.

This site records governance problems in several sports: rival federations in
powerlifting and kickboxing, a national professional body outside the
international one in sumo and muaythai, and two bodies claiming dragon boat.
Cheerleading's is a fourth kind and the only one of its type here — the sport's
competitive structure is a commercial property, and the international
federation is a separate thing that sits above it.

The recognition question follows from that. An IOC assessment of a sport
normally asks about national federations, athlete pathways and governance
independence. Cheerleading's answer in its largest market involves a company.

There is one further political dimension the federation handles carefully.
Cheerleading in the United States is entangled with legal questions about
whether it counts as a sport for the purposes of gender-equity requirements in
education funding — a question courts have answered inconsistently, and one
where being recognised as a sport has consequences far outside competition.
