# How dancing got a right answer

People danced in pairs everywhere long before anyone competed at it. What a
competition needed was an agreed technique, and Britain wrote one between the
two world wars.

In the 1920s British teaching bodies set down the figures, the footwork, the
timing and the holds of the ballroom dances. The same technique could now be
taught in every school in the same way. It could also be judged, because a
step now had a correct form.

<Photo id="dancesport-kensington-1921" />

The dances settled into two lists of five. The
<Term id="standard">Standard</Term> programme is waltz, tango, Viennese waltz,
slow foxtrot and quickstep. The <Term id="latin">Latin</Term> programme is
samba, cha-cha-cha, rumba, paso doble and jive. A
<Term id="couple">couple</Term> dances all five, one after another, and the
result is taken across all of them.

<Photos ids="dancesport-standard-2010 dancesport-latin-2012" />

Competitive ballroom spread from Britain across Europe, and each national
body used its own list of dances and its own judging habits. [An
international council for amateur dancers was formed in
1957](#dancesport-1957-federation) and fixed the two programmes. They have
not changed since.

<Photo id="dancesport-kiel-1963" />

The council became the International DanceSport Federation in 1990 and the
World DanceSport Federation in 2011. The word "sport" in the name was chosen
with a purpose. Through the 1990s the federation rebuilt itself to meet
Olympic criteria, and [in 1997 the International Olympic Committee recognised
it](#dancesport-1997-ioc-recognition).
