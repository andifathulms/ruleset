# Origin and invention

Competitive ballroom is the codification of a social activity, and the
codifying was done in Britain between the wars.

Social partner dancing existed everywhere; what did not exist was an agreed
technique. In the 1920s British teaching bodies standardised the figures,
footwork, timing and holds of the ballroom dances, producing a written
technique that could be taught identically and — crucially — judged. That work
turned dancing into something with a right answer, which is the precondition for
a competition.

The competitive programmes settled into two lists of five. **Standard**: waltz,
tango, Viennese waltz, slow foxtrot, quickstep. **Latin**: samba, cha-cha-cha,
rumba, paso doble, jive. Both are danced in sequence and a couple's result is
an aggregate across all five.

International governance came in 1957 with the International Council of Amateur
Dancers, which became the International DanceSport Federation in 1990 and the
World DanceSport Federation in 2011. The renaming was not cosmetic: the word
"sport" was doing institutional work, and the federation reorganised itself
around Olympic criteria — anti-doping compliance, eligibility rules, a
recognisable governance structure — through the 1990s.

IOC recognition arrived in 1997 and has now been held for nearly three decades
without producing a place on the programme.

What did arrive was breaking, in 2024, under this federation's governance,
because the WDSF had the recognition breaking needed and breaking had the
audience the IOC wanted. It was dropped for 2028. The federation's newest
discipline reached the Games and its founding ones never have.
