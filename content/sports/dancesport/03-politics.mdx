# Thirty years at the Olympic door

The World DanceSport Federation has been recognised by the International
Olympic Committee since 1997. Ballroom and Latin dancing have never been on an
Olympic programme.

The federation did a great deal to get there. It put "sport" in its name. It
adopted anti-doping rules, eligibility rules and a structure like that of
other international federations. It replaced its judging with [a system of
marks that a spectator could follow](#dancesport-absolute-judging). None of
this produced a place at the Games.

## Breaking gets the place

In 2017 the federation took on the governing of
[breaking](/sports/breaking/), a street dance with no recognised federation
of its own. The organisers of the Paris Games wanted a young, urban sport
that would work on television. Breaking needed a recognised federation to be
admitted under, and the ballroom federation had the recognition.

[Breaking was danced at the 2024 Olympic Games](#dancesport-breaking-olympic)
and was not included for 2028. Parts of the breaking community had disputed
whether a ballroom body should govern their dance at all.

So the federation's recognition did win an Olympic place, for a dance it did
not create and had governed for three years. The dances it has governed since
1957 are still outside.

## Two federations, one set of dances

The World DanceSport Federation is the amateur side of ballroom. A separate
body, the World Dance Council, governs the professional side of the same
dances. Couples, judges and events are divided between the two, and for a long
time neither has recognised the other. There have been disputes over who may
compete where, rival world championships, and couples who had to choose a
side. No world title is accepted by everyone.
