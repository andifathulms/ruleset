# Governing body politics

Two schisms and one long, unsuccessful campaign — and then a strange
consolation prize.

**The amateur–professional split.** Competitive ballroom is governed by more
than one body. The WDSF is the amateur-side, IOC-recognised federation; the
World Dance Council governs the professional side of the same dances. Couples,
adjudicators and events are distributed between them, and the two have a long
history of mutual non-recognition. It is the same shape of problem this site
records in powerlifting and, differently, in sumo: a sport whose best
competitors and whose Olympic-facing federation are not necessarily in the same
place.

**The Olympic campaign.** The WDSF has held IOC recognition since 1997 and
restructured itself thoroughly in pursuit of inclusion — the rename around
"sport", anti-doping compliance, governance reform, and a new judging system
designed to produce results a spectator could read. None of it worked.
Dancesport has never been on an Olympic programme.

**Breaking.** In 2017 the WDSF took on governance of breaking, which had no
recognised federation of its own. Breaking was then contested at the 2024
Olympics and dropped for 2028. The federation's asset in that transaction was
its recognition — the thing thirty years of campaigning had produced — and it
converted into a place for a discipline the federation did not create and had
governed for three years, rather than for the ones it had governed since 1957.

Parts of the breaking community disputed whether a ballroom body should govern
the form at all; that argument is recorded on this site's breaking pages. From
dancesport's side, the notable fact is that its recognition turned out to be
worth something to somebody else.
