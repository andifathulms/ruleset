# Governing body politics

Wushu's institutional history is a single, sustained, well-funded Olympic
campaign that has not worked.

The IWUF was founded in Beijing in 1990 and secured Asian Games inclusion the
same year. China's support has been consistent and substantial — the federation
is headquartered there, the sport's elite standard is set by the Chinese
specialist school system, and the campaign for Olympic inclusion has been
pursued with state backing for three decades.

The 2008 arrangement is the most remarkable thing in it. Wushu was not accepted
onto the Beijing Olympic programme. What happened instead was that a wushu
tournament was staged in Beijing during the Games, with IOC sanction, outside
the programme, with medals that were not Olympic medals. It was not a
demonstration sport either — the IOC had discontinued that category after 1992.
No other sport covered here has been present at a Games in that way.

The federation presented it as a step towards inclusion. Eighteen years later
there is no Olympic place. Wushu appeared at the 2014 Youth Olympic Games in
Nanjing and remains on continental programmes and at the World Games.

The obstacles are identifiable. The sport is two disciplines with nothing in
common, which makes it hard to present as one entry. Its elite standard is set
overwhelmingly by one country. And its judged discipline arrived at
declared-difficulty scoring in 2003 at exactly the moment gymnastics was
demonstrating how contested that model becomes.
