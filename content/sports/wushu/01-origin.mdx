# A sport designed by a state

*Wushu* is the Chinese word for martial arts, and for most of history it named
hundreds of different things. There were regional styles, family lines and
temple traditions. They had no shared course of teaching and no shared
vocabulary, and there was no way to hold a competition between them.

<Photos ids="wushu-shaolin-bamboo wushu-shaolin-performance" />

## The 1950s

From the 1950s the Chinese state sports authorities set out to make one
national sport from all of this. [They chose techniques, wrote standard
routines, defined events and set competition
rules](#wushu-state-codification). The result could be taught the same way
across the country, judged the same way, and later exported. It is now called
contemporary wushu, and its judged discipline is
<Term id="taolu">taolu</Term>.

<Photo id="wushu-jian-pair" />

The argument about it began at once and has not ended. People from the
traditional lines say the state chose what looks impressive, and made a
performance that has lost its fighting content. The federation says that
standard routines are what made it possible to teach and contest the sport at
all.

## A fight is added

Standard routines gave wushu a judged discipline and no contest between two
people. <Term id="sanda">Sanda</Term> was [developed from the 1970s to supply
one](#wushu-sanda-platform). It is fought on a raised platform taken from the
traditional <Term id="lei-tai">lei tai</Term> challenge fights, and forcing an
opponent off the edge scores.

## Going abroad

[The International Wushu Federation was founded in Beijing in
1990](#wushu-1990-iwuf). Wushu was contested at the Asian Games in the same
year, at an edition China hosted, and international championships followed
from 1991.
