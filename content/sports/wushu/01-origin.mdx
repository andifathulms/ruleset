# Origin and invention

Wushu is the only sport on this site that was designed by a government.

The raw material was vast: Chinese martial arts existed in hundreds of regional
styles, family lineages and temple traditions, with no common curriculum, no
shared vocabulary and no mechanism for holding a competition between them.
*Wushu* is simply the Chinese word for martial arts, and it covered all of it.

From the 1950s, Chinese state sports authorities set out to produce a national
sport from that material. They selected techniques, wrote standardised
routines, defined events, and established competition rules — creating a
discipline that could be taught identically across the country, judged
consistently, and eventually exported. The result is what is now called
contemporary wushu, and it is a deliberate construction rather than a
codification of an existing practice.

That origin is the source of the sport's longest-running argument.
Practitioners of traditional lineages have held since that standardisation
selected for what looks impressive and produced a performance discipline
detached from the martial content it came from. The federation's position is
that standardisation is what made the sport teachable and contestable at all.
Both are recorded here.

The full-contact discipline came later. **Sanda** was developed from the 1970s
to give the sport an actual contest, fought on a raised platform drawn from the
traditional *lei tai*, where forcing an opponent off the edge scores.

The International Wushu Federation was founded in Beijing in 1990, the same
year wushu entered the Asian Games. Its Olympic history is the strangest on
this site: a wushu tournament was held inside the 2008 Beijing Games, with IOC
sanction, without being an Olympic event.
