# From exercise class to competition

Gymnastics began as physical education, and for most of its early life nobody
competed at it. There were two rival systems. Friedrich Jahn's German
*Turnen* was built around <Term id="apparatus">apparatus</Term>, and he opened
his first open-air gymnastics field in the Hasenheide park in Berlin. Per
Henrik Ling's Swedish system was built around free exercise and posture,
performed by a whole class in unison.

<Photos ids="gymnastics-hasenheide-1903 gymnastics-stockholm-1910" />

The apparatus tradition won, and the sport is still organised by apparatus.
The federation now called the FIG was founded in 1881, before the modern
Olympic movement existed, and it is the oldest international federation of
any sport. Gymnastics on the parallel bars was part of the first modern
Olympic programme, in Athens in 1896.

<Photo id="gymnastics-athens-1896" />

The classroom left one idea behind. The point of an exercise was to perform a
prescribed movement correctly. A race or a throw has a different aim, and so
gymnastics grew a different kind of rulebook. The
<Term id="code-of-points">Code of Points</Term> lists the movements and says
what a score is. In a sport like this, changing the rules can change the
meaning of the number, and [in 2006 it did](#fig-2006-open-ended-code).

Until 1997 the classroom idea was also half of every competition. Each gymnast performed a
<Term id="compulsory">compulsory exercise</Term>, the same set
<Term id="routine">routine</Term> as everyone else, next to a routine of their
own. Competitions ran to two full sessions for each phase, and the
compulsories were thought unwatchable for a general audience. [They were
removed in 1997](#fig-1997-compulsories-abolished). A championship became
about half as long, and it lost the part in which every gymnast was compared
on the same material.
