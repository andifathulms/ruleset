# Equipment evolution

Gymnastics apparatus changed continuously and mostly quietly: fibreglass rails
on the uneven bars, sprung floors, foam landing surfaces of increasing depth,
and the raising and separating of the uneven bars from a shape a gymnast could
straddle to one they swing between. Each of these enabled skills that were
previously impossible, and each therefore fed the difficulty inflation that
eventually broke the 10.0 scale.

One apparatus change is a rule change with a date and a cause. In 2001 the
vaulting horse was replaced by the vaulting table — wider, longer, sloped, with
a much larger contact area. The horse was a piece of nineteenth-century
gymnasium furniture that gymnasts were now hitting at sprint speed while
rotating, and the surface a hand could safely land on was the width of two
hands.

The trigger was Sydney in 2000, where the horse stood five centimetres too low
for an entire rotation of the women's competition. Gymnasts who had trained a
vault to a known height found the apparatus was not where their body expected it
to be. Some crashed, some withdrew, and the review that followed asked why the
apparatus had so little tolerance for error in the first place. The answer was
the contact area, and the table was the response.

Every vault difficulty value was reissued for the new apparatus. This is quietly
a comparability break of its own, and a smaller one than 2006 only because
nobody was keeping a vault-score series across it.
