# The apparatus

Most changes to the <Term id="apparatus">apparatus</Term> came quietly. The
rails of the uneven bars became fibreglass. Floors were given springs. Foam
landing surfaces grew deeper. The uneven bars were raised and moved apart,
from a shape a gymnast could straddle to one she swings between. Each change
made new skills possible, and harder skills are what in the end broke the
scale that stopped at 10.0.

<Photos ids="gymnastics-bars-2018 gymnastics-foam-pit" />

Grips, chalk and tape are allowed and are hardly regulated at all. The
apparatus is what the federation specifies closely.

## Sydney, 2000: the horse that stood too low

Until 2001 the <Term id="vault">vault</Term> was performed over a vaulting
horse. It was a piece of nineteenth-century gymnasium furniture, long, narrow
and rounded. Gymnasts had come to hit it at sprint speed while rotating, and the surface a hand could safely land on was the width of two
hands.

<Photos ids="gymnastics-horse-1904 gymnastics-horse-1959" />

At the 2000 Games in Sydney the horse was set five centimetres too low for a
whole rotation of the women's competition before anyone found the error. The
gymnasts had trained their vaults to a known height, and the horse was not
where their bodies expected it. Some crashed. Some withdrew.

The review that followed asked why the apparatus forgave so little. Its answer
was the small contact area, which left no margin for a hand placed slightly
wrong. [In 2001 the horse was replaced by the vaulting
table](#fig-2001-vaulting-table), which is wider, longer and sloped, with a
much larger surface to push from.

<Photo id="gymnastics-vault-table-2018" />

Every vault was given a new difficulty value for the new apparatus, so a vault
score from before 2001 and one from after it were not earned on the same
thing.
