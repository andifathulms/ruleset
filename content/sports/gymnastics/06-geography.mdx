# Where it is played

Gymnastics has the smallest and most concentrated elite population of any sport
on this site, and it is concentrated by infrastructure rather than by climate or
culture. The sport requires a specialised hall, apparatus, deep matting, a foam
pit and a qualified coach. Countries with those in quantity produce gymnasts and
countries without them do not, almost without exception.

The historical map is a map of state sports systems: the Soviet Union, Romania,
East Germany, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, and later China. Centrally funded
programmes could identify children at six or seven and put them in residential
training, which is exactly what a sport with this specialisation age rewards.
The United States and Japan reached the same place by other routes — a dense
private club system in one case, and sustained federation investment in the
other.

Two rule changes on this page are legible as responses to that concentration.

**The minimum age**, raised to fifteen in 1981 and sixteen in 1997, was a
response to a recruitment pattern that the strongest programmes had established
and that others copied. It is a safeguarding rule, and it is also a rule about
which training systems the sport is willing to reward.

**Judging blocs** — the accusation that judges from allied nations score each
other's gymnasts favourably — is a geography problem before it is an integrity
problem. Anonymised panels, discarded outliers and statistical monitoring of
judges all exist because the pool of qualified international judges is drawn
from the same small set of countries as the gymnasts.
