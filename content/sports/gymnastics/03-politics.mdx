# Governing body politics

The FIG's defining structural problem is that it must certify subjectivity. A
measured federation ratifies a number produced by an instrument; a judged
federation ratifies a number produced by people, and its entire credibility
rests on those people being seen to be impartial. Every significant governance
episode in the sport's modern history is a version of that problem.

Judging blocs — the recurring accusation that judges from allied nations score
each other's gymnasts favourably — are older than television. The federation's
countermeasures have escalated correspondingly: discarding the highest and
lowest scores, then anonymising panels, then separating difficulty from
execution in 2006, then statistical evaluation of judges against the panel
median. Each measure is an attempt to make a judgement auditable without making
it mechanical.

The 2006 Code is the largest of those measures, and it is telling that the
federation presented it as a competitive reform — the sport had run out of room
above 10.0 — rather than as an integrity reform. Both descriptions are true and
the entry records both, because which one drove the decision is exactly the kind
of question the sources cannot settle.

The other long-running politics is age. The minimum went from fourteen to
fifteen in 1981 and to sixteen in 1997, against a background of federations
misstating ages, and it remains contested: raising it protects children and also
removes competitors at the point where the sport's demands and a small body most
favourably coincide.
