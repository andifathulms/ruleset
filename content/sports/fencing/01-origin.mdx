# Three weapons from the duel

Fencing comes from the duel, and each of its three weapons keeps a different
part of it.

<Photo id="fencing-angelo-1765" />

## Épée

The <Term id="epee">épée</Term> is the duelling sword itself. The whole body
is the target, because a real duel had no forbidden places to hit. There is no
<Term id="right-of-way">right of way</Term>, because a duel had no referee to
award it. If both men were hit, both were hit.

## Foil

The <Term id="foil">foil</Term> was the practice weapon. Its target is the
torso, where a killing thrust was aimed. Its right-of-way rule began as a
lesson for pupils: do not throw yourself onto the opponent's point in order to
score, because with real swords that kills you.

<Photos ids="fencing-epee-2013 fencing-foil-2013" />

## Sabre

The <Term id="sabre">sabre</Term> is the cavalry weapon. The target is
everything above the waist, because that is what a rider could reach on
another rider. It scores with the edge because a sabre cuts.

<Photo id="fencing-sabre-2013" />

So fencing's three sets of rules are three real situations kept alive as
regulations. They were inherited from fighting and were never designed as a
game.

## The Olympic sport

Fencing was at the first modern Olympic Games in Athens in 1896, and it has
been on every programme since. Only five sports can say that. Its
international federation, the Fédération Internationale d'Escrime, was founded
in 1913.

<Photo id="fencing-athens-1896" />

There are twelve possible Olympic events: three weapons, individual and team,
for women and for men. For decades the Games could not fit all twelve inside
the number of athletes fencing was allowed. Some team events were left out of
each Games, in turn, and which ones were dropped was argued over every time.
The programme has now settled at the full twelve.
