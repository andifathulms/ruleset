# Origin and invention

Fencing descends from the duel, and the three weapons are three different
answers to the question of what a duel was for.

**Épée** is the duelling sword itself. The whole body is target because a real
duel had no target restrictions, and there is no priority because in a real
duel there was no referee to award one — if both parties hit, both were hit.
Épée's rules are the closest thing on this site to a sport that simply
describes what would have happened.

**Foil** was the practice weapon. Its restricted target — the torso — reflects
where a killing thrust was aimed, and its right-of-way convention is a training
rule: do not fling yourself onto your opponent's point to score, because in the
real thing that kills you. Foil's priority rules are an artefact of a sport
teaching survival.

**Sabre** is the cavalry weapon. Target is everything above the waist because
that is what a mounted opponent could reach, and it scores with the edge
because a sabre cuts.

So fencing's three sets of rules are three historical situations preserved in
regulation, which is unusual — most sports here have rules that were designed
for a game. Fencing's were inherited from something that was not.

The FIE was founded in 1913 and fencing has been on every modern Olympic
programme. It is one of only five sports of which that is true.
