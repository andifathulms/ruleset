# Governing body politics

Fencing's governance has been shaped by one recurring accusation — that
judging favoured particular nations — and its answer has been to remove judging
wherever it could.

Before electrification, a bout was decided by a president and four side judges,
and panels were assembled from the nations present. The suspicion that this
produced national bias was constant and occasionally proven; the most notorious
case is the 1976 Games, where a competitor in modern pentathlon's fencing phase
was found to have rigged his épée to register hits that had not landed, which
is a fraud only possible in a sport where the apparatus is the arbiter.

Electrification was the federation's structural answer, and it worked for the
question it addressed. What it could not address was priority, and priority
disputes remain the sport's political sore point — a single referee decides,
appeals are limited, and the decision often determines a medal.

The other long-running argument is the programme. Fencing has twelve possible
Olympic events and for decades could not run them all within its athlete quota,
so team events rotated out by weapon and sex each Games. Which team event was
dropped was a genuine political question with medals attached, resolved
differently each cycle until the full twelve were accommodated.

The weapons themselves also compete for institutional attention. They have
different constituencies, different national strengths and different views on
what fencing is, and every timing or rule revision to one of them is argued
about by the other two.
