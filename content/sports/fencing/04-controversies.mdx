# A death, a rigged sword and a setting

## Rome, 1982

Vladimir Smirnov was the reigning Olympic foil champion. During a bout at the
world championships in Rome, his opponent's blade broke. The broken end went
through Smirnov's mask and into his brain. He died nine days later.

The federation changed the equipment. [Within two years it had new standards
for blades, masks and uniforms](#fie-1984-blade-and-mask-standards). Blades
must be made of <Term id="maraging-steel">maraging steel</Term>, which breaks
without leaving a long sharp fragment. Masks and uniforms must resist a
puncture of a stated force.

<Photo id="fencing-mask-2013" />

The standards have held. There has been no comparable death at the top level
since.

## The rigged épée, 1976

Once the apparatus decides the hit, the apparatus can be cheated. At the 1976
Games, a competitor in the fencing phase of
[modern pentathlon](/sports/modern-pentathlon/) was found to have rigged his
épée so that it registered hits that had not landed.

This is also why fencing has many rules about equipment that fails testing. A
faulty body wire can change a result.

## Sabre, 2005

By the early 2000s almost every sabre fencer attacked in the same way: a long,
committed run from the starting line. Bouts were repetitive and hard to
follow. [The federation changed the timing inside the scoring
machine](#fie-2005-sabre-timing). The old attack stopped paying, and bouts
became shorter and faster within a season.

## Masks you could see through

Transparent masks were introduced so that spectators could see the fencers'
faces. They failed in testing and were withdrawn. It is a rare case of a
change made for television being reversed for safety.
