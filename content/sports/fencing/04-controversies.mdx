# Controversies that forced rule changes

**The judges.** Not an incident but a century of them. Visual judging of a
touch delivered in a fraction of a second, by a panel drawn from competing
nations, produced results that could not be verified and were routinely
disputed. The federation's response was the most thorough automation of any
sport of its era, delivered in three instalments as the engineering allowed.

**Vladimir Smirnov, Rome, 1982.** The reigning Olympic foil champion was killed
when an opponent's blade broke during a bout and the fragment passed through
his mask and into his brain. He died nine days later.

The federation's response was material rather than procedural: maraging steel
for blades, because it fails without producing a long sharp fragment;
puncture-resistance standards for masks and uniforms. Fencing is one of very
few sports on this site whose safety rules were written after a death in
competition, and the standards have held — there has been no comparable
fatality at elite level since.

**Sabre's monoculture.** By the early 2000s sabre had converged on a single
dominant approach, a long committed attack from the line, which made bouts
repetitive and difficult for anyone to follow. The federation revised the
timing constants in the scoring apparatus in 2005 and the weapon changed within
a season.

This is the entry this site finds most striking in fencing. No rule of conduct
was amended. The sport was reshaped by changing a number that governs when a
machine stops listening.

**Transparent masks.** Introduced to make the sport more watchable, withdrawn
after failures in testing. A rare case of a broadcast-motivated equipment change
reversed on safety grounds.
