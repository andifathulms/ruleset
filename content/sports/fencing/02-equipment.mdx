# Equipment evolution

Fencing is the sport on this site where equipment changes did the most and are
discussed the least, because they arrived slowly and one weapon at a time.

**Electrification** is the whole story. Before it, a hit existed when a
president and four judges agreed they had seen one. Afterwards it existed when
a circuit closed. Épée in 1936, foil in 1957, sabre in 1989 — fifty-two years
to convert one sport, weapon by weapon, in order of engineering difficulty.

Épée was first because it is the simplest: the whole body is target, so the
apparatus only has to register a point landing anywhere. Foil needed a
conductive jacket so the machine could tell torso from arm. Sabre needed a
jacket covering everything above the waist and had to register contact anywhere
along an edge, which is why it waited another thirty-two years.

**Maraging steel** is the safety change, and it has a date and a name. In 1982
Vladimir Smirnov, the reigning Olympic foil champion, was killed at the world
championships when an opponent's blade broke and the fragment went through his
mask. Within two years the federation required blade steel that fails without
producing a long sharp fragment, and set puncture-resistance standards for
masks and uniforms.

**The scoring machine's timing constants** are the strangest equipment rule
here. How long after one fencer's hit the apparatus will still register the
other's is a number inside a box, and changing it in sabre in 2005 changed the
weapon's dominant style within a season. A federation reshaped a sport by
adjusting a setting, without amending a single rule about what fencers may do.
