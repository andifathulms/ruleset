# Officiating and technology

Fencing is the best illustration on this site of a boundary that keeps
appearing: a machine can answer *what happened*, and cannot answer *what it
meant*.

**What happened** was solved in three stages. Épée in 1936, foil in 1957,
sabre in 1989. A hit now registers because a circuit closed, and the four side
judges who used to decide it no longer exist. That is a category of official
abolished by technology, which puts fencing alongside tennis and its line
judges — except fencing did it seventy years earlier and did not stop being a
judged sport, because of what was left.

**What it meant** is priority, and it has never been mechanised. In foil and
sabre, when both fencers land, the point goes to whoever had right of way —
whoever's attack was correctly formed and initiated first. That is a judgement
about the structure of an action rather than about a position at an instant,
which is exactly the class of question that football's handball law asks and
that no camera has ever settled anywhere on this site.

So a modern fencing referee has one job, and it is the hard one. Video appeal
exists in a limited form and reviews the referee's priority call, which means
the review is a second opinion rather than a measurement.

The apparatus itself has become an officiating instrument in a subtler way. Its
timing constants decide which exchanges register as one hit and which as two,
and the federation sets them. That is a rule with no words, applied by
equipment, and revising it in sabre in 2005 did more to that weapon than any
rule change in its history.
