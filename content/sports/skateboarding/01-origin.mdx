# Origin and invention

Skateboarding was invented by surfers who wanted something to do when the sea
was flat, and it has never entirely stopped being that.

It emerged in coastal California in the 1950s and 1960s — boards with roller
skate wheels, ridden as sidewalk surfing — and the language it still uses comes
from surfing: carving, lines, style. The two sports arrived at the Olympic
programme together in 2020, which is fitting.

Two technical developments made the modern sport. **Urethane wheels**, in the
early 1970s, gave grip and speed that clay and steel could not, and made
serious riding possible. **The ollie** — Alan Gelfand's no-hands aerial, adapted
to flat ground by Rodney Mullen — let a rider lift the board without touching
it, which is the foundation of essentially every street trick that followed.

What skateboarding never developed was a governing structure. It had contests,
professionals, magazines, video parts and a strong internal sense of what was
good, transmitted through peers rather than through coaching. Judging was
holistic and done by skaters.

That absence is why the Olympic project required an external federation, and
why the sport's scoring looks the way it does. World Skate — previously the
roller sports federation — took skateboarding on in 2017, and the sport arrived
at the Games with contests it already ran and a scale it had never written down.
