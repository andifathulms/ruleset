# Governing body politics

This is the sharpest case on the site of a federation's recognition benefiting
a discipline that had just arrived.

The roller federation was founded in 1924 and spent nearly a century governing
artistic skating, speed skating and rink hockey without ever placing any of
them on an Olympic programme. Rink hockey reached a demonstration event at
Barcelona in 1992, in a country where it is a mainstream professional sport,
and the IOC discontinued the demonstration category afterwards.

The obstacle is easy to state and hard to answer: every roller discipline has
an ice counterpart already on the Winter programme. Artistic roller skating is
figure skating on wheels. Roller speed is speed skating without ice. A
selection committee asked to add either can reasonably say the Games already
contests that sport.

In 2017 the federation merged with the international skateboarding body to
become World Skate. Skateboarding needed a recognised federation to be
admitted; the roller body had recognition. Skateboarding was on the Tokyo
programme in 2021.

So the asset built over ninety years by artistic skaters, speed skaters and
rink hockey players delivered an Olympic place to a discipline that had joined
four years earlier — and it did so precisely because skateboarding was the one
discipline with no ice equivalent to be compared against.

This site records the same transaction under a ballroom federation, whose
recognition delivered the 2024 Games to breaking. In both cases the objection
from the incoming discipline's own community — that a federation from another
tradition should not govern it — was loud. The objection from the federation's
older disciplines has been quieter and is, on these facts, better founded.
