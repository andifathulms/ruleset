# Judges, clocks and a foul count

The three disciplines are officiated in three ways.

## Artistic: a panel of judges

Judges mark the technical elements of a routine and its components. The
structure comes straight from figure skating. They count the turns in each
jump, judge how well it was done, and mark the routine as a whole. An
<Term id="under-rotation">under-rotated jump</Term> is short by a part of a
turn, and the judges must see that at full speed. Nothing but the judges' eyes
checks the count.

Artistic skating took on the problems of figure skating's judging along with
the method: judges voting in national blocs, a small pool of judges, and the
difficulty of separating two clean routines that are not equally hard. It has
followed the reforms of the ice sport.

## Speed: the clock and the referee

Speed races are timed. Photo finishes and transponders do the measuring. The
officials watch how the skaters race. They penalise leaving a lane, blocking
another skater and, in a <Term id="mass-start">mass start</Term>, cutting
across a rival on a bend.

## Rink hockey: counting fouls

Rink hockey referees have the most to do. They watch for sticks raised above
the shoulder, for charging, and for a ball played with the hands or too high.
They also keep the count of <Term id="team-fouls">team fouls</Term>. Each
foul by a player is added to the team's total. Once the total passes the
limit, every further foul gives the other team a
<Term id="direct-free-hit">direct free hit</Term>.
[Basketball](/sports/basketball/) counts team fouls in a similar spirit. In
rink hockey the count makes a team's discipline part of the score.
