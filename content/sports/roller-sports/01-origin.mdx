# Origin and invention

Roller sports exist because ice does not, most of the time, in most places.

The roller skate was developed through the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries
as a way of skating without ice, and the quad skate — four wheels in a square,
patented in the 1860s — was the design that made it practical. Rinks followed,
and with them the activities that filled them.

Each of the three main disciplines was built as a land equivalent of an ice
sport, and each imported the ice sport's structure wholesale. **Artistic roller
skating** took figure skating's disciplines, its jumps, its names and its
judging. **Roller speed skating** took speed skating's distances. **Rink
hockey** took a stick, a ball and a goal, and became a substantial sport in
southern Europe and Latin America in its own right.

The Fédération Internationale de Roller Sports was founded in 1924 to govern
them together, and its disciplines have been contested internationally ever
since.

Two things happened afterwards that this site records as the interesting part.
Around 1990, speed skating changed from quad skates to inline, while artistic
and rink hockey stayed on quads — so one federation ended up with disciplines
on incompatible equipment. And in 2017 the roller federation merged with the
international skateboarding body to form World Skate.

Four years after that merger, skateboarding was on the Olympic programme. The
disciplines the federation had governed since 1924 were not, and never have
been.
