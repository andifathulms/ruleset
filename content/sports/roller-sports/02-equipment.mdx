# Equipment evolution

One federation, two incompatible kinds of skate, and a good reason for each.

**Quad skates** carry four wheels in a square. That footprint gives lateral
stability — you can stand still, push sideways, and stop — and it is why
**artistic** and **rink hockey** are still contested on them. An artistic boot
also carries a **toe stop** at the front, which does the job a figure skate's
toe pick does: it is what a jump is launched from and, in the artistic
vocabulary, what several jumps are named after.

**Inline skates** put the wheels in a line. They roll faster, hold a line
better, and are closer in mechanics to an ice blade — and around 1990
competitive **speed skating** changed over to them almost completely within a
few years.

The other disciplines did not follow, and could not. A stick-and-ball game
needs a skater who can stop and change direction laterally; an artistic
programme needs the toe stop and the square platform. So the federation's
athletes are on equipment that is not interchangeable at all, which is unusual
even among the multi-discipline bodies on this site.

**Rink hockey** adds the rest: a curved stick, a hard ball, and goalkeeper
equipment that is closer to ice hockey's than to anything else — helmet, chest
protection, large leg guards and gloves — because a ball struck at close range
in a boarded rink has to be stopped by somebody.

Wheel compound and hardness are chosen for the surface, as they are in
skateboarding, which is one of the few things the merged federation's
disciplines genuinely share.
