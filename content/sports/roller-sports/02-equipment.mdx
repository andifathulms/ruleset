# Two kinds of skate

A speed skater and an artistic skater cannot swap skates. The disciplines use
two designs, and each has a reason.

## Four wheels in a square

A <Term id="quad-skate">quad skate</Term> is steady from side to side. A
skater on quads can stand still, push sideways and stop. Artistic skaters and
rink hockey players both need that. The artistic boot also has a
<Term id="toe-stop">toe stop</Term> at the front. It does the work of the toe
pick on a figure skate: the skater pushes off it to start a jump.

## Wheels in a line

An <Term id="inline-skate">inline skate</Term> rolls faster and holds a
straight course, and it moves more like an ice blade. Inline design improved
quickly in the late 1980s. Around 1990 [speed skating changed over to
inlines](#roller-inline-transition), almost completely, within a few years.

<Photos ids="roller-sports-inline-speed-skates roller-sports-berlin-2017" />

Times from before and after the change cannot be compared. Nobody had to rule
on the old marks, because nobody raced on quads any more.

<Photos ids="roller-sports-leveille-1910 roller-sports-track-finish-2017" />

The other two disciplines stayed on quads. A hockey player has to stop and
turn sideways in an instant. An artistic skater needs the toe stop and the
square base.

## Stick, ball and goalkeeper

Rink hockey adds a curved stick and a hard ball.

<Photos ids="roller-sports-goalkeeper-kneel roller-sports-hockey-stick" />

The ball is hit hard from close range in a small rink, and somebody has to
stand in front of it. Over time the goalkeeper's protection grew, as it has
in every sport of this kind. The goalkeeper now wears a helmet, a chest
protector, large leg guards and gloves, and carries more equipment than any
other roller athlete.

<Photos ids="roller-sports-france-portugal-2007 roller-sports-goalkeeper-crouch" />

Skaters in every discipline choose the hardness of their wheels to suit the
surface. [Skateboarders](/sports/skateboarding/) do the same.
