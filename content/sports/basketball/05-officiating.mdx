# Officiating and technology

Basketball's officiating burden is unlike anything else here: three officials
watching ten players in continuous contact on a small floor, adjudicating
several hundred discrete events per game, while four separate clocks run.

The technology has gone almost entirely to the clocks, and that is the pattern
worth noticing. **Instant replay** was written into the FIBA rules in 2014 for
a defined list, and the list is dominated by questions of time and arithmetic:
whether a shot was released before the buzzer, whether it was worth two points
or three, whether the shot clock was set correctly. Those are facts with
correct answers, and a camera settles them.

What the review system does not touch is the foul, which is the sport's
central judgement and by far its most argued. Whether contact was incidental,
whether a defender had established position, whether a shooter initiated the
contact — these are the basketball equivalent of football's handball law, and
for the same reason: the rule asks about a state of the world that no camera
can photograph.

The sport's one genuine attempt to make a foul judgement mechanical is the
**no-charge semi-circle**, added by FIBA in 2010. Inside the arc under the
basket, a defender cannot draw a charging foul. It replaces a judgement about
who was moving with a question about where somebody's feet were, which is
exactly the move badminton made with its service height in 2018 and athletics
makes wherever it can.

It is also worth recording that basketball trusts its officials with an unusual
amount of discretion by design. The technical foul and the unsportsmanlike foul
are deliberately broad, and the sport has not tried to narrow them.
