# Who makes the calls

Three officials watch ten players in constant contact on a small floor. A
crew at a table beside the court runs the clocks, and in basketball that is a
real part of the officiating, because the
<Term id="shot-clock">shot clock</Term> decides who has the ball.

Technology has gone mostly to the clocks. FIBA [wrote instant replay into its
rules in 2014](#fiba-2014-instant-replay) for a fixed list of questions. Was
the shot released before the buzzer? Was it worth two points or three? Was the
shot clock set correctly? Each has a correct answer, and a camera can find it.

Replay does not decide the ordinary <Term id="foul">foul</Term>, which is the
call players and crowds argue about most. Was the contact accidental? Had the
defender taken up a position first? Did the shooter cause the contact? A
camera can show what happened. It cannot say what the contact meant.

FIBA has tried once to turn a foul judgement into a measurement. In 2010 it
[added the no-charge semi-circle](#fiba-2010-arc-and-lane) under the basket. A
defender standing inside it cannot win a <Term id="charge">charge</Term>. The
referee no longer asks who was moving. The referee asks where the defender's
feet were. [Badminton](/sports/badminton/) made the same kind of change when
it fixed the height of its serve in 2018.

Basketball also leaves some calls wide open on purpose. The technical foul and
the unsportsmanlike foul are broad, and the sport has not tried to narrow
them.
