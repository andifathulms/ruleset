# Controversies that forced rule changes

**Fort Wayne 19, Minneapolis 18.** On 22 November 1950 the Fort Wayne Pistons
beat the Minneapolis Lakers by holding the ball. There was no rule requiring a
team to shoot, so a side in front could simply stop playing, and the only
counter available to the trailing team was to foul deliberately. The game is
the most cited trigger in the history of sports rulemaking, and it is on this
site because the PRD names it.

Four years later the NBA adopted a twenty-four second shot clock. The number
is attributed to Danny Biasone and to an arithmetic almost too neat to be true:
divide the seconds in a game by the number of shots a watchable game seemed to
contain. It worked immediately, scoring rose, and no serious basketball
competition has been played without a shot clock since.

**Wilt Chamberlain.** A run of rules is attributable to one player being too
good at exploiting geometry the rules had not anticipated — the widening of the
lane, offensive goaltending, restrictions on inbounding the ball over the
backboard, rules against leaving the floor from behind the free-throw line.
Basketball legislates against individuals more visibly than any other sport
here.

**Isolation basketball.** The NBA's illegal-defence rules had been written to
prevent zone defences and had grown into a body of regulation nobody could
officiate consistently. Their effect was one attacker working against one
defender while three team-mates stood clear, which the league judged
unwatchable. Zone defence was legalised in 2001 and a defensive three-second
rule added to stop the obvious abuse.

**The buzzer.** Almost everything basketball reviews on video is a question
about time — was the ball released before the horn, was the shot clock right,
was that two points or three. A sport governed by four clocks has an
officiating problem shaped like a stopwatch.
