# Three problems the rules had to solve

## Fort Wayne 19, Minneapolis 18

On 22 November 1950 the Fort Wayne Pistons played the Minneapolis Lakers, the
best team in the league. Fort Wayne got in front and then held the ball for
long stretches. No rule said a team had to shoot. A team that was ahead could
simply stop playing, and the only answer for the team behind was to
<Term id="foul">foul</Term> on purpose. Fort Wayne won 19–18.

Four years later the NBA brought in [a shot clock of twenty-four
seconds](#nba-1954-shot-clock). The number is credited to Danny Biasone. He is
said to have divided the seconds in a game by the number of shots a watchable
game seemed to contain. Scoring rose at once, and serious basketball has been
played with a <Term id="shot-clock">shot clock</Term> ever since.

## One player too good for the rules

Wilt Chamberlain was tall and athletic enough to use space around the basket
in ways the rules had not expected. A run of rules is linked to him. The lane
was widened. <Term id="goaltending">Goaltending</Term> by an attacker was
defined. Passing the ball in over the backboard was restricted. So was leaving
the floor from behind the free-throw line.

## One against one

For decades the NBA banned <Term id="zone-defence">zone defence</Term>. Every
defender had to guard one opponent. The rules that enforced this grew so
complicated that referees could not apply them the same way twice.

Teams learned to use them. One attacker took on one defender while the other
three attackers stood clear, so that their defenders had to stand clear too.
The league judged the result unwatchable. In 2001 it [made zone defence
legal](#nba-2001-zone-defence). It added a three-second limit for defenders at
the same time, so that a defender could not simply wait under the basket.
