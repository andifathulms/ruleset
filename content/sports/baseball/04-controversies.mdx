# Four times the game was rebalanced

Baseball's rules are made by a league. The World Baseball Softball
Confederation governs the international game, but each change below was a
decision of Major League Baseball or of one of its two leagues.

## 1968, the Year of the Pitcher

In 1968 almost nobody could hit. Bob Gibson of the St. Louis Cardinals
finished the season with an earned run average of 1.12. Carl Yastrzemski of
the Boston Red Sox won the American League batting title at .301, the lowest
average ever to win one. Scoring in both leagues fell to a level the sport
thought unwatchable.

<Photos ids="baseball-gibson baseball-yastrzemski" />

The answer came within months. For 1969 [the mound was lowered from fifteen
inches to ten, and the strike zone was made
smaller](#mlb-1969-mound-and-strike-zone). Both changes made pitching harder
and hitting easier, and that was their purpose. It also means that hitting
figures from before 1969 and after it were made under different conditions.

## Two leagues, two rules

In 1973 the American League let a
<Term id="designated-hitter">designated hitter</Term> bat in place of the
pitcher. Its attendance and scoring were behind the National League's, and
the pitcher was the weakest batter in every line-up.
[The National League refused](#mlb-1973-designated-hitter). Each league saw
the other's rule as a step away from what baseball was.

So one competition used two sets of batting rules for forty-nine years. In the
World Series the rule depended on which team's park the game was in. People
compared the pitchers of the two leagues anyway, although they faced different
line-ups.

Later the two leagues began to play each other during the season. Teams built
their squads for one rule and played many games under the other. The
pitcher's turn at bat had become close to an automatic
<Term id="out">out</Term>. In 2022
[the National League adopted the designated
hitter](#mlb-2022-universal-designated-hitter).

## The shift

Moving fielders to where a batter usually hits the ball is an old idea. A
newspaper described it being used against Cy Williams in May 1923. In the
2010s teams had data that showed where every batter hit the ball. They placed
their fielders to match, sometimes with three infielders on one side of second
base, and fewer ground balls became hits.

<Photos ids="baseball-shift-1923 baseball-shift-2015" />

The <Term id="shift">shift</Term> was legal and it worked. From 2023
[two infielders must stand on each side of second base, with both feet on the
infield dirt](#mlb-2023-pitch-clock-and-shift-ban). Some people say this
punishes teams for thinking correctly. The same objection was made when
[basketball](/sports/basketball/) made rules against certain defences and when
[football](/sports/football/) changed its offside law in 1925.

## The clock

For a century and a half baseball had no clock of any kind. Games had been
getting longer for decades, while the ball was in play for less of the time.
The sport's own analysis found the lost time in the gaps between pitches.

In 2023 a <Term id="pitch-clock">pitch clock</Term> arrived, in the same
package as the shift rule and larger bases. Rule changes in baseball go
through bargaining with the players, who have the most organised players'
body in American sport. The clock was negotiated with them, and that is why
it came as a package. Games became much shorter at once.
