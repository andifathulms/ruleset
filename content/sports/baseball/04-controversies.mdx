# Controversies that forced rule changes

**1968, the Year of the Pitcher.** Scoring collapsed. Bob Gibson recorded an
earned run average of 1.12; Carl Yastrzemski won a batting title at .301, the
lowest figure ever to do so. The sport concluded that pitching had won too
completely and, within months, lowered the mound from fifteen inches to ten and
shrank the strike zone.

That is a governing body reaching into the geometry of the field to change a
statistical outcome, and it worked immediately. It also severed every
offensive statistic in the sport.

**The designated hitter, 1973.** Adopted by one league, refused by the other,
and the split lasted until 2022. For half a century, comparing an American
League pitcher's earned run average with a National League pitcher's was
comparing performances under different rules, and everyone did it anyway.

**The shift.** Through the 2010s, teams used batted-ball data to position
fielders where a particular hitter actually hits the ball, and batting averages
on ground balls fell accordingly. It was legal, effective, and the product of
analysing the sport's own records. In 2023 it was restricted.

This is the same act as basketball banning zone defence and football's 1925
offside change: a rule written against a way of playing that was working too
well. The objection — that it penalises teams for thinking correctly — is the
same objection in all three cases.

**Time.** Game length had risen for decades while the ball was in play for less
of it. A sport that had defined itself by having no clock put one on the gap
between pitches in 2023, and the games got substantially shorter immediately.
