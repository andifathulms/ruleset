# Officiating and technology

Baseball is almost perfectly suited to review technology, because nearly every
call is a question about where an object or a body part was at a specific
instant. Was the foot on the bag before the ball arrived? Did the ball clear
the wall? Was it fair or foul?

**Replay** arrived in 2008 for boundary calls and expanded in 2014 into a
manager's challenge covering most of the sport. It is uncontroversial, and the
reason is that it answers factual questions with correct answers.

The exception is the one that matters most. **Balls and strikes** are still
called by a human, several hundred times a game, and the strike zone is
defined partly by the batter's stance — from the midpoint of the torso to below
the kneecap, as the batter is prepared to swing. That is a zone that changes
shape for every hitter and moves as they move.

Automated ball-strike systems exist, have been trialled extensively in the
minor leagues, and are technically capable. The sport has not adopted them at
the top level, and the argument against is not accuracy. It is that framing —
a catcher's skill in receiving a borderline pitch so that it looks like a
strike — is regarded by much of the sport as a real skill worth preserving,
and automation would delete it.

That is a genuinely unusual position on this site. Tennis removed its line
judges because the machine was better. Baseball has a machine that is better
and has declined so far, because it values something the machine would remove.
