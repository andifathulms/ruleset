# Who makes the calls

A major league game has four umpires, and six in the postseason. Nearly every
call they make is a question about where a ball or a body was at one instant.
Was the runner's foot on the base before the ball arrived? Did the ball clear
the wall? Was it fair or foul?

<Photo id="baseball-umpire-home" />

A camera answers that kind of question well.
[Video review began in 2008](#mlb-2008-instant-replay) for calls about home
runs only: fair or foul, over the fence or not, touched by a spectator or not.
In 2014 it grew into a manager's challenge that covers most calls. It has
caused little argument, because it gives correct answers to questions of fact.

<Photo id="baseball-under-review" />

The exception is the most common call in the game. An umpire calls
<Term id="ball">balls</Term> and <Term id="strike">strikes</Term> several
hundred times a game. The <Term id="strike-zone">strike zone</Term> is defined
partly by the batter's stance. It runs from the midpoint of the torso to just
below the kneecap, as the batter stands ready to swing. So the zone has a
different shape for every batter, and it moves when the batter moves.

Machines that can call balls and strikes exist. They have been tried for years
in the minor leagues, where a team can challenge the umpire's call and the
scoreboard shows the pitch against the zone.

<Photo id="baseball-abs-2025" />

The argument against the machine is about a skill, and has little to do with
accuracy. A good catcher can receive a pitch on the edge of the zone so that
it looks like a strike. This is called framing. Much of the sport sees it as a
real skill, and a machine would remove it. [Tennis](/sports/tennis/) replaced
its line judges because the machine was better. Baseball has been slower,
because it values something the machine would take away.
