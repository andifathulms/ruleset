# Origin and invention

Racquetball is the most precisely dated sport on this site, and one of the very
few with a single named inventor.

In 1950, Joe Sobek, a professional tennis and handball player, was working at
the Greenwich YMCA in Connecticut and wanted a game that was easier on the
hands than handball and quicker to pick up than squash. He designed a short
strung racquet, chose a lively rubber ball, wrote a set of rules, and called
the result paddle rackets.

The decisive choice was what he did *not* change. He kept handball's court —
the sealed four-wall box that already existed in YMCAs across the United
States. A sport that needs no new facilities can spread as fast as people want
to play it, and through the 1960s and 1970s racquetball did exactly that,
becoming one of the fastest-growing participation sports in American history.

The rules he wrote removed nearly every boundary. No tin at the base of the
front wall, no out line above it, no side lines, and the ceiling in play. A
rally ends when the ball bounces twice or fails to reach the front wall, and
almost nothing else ends it. That is what made the game immediately playable,
and it is still the sport's defining structural fact.

The International Racquetball Federation followed in 1979 — arriving, as it
turned out, at the top of the curve. American participation peaked around then
and fell sharply as commercial clubs converted courts into gym floor. The
federation has spent its life governing a sport that grew abroad while
contracting at home.
