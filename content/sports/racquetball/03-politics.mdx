# Governing body politics

The IRF governs a sport with a strong international competitive structure and a
collapsing facility base in its home country, and those two facts drive
everything.

Racquetball boomed in the United States through the 1970s, and the boom was
built on commercial fitness clubs. When the fitness market shifted in the 1980s
and 1990s towards cardio and weights, those clubs converted courts into floor
space, and the sport lost its infrastructure faster than it lost its players.
This is the clearest case on this site of a sport declining for reasons that
had nothing to do with its rules, its governance or its athletes.

Internationally the picture is better. The federation, formed in 1979, holds
world championships, and racquetball has a Pan American Games place — a real
multi-sport platform, and the sport's most secure institutional asset. Mexico
and Bolivia in particular have become powers, and Bolivia's success is
disproportionate to anything about its size or wealth.

The comparison the sport cannot avoid is squash. Two enclosed-court racket
sports of similar size and similar international spread, one of which spent
decades campaigning for Olympic inclusion, rewrote its scoring, invested in
glass-court broadcast presentation, and was added to the 2028 programme.
Racquetball did not make those moves and is at the World Games.

Whether racquetball could have made them is genuinely arguable — its court is
harder to present, because a ceiling in play makes overhead camera work
difficult and a sealed box is harder to open up for spectators than a squash
court is. But the divergence is the sport's central political fact.
