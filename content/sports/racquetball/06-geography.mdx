# Where it is played

An American invention whose competitive centre of gravity has moved south.

**The United States** invented racquetball, drove its boom, and remains a major
competitive power — but it is also where the sport's decline is sharpest. The
commercial clubs that built it converted their courts to gym floor from the
1980s onwards, and a generation of players lost their facilities rather than
their interest.

**Mexico** is now arguably the strongest nation in the sport, with a deep
competitive structure and sustained international success. **Bolivia** is the
outlier worth pausing on: a country of modest size and resources that has
produced world champions and beaten far larger nations consistently, on the
strength of a concentrated national programme. It is the same shape of story as
Colombia in ultimate and in finswimming — a small investment in a small sport
producing results out of all proportion.

**Canada**, **Argentina**, **Costa Rica**, **Chile**, **Guatemala** and
**Ecuador** all compete seriously, which makes racquetball unusually American
in the hemispheric sense. Its Pan American Games place both reflects and
reinforces that.

Beyond the Americas, **Japan**, **South Korea**, **India** and a scattering of
European countries play it, but the sport has never taken root in Europe the
way squash did.

The facility question governs everything. Racquetball needs a specific enclosed
court, those courts were built for a boom that ended, and very few have been
built since. A sport that spread because the courts already existed is now
constrained for exactly the same reason.
