# The bar and the kit

A hundred kilograms is a hundred kilograms, whether it was lifted in 1960 or
last week. The thing being measured is the object itself, so better equipment
cannot quietly add to the result. Weightlifting has never had the kind of
equipment race that the javelin gave [athletics](/sports/athletics/) or the
swimsuit gave [swimming](/sports/swimming/).

<Photos ids="weightlifting-loading-plates weightlifting-leipzig-1955" />

The men's bar weighs 20 kilograms and the women's 15. The
<Term id="bumper-plates">plates</Term> are calibrated and coloured by weight,
so a spectator and a referee can read the load from a distance.

What has improved is everything around the bar. Bearings and bushings let the
ends of the bar turn freely, which makes it easier to turn the bar over in a
<Term id="snatch">snatch</Term>. Rubber-coated plates let a lifter drop the bar
from overhead without wrecking the platform, and that changed training more
than competition. Shoes with a raised, rigid heel let the lifter sit deeper
under the bar.

None of these brought a new rule. The specification for the bar and plates has
held for decades.

<Photos ids="weightlifting-snatch weightlifting-clean-and-jerk" />

The rules about what a lifter wears exist so the referees can see. The
<Term id="singlet">singlet</Term> must leave the elbows and knees uncovered.
The referees need to see whether the arms straightened cleanly and whether the
knees locked.

The belt and the wraps are limited by size only. A belt may be up to 12
centimetres wide, wraps have a maximum length, and the rules say nothing about
how either is made.
