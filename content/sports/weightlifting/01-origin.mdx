# Origin and invention

Weightlifting is one of the few sports on this site whose activity needed no
inventing at all — lifting a heavy object is not a game — and whose competition
had to be invented almost entirely.

Nineteenth-century strongman exhibitions were the immediate ancestor: music
hall and circus performers lifting objects of contested weight, in movements
nobody had defined, judged by acclamation. Turning that into a sport meant
answering three questions that had no obvious answers. What counts as a lift?
What counts as the same weight? And who is a competitor allowed to be compared
with?

The sport's entire rulebook is still those three questions. The first produced
prescribed movements and the referees who judge them. The second produced
calibrated plates and the one-kilogram increment. The third produced bodyweight
categories, which is where all of this sport's trouble lives.

Weightlifting was on the programme of the first modern Games in 1896, in a form
that would be unrecognisable — one-handed and two-handed lifts, no bodyweight
categories at all. The International Weightlifting Federation was founded in
1905. Categories arrived in the 1920s, three prescribed lifts were settled
around the same time, and the sport looked broadly stable until 1972.

Women's weightlifting was contested at world level from 1987 and entered the
Olympic programme in 2000, which is late even by the standards of the sports
here.
