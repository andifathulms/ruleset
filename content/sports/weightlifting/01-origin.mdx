# From the music hall to the platform

Lifting a heavy object needed no inventing. The competition around it had to
be built almost from nothing.

In the nineteenth century lifting was a stage act. Strongmen in music halls
and circuses lifted objects whose weight nobody had checked, in movements
nobody had defined, and the crowd judged them by its applause. A poster for
Eugen Sandow at the Trocadero in 1894 shows him holding a bar overhead with a
man sitting in a basket at each end.

<Photos ids="weightlifting-sandow-1894 weightlifting-sandow-1902" />

Turning the act into a sport meant answering three questions. What counts as a
lift? What counts as the same weight? Who may a lifter be compared with?

The rulebook is still built on those three answers. The first gave the sport
its prescribed movements and the <Term id="referees">referees</Term> who judge
them. The second gave it calibrated <Term id="bumper-plates">plates</Term> and
the one-kilogram step between weights. The third gave it the
<Term id="category">bodyweight category</Term>, and most of the sport's later
trouble comes from that one.

Weightlifting was part of the first modern Games in 1896. A lifter of today
would hardly know it: there were one-handed and two-handed lifts and no
bodyweight categories at all. The International Weightlifting Federation was
founded in 1905. Categories arrived in the 1920s, and three lifts were settled
at about the same time: the <Term id="snatch">snatch</Term>, the
<Term id="clean-and-jerk">clean and jerk</Term> and the clean and press.

<Photos ids="weightlifting-charite-1952 weightlifting-kono-1952" />

By 1952 the sport had its shape. Tommy Kono of the United States won the
Olympic title at Helsinki that year, and lifters such as Bram Charité of the
Netherlands still stepped onto the platform in trousers.

The three-lift sport lasted until 1972, when [the press was
removed](#iwf-1973-press-abolished). Women lifted at world level from 1987 and
at the Olympic Games from 2000.
