# Three referees and a jury

Three <Term id="referees">referees</Term> watch every
<Term id="attempt">attempt</Term>, and the majority decides. They have few
questions to answer. Did the bar go overhead in the prescribed way? Was it
held still, with arms and legs locked? Did anything but the feet touch the
<Term id="platform">platform</Term>?

<Photo id="weightlifting-rio-snatch" />

One call causes most of the arguments: the
<Term id="press-out">press-out</Term>. The lifter's arms begin to straighten
and then keep pushing, where they should lock in one motion. It happens fast
and the bar partly hides it. It is the same kind of judgement that ended the
press in 1973, and the sport has never fully solved it.

## The jury can overrule

At major competitions a <Term id="jury">jury</Term> sits above the referees
and can reverse them, with video to help. At the Rio Games in 2016 the
referees passed a lift by Behdad Salimi of Iran in the heaviest class. The
jury ruled it out, and Salimi spread his arms in disbelief. The same thing can
happen in [judo](/sports/judo/), where the officials on the mat do not have
the last word either.

## What the machines do

Technology handles the questions that have a correct answer. Calibrated plates
and a published order of attempts confirm what is on the bar. The clock is
automatic.

Nothing measures the lift itself. There is no sensor on the bar and no
automatic check of the arms, nothing like the electronic scoring of
[taekwondo](/sports/taekwondo/). A press-out is a judgement about how a
movement was made, and that is harder to hand to a machine than a position at
one moment.

Two other checks happen away from the platform: the
<Term id="weigh-in">weigh-in</Term> and the drug test. In weightlifting they
have changed the record book more than any referee has.
